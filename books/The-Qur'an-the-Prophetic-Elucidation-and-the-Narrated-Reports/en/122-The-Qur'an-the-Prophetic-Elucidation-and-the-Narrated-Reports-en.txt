The Qur'an, the Prophetic Elucidation, and the Narrated Reports
• A Condensed Conceptual Translation

Introduction: The Sufficiency of the Qur'an and the Perfection of Legislation
This book addresses a fundamental question that confronts us in every gathering and commentary, most commonly posed as: "How will you pray, fast, and perform pilgrimage if you abandon the heritage?" This question is asked with remarkable confidence, yet the one asking it fails to notice that it rests on a dangerous, unstated premise: that God's Book is incomplete and requires another source to complete it.
We return to the decisive point: God has categorically denied this deficiency, saying: "We have not neglected anything in the Book" (6:38). He did not say "most things" or "its fundamentals," but used the expression "anything"—which encompasses everything and closes every gap.
This is further clarified and confirmed by His saying: "And We have sent down to you the Book as clarification for all things, and as guidance and mercy and good tidings for the Muslims" (16:89). The Qur'an is not a riddle awaiting decipherment, nor is it conceivable in God's justice that He would suspend His servants' eternal fate on the memory of a narrator or the preference of one scholar over another. The proof on Judgment Day must be clear and reach every person without intermediary.
Thus God settled the matter Himself: "And We have certainly made the Qur'an easy for remembrance, so is there any who will remember?" (54:17), repeated four times in one chapter to block every claim that the Book is difficult and requires intermediaries. The Qur'an is the clear, detailed Book whose understanding God has made easy for remembrance.
Abandoning the heritage is not disparagement of those who wrote it, but rejection of making it a parallel source of legislation for the lawful and unlawful alongside God's preserved Book. There is a vast difference between reading something for benefit and legislating by it and building religious rulings upon it.
We are sometimes told the Qur'an is concise and needs someone to detail it, but God attributed detail to Himself: "Is it other than God I should seek as judge while it is He who has sent down to you the Book explained in detail?" (6:114). Who would dare say God's detail is incomplete?
The call here is not to imitate anyone, but to open God's Book and present everything that reaches us before it: what agrees with it is truth, and what contradicts it is rejected. The most dangerous testimony that could be held against us is abandoning this Book—and abandonment is not merely ceasing to recite it, but making it only for blessing while relying on other sources for legislation.

Chapter One: The Temporal Gap and the Absence of Recording
The contemplative mind confronts a historical fact no one denies: the Prophet died in the eleventh year of Hijra, while the recording of what is called "the Sunnah" did not begin until a vast period later. If we trace the birth dates of those who collected these narrations, we find the first was not born until 183 years after the Prophet's death, another after 195 years, another after 198 years. We are not facing a gap of ten or twenty years, but two full centuries—equivalent to six consecutive human generations transmitting this heritage by memory alone before it was written.
The Problem of Oral Transmission and the Fragility of Memory
The absence of recording throughout these centuries raises a fundamental question: Where are the original documents and manuscripts that should correspond to the thousands of narrated hadiths with their witnesses and dates? Total reliance on oral transmission makes it vulnerable to distortion. If you told a clever man a two-line story and he transmitted it to another after only five minutes, something minor would change unintentionally. What then of transmission spanning two hundred years through dozens of people in each chain?
Moreover, humans by nature change. A narrator may be truthful in youth and lie in old age, or be trustworthy then weaken under pressure of authority or temptation of money. Any system building its confidence on the truthfulness of non-infallible persons across centuries is fragile and cannot be the basis of a religion for which we will be held accountable on Judgment Day.
The Mystery of the Lost Friday Sermons and Selective Transmission
Among the strongest evidences that this transmission was not faithful and complete preservation is the case of the Friday sermons. Friday prayer was prescribed for nearly ten years, meaning the Prophet delivered approximately 480 Friday sermons before hundreds of Muslims in the most important weekly guidance occasion. Yet if you open all heritage references, you will not find a single complete Friday sermon written for the Prophet. In a strange paradox, public sermons delivered before crowds are absent, while references overflow with private details of what happened behind the doors of the Prophet's house. This clearly proves the process was subject to deliberate selection—the narrator transmits what serves a specific purpose and omits what does not.
Comparison Between What God Preserved and What Was Left to Humans
The truth becomes radiant when we compare the path of the Book God guaranteed to preserve with the path of narrations people left to themselves:
• The Book's path: God guaranteed its collection, recitation, and clarification: "Indeed, upon Us is its collection and its recitation" (75:17). God did not leave His Book to memory alone, but sent it down as written text recited from "purified pages," a "book inscribed in unrolled parchment." Thus Muslims across the earth read one identical copy without difference in a single letter.
• The narrations' path: Left to people and oral transmission, reaching us as a mass of contradictory texts—one scholar authenticates, another weakens; they agree neither on their number nor their content. If these narrations were a second revelation and completion of religion as claimed, God would have preserved them as He preserved the other half of His religion. God does not guarantee preservation of half the religion and leave the other half to loss and disagreement.
If these hadiths were an integral part of religion's completeness, the Prophet—most eager of people for the trust of conveyance—would have ordered their collection and writing himself, not left them scattered in chests. This historical reality documented in their own books obligates us to adhere to the Qur'anic principle: "And do not pursue that of which you have no knowledge" (17:36)—for a narration passing through six generations without recording never reaches certainty, and God's religion is built only on absolute certainty.

Chapter Two: The Pioneers of Recording and Those Coming from Persia
When we reflect on the foundations of the heritage presented today as a second half of religion, we confront a historical fact no one denies but few pause at its deep implications: the six primary books upon which the heritage was built—considered by many the most authentic after the Qur'an—their authors are all from outside the Arabian Peninsula, without exception. The two sources said to be "the most authentic books after God's Book" were born, raised, and educated in Persia; they were not Arab, did not live in the environment of the mission or its surroundings.
Why Did the People of Mecca and Medina Absent Themselves from Recording Their Religion?
Here the logical mind asks and pauses long: Is not one who witnessed the events, lived in their environment, and spoke their language more worthy of transmitting them than one who came from outside after generations and centuries? Why did the Arabs—the people of Mecca and Medina who witnessed the revelation and spoke the Qur'an's language as their mother tongue—not record their religion themselves? Why wait two centuries for people from distant environments to collect for them what is said to be "half their religion"?
The answer places us before two logical possibilities:
1. Either the Companions and first generation saw no need for this recording, contenting themselves with God's Book as revealed, considering it sufficient and complete.
2. Or the Prophet truly prohibited them from writing anything other than the Qur'an (as some heritage narrations themselves mention). If so, who permitted what the Prophet prohibited later? In either case, it becomes clear that recording was not an original religious necessity but occurred later for other reasons.
The Historical and Political Context of the Abbasid Era
We cannot separate the recording process from the historical and political context in which it appeared. This recording movement occurred in the Abbasid era—an era historically distinguished by strong Persian cultural and political influence in the Abbasid state, where ministers, scribes, and advisors of Persian origin emerged.
The fundamental question here is not about the existence of this Persian cultural influence, but: Did this influence extend to religion itself? Did the recordings of that era affect by the cultural and political context in which they arose? The reflective researcher notes that many concepts entering religion through this heritage (such as anthropomorphism) have parallels in ancient cultures preceding Islam, specifically in those regions. This similarity cannot be ignored and raises an urgent question about the extent of "influence and being influenced" to which those narrations and concepts were subject.
Manufacturing an Integrated System, Not Merely Texts
The matter did not stop at collecting scattered texts, but extended to who formulated concepts and established the sciences surrounding these hadiths. In that same era, under that cultural and political influence, "hadith sciences," "jurisprudence principles," and "criticism and authentication" emerged and crystallized. They established a complete system with its rules and terminology, then positioned this system as a guard preventing any criticism. Whoever discusses a narration is told: "You don't understand hadith science." Whoever asks is told: "Refer to the conditions of narration." The system became self-protecting, exactly as religious men in previous nations did when they erected a barrier between people and their Lord's Book under the pretext that people "don't understand."
We are not attacking the persons of these recorders, their origins, or intentions—there is no superiority of Arab over non-Arab except in piety, and a man among them may be truthful and sincere. But sincerity of intention does not correct methodology, nor does it transform what humans wrote into infallible revelation from God. Religion is God's preserved Book which falsehood cannot approach, and the loss of this later recording heritage is only the loss of human effort, diminishing not a single letter of God's religion preserved in the Qur'an.

Chapter Three: The Problem of Authentication and Weakness, and the Myth of Consensus
When you discuss a hadith from heritage references and show its problems, a familiar scene repeats: you bring a hadith authentic according to one scholar, and he says it is weak according to another. You bring a second hadith, and he says there is disagreement. You ask: Where is certainty then? He resorts to the ready phrase: "The scholars have consensus." This is the vortex in which the mind has circled for centuries—a closed loop; whenever you grasp one end, the other slips away.
The cause of this confusion is that we deal with "elastic" material with no controlling standard or stability.
The Fragility of the Authentication and Weakening Process
If you asked the most learned of their scholars: How many authentic hadiths are there precisely? He could not give you a single agreed-upon number. The reason is simple: what is authentic according to one scholar may be weak according to another; what one accepts another rejects. Imagine a source said to be "the second legislation" after God's Book, yet no one can determine its content with certainty! What building is this whose foundation changes with the engineer?
In contrast, if you asked a Muslim at the far end of the earth and another at its nearest about a Qur'anic verse, they would give you the same text letter by letter. The Qur'an needs no one to authenticate or weaken it, for it is preserved by God's preservation. The Qur'anic criterion is decisive: "And if it were from other than God, they would have found within it much contradiction" (4:82). Since disagreement in authentication and weakening is much and visible, it is definitively not from God.
Perhaps the clearest example of this fragility is what happened in the modern era. After 14 centuries of narrating and recording these hadiths, a very late figure (Nasir al-Din al-Albani) came to authenticate hadiths considered weak and weaken hadiths considered authentic. It became said: "Albani authenticated it" and "Albani weakened it"! Is it conceivable that God's religion for which we will be held accountable is left open for anyone to rearrange, authenticate, and weaken across centuries?
Disagreement in Meaning and Application
The problem does not stop at narration authenticity but extends to its understanding and application. A single hadith may be interpreted one way by a jurist and completely oppositely by another. Consequently, contradictory rulings are built: what is lawful in one school may be unlawful in another; what is obligatory according to one is disliked according to another. The text is one, but understandings differ and schools diverge. God warned us of this division and made it a characteristic of polytheists: "Of those who divided their religion and became sects, each faction rejoicing in what it has" (30:32).
"Consensus": The Greatest Lie
When disagreements surface and arguments collapse, the weapon of "consensus" is brandished. When "the community has consensus" or "the scholars have consensus" is said, the questioner falls silent and discussion stops. But the shocking truth is that this alleged consensus does not exist in jurisprudential and practical reality!
Search any detail of worship (prayer, zakat, fasting) and you will find the extent of disagreement revealing they agreed on no single jurisprudential ruling, nor on interpreting a single verse, but differed even on conditions for accepting narration itself. The only thing they truly agreed upon is "the existence of a second legislative source other than the Qur'an," then differed on every detail of this second source. Consensus is thus merely a tool to silence questioners and an attempt to make this "alleged human consensus" rule over Qur'anic texts.
Add to this that arguing by majority of scholars or numerical majority is a fallen argument by the Qur'an's own text. God criticized the majority and never made it evidence of truth, but repeatedly described it as misguidance and conjecture: "And if you obey most of those upon the earth, they will mislead you from the way of God" (6:116), and: "And most of them believe not in God except while they are associating others with Him" (12:106).
The Result: Permanent Anxiety vs. the Tranquility of Certainty
This confused methodology left the ordinary Muslim in permanent anxiety and confusion, asking and finding contradictory answers, not knowing whether what he does is lawful or unlawful. While if he held fast to the Book alone (God's firm rope), he would find God's religion clear, evident, and easy.
Those who justify this contradiction with the narration "difference in my community is a mercy" invert concepts. The Qur'an says division is blameworthy and destructive, while they say it is richness and mercy! The only exit from this vortex is returning to what has no disagreement: God's preserved Book, which Muslims at the earth's farthest and nearest read without differing on a single letter.

Chapter Four: Offense Against the Prophet in Heritage Books
We enter in this chapter a truly painful door: what was attributed to our Prophet in heritage books that offends him while he is innocent of it. Before beginning, we must affirm something important: in this chapter we do not attack the Prophet, but defend him and absolve him of what was attached to him. The one who truly offended him is who narrated, recorded, and authenticated these narrations, not who presents them today before God's Book and rejects them.
We will not bring narrations from weak books so it is not said we cherry-pick, but rely on what they consider "their most authentic sources" to see if these narrations withstand the Qur'anic criterion.
The Urgent Question: Why Were These Narrations Hidden from Us?
The first thing we must ask: Why did we grow up hearing what is beautiful and acceptable, never hearing these narrations in our schools or gatherings? The answer is simply that these narrations—which shock the mind and wound the heart—remained imprisoned in volumes and did not appear to people until books became open to everyone in the information age. When people read for themselves what was not read to them, the shock occurred and questioning began: If this exists in their most authentic sources, why was it hidden from us all these years?
First Door: Portraying the Prophet as Destitute and Poor
Among the most prominent doors of offense is portraying the Prophet as poor and destitute, spending the night hungry, forced to pawn his armor to a non-believer to eat. We thought this was asceticism, but when we presented it to God's Book, the opposite became clear.
• The Qur'anic criterion: God says to His Prophet: "And your Lord is going to give you, and you will be satisfied" (93:5), and clearly says: "And He found you poor and made you rich" (93:8). God informs us He enriched His Prophet until he was satisfied, and enrichment is the opposite of poverty. How could he spend the night hungry whom God enriched?
• The logical contradiction: The same narrations mention that around the Prophet were those who equipped armies. Why did he not borrow from them and instead pawn to a non-believer? God denied that anyone has a favor upon His Prophet to be recompensed. True asceticism is possessing without being enslaved by possessions, not being destitute without choice. Asceticism is not built on contradicting a clear Qur'anic text confirming God's enrichment of His Prophet.
Second Door: Portraying Him as Disturbed, Contemplating Suicide
This door is more painful: in their most authentic sources they narrate that the Prophet at the beginning of revelation thought of ending his life by throwing himself from mountain peaks, and were it not for Gabriel appearing, he would have done so.
• The Qur'anic criterion: Is it conceivable that one sent by God is portrayed as a disturbed man doubting what he sees? God affirms His Prophet's sound mind and denies his opponents' claim: "You are not, by the favor of your Lord, a madman" (68:2), and describes him: "And indeed, you are of a great moral character" (68:4). Great moral character never coexists with such disturbance. In the most intense moments, the divine address was reassurance and affirmation: "Your Lord has not taken leave of you, nor has He detested you" (93:3). The Prophet was protected and supported, not a man left to his confusion and despair as they claimed.
Third Door: Portraying Him as Bewitched (Agreeing with the Oppressors' Claim)
They claimed the Prophet was bewitched until he imagined he did something he did not do.
• The Qur'anic criterion: It suffices to know that God quoted the oppressors' saying about the Prophet and condemned them for it—the very saying they authenticate today: "When the wrongdoers say, 'You follow not but a man affected by magic'" (25:8). Their saying matches the oppressors' saying letter by letter! Moreover, God guaranteed His protection from people: "And God will protect you from the people" (5:67). How could magic penetrate to disrupt the message's conveyance if he was protected? This narration not only offends the Prophet but touches God's power itself, as if saying God did not protect His Messenger.
Fourth Door: Violating Privacies
These volumes entered the Prophet's chambers and privacies and his family's privacy in ways one is ashamed to mention. The question every Muslim must ask: What religious benefit returns to the community from knowing what happened behind his closed doors? Why were these precise details preserved while public Friday sermons vanished?
The Real Purpose: Undermining Confidence
What is the purpose of collecting these narrations in the image of a disturbed, bewitched, poor man? The answer is clear: undermining confidence in the bearer of the message, because it inevitably leads to undermining confidence in what is borne—the Book. When a youth reads this contradiction, he is disturbed, and is told: "Submit and don't discuss." Either he cancels his mind, or abandons religion entirely.
But there is a third path—the path of salvation: to keep our minds and our religion by rejecting the narration that contradicts God's Book. The greatest honor to our Prophet is not in appearances, but in absolving him of what was attached to him contradicting the Qur'an, for God chose him and bore the message, and He knows best where to place His message.

Chapter Five: Abu Hurayra and Confessions of Concealment and Selective Narration
When we speak of the parallel religious heritage, we cannot bypass the person upon whom the greatest part of this entire structure rests. We speak of the greatest transmitter to whom the largest number of hadiths was attributed (over 5,000 according to their sources), known as Abu Hurayra.
We do not enter into details of his personal biography or historical disputes about him—that door is long—but stop at a dangerous confession he narrated about himself, recorded and authenticated in their most reliable sources.
The Confession of Concealment and Selective Transmission
It was narrated from him with a chain they authenticate that he was asked about the abundance of what he narrated, and he answered confessing: "I memorized from the Messenger of God two vessels: as for one, I spread it; as for the other, if I spread it, this throat would be cut."
Pause long at this confession! The man upon whom the greatest transmission rests says plainly he revealed half and hid half, deciding for himself what reaches people and what does not. This confession, existing in their most authentic sources, destroys the idea of absolute trustworthiness in transmission. How is it conceivable that a religion, for which we will be held accountable on Judgment Day, is built on a basis subject to a narrator's selection—concealing what he wishes and spreading what he wishes?
Let us analyze this "concealed vessel" logically:
• If it was beneficial knowledge connected to religion, who gave him the right to conceal it from the community and people more in need of it?
• If it was harmful knowledge, why did he memorize it from the Prophet at all?
• If it was political secrets unrelated to religion, why connect it to what he memorized from the Prophet and place it in religion's category? The most likely answer is that the "second vessel" matter is a psychological trick intended to give an aura of mystery and greatness to what he narrates, as if saying: "I gave you only the little; I have what is greater," to increase the awe of what he narrates and elevate his status among people. This is a methodology of authority, not of trustworthiness.
Concealment in the Qur'anic Balance
When we present this methodology to God's Book, we find a decisive ruling. God commanded His Prophet to convey clearly and completely without exception: "O Messenger, announce that which has been revealed to you from your Lord, and if you do not, then you have not conveyed His message" (5:67). If the Messenger himself does not have the right to conceal a single letter, how is this right given to a narrator concealing an entire vessel? God made concealing clear signs and guidance among the greatest sins, cursing its doer: "Indeed, those who conceal what We sent down of clear proofs and guidance after We made it clear for the people in the Scripture—those are cursed by God and cursed by those who curse" (2:159). How do we build religion on the words of one who confesses with his tongue that he concealed?
The Paradox of the Huge Number and Short Companionship
What increases the problem is that this huge number attributed to him (over 5,000 hadiths) corresponds, according to their own narration, to a relatively short companionship period (a few years at the end of the Prophet's life). Logic asks: How did one who accompanied the Prophet a few years carry more than those who accompanied him for decades? How is attributed to him alone what exceeds the total attributed to dozens of major Companions who were closer and longer in companionship? This strange disparity raises a question that cannot be ignored about the source of this huge quantity.
Contradiction and Collapse of Reliability
The matter does not stop at abundance or concealment; the narrations attributed to him are full of clear contradictions. You find a hadith affirming something, and another narrated from him himself denying it! As if the Prophet (far above that) said the statement and its opposite. No mind can combine accepting his confession of "self-censorship" (spreading what suits and concealing what disturbs) with accepting everything he narrates; one who confesses selection is not known where he selected and where he transmitted faithfully, and the judicial rule says doubt in part extends to the whole.
Summary of This Door
This heritage structure not only transmitted with concealment, but linked religion to one person in a dangerous connection, so criticizing the narration became considered criticizing religion. God's Book is free of this linkage; God did not suspend His religion on an individual's trustworthiness, but sent down His Book and guaranteed its preservation, so religion is suspended on God alone.

Chapter Six: Critique of Introduced Concepts (Occasions of Revelation, Sacred Hadiths, and Grave Punishment)
We enter in this chapter three major issues entrenched in Muslims' minds until many thought they were religious constants, though when presented to God's Book they collapse one after another. We will address them with reflection and analysis.
First: Occasions of Revelation (Transforming Eternal Legislation into Reaction)
The concept of "occasions of revelation" falls under what God condemned: "And they did not appraise God with true appraisal" (6:91). They claimed the revelation of many verses was tied to events and persons: so-and-so asked and a verse answering was revealed; so-and-so did something and a verse commenting was revealed. As if God—exalted above that—was waiting for something to occur on earth to respond with legislation!
• Reaction or eternal knowledge? If we assume so-and-so had not asked, would we remain without legislation on that matter until Judgment Day? This conception makes the sent-down Book merely a "reaction" to events rather than a ruling preceding them, and makes divine legislation dependent, not independent. God encompasses all things in knowledge and decided: "We have not neglected in the Book anything" (6:38). How does He wait for events to complete His Book? The complete, comprehensive does not wait for an event to complete, for if it waited, it would have deficiency before its occurrence.
• Difference between stories and rulings: We must differentiate between stories and rulings. Stories are by nature narration of events that occurred; rulings (lawful and unlawful) are eternal legislations emanating from God's encompassing knowledge. The lawful is lawful and the unlawful is unlawful whether anyone asked or not.
• Restricting Qur'anic meaning: Linking a verse to a specific occasion of revelation restricts its comprehensive meaning and confines it to that event. Understanding the Qur'an transforms from direct reflection to searching narrations for what happened in the past, stripping the Book of its "clear" quality and making it veiled by external understanding conditions. The Qur'an addresses the Muslim today as it addressed the first ones, and its understanding needs no knowledge of what happened in Medina's market 1,400 years ago.
Second: Sacred Hadiths (Unpreserved Speech and Anthropomorphism)
Sacred hadiths are defined as direct speech attributed to God ("God said") but not in the Qur'an.
• The logical dilemma (why was it not preserved?): We are before speech said to be from God, yet God did not preserve it as He preserved His Book, but left it to narrators' trustworthiness and memory across centuries! The equation is clear: If it is God's speech, why did He not preserve it? If it is not His speech, why attribute it to Him? God who guaranteed preserving the Reminder does not leave speech from Him to loss and forgetting. Did God forget something and compensate outside the Book? Far above that, for the Book is "clarification for all things."
• Anthropomorphism of the divine essence: The greatest danger in these hadiths is their excess in describing God with explicit material human attributes (affirming hand as organ, leg, actual sitting, descending and coming). These concepts leaked from ancient cultures preceding Islam. This anthropomorphism directly clashes with two firm Qur'anic principles: "There is nothing like Him" (42:11) and "Vision does not perceive Him" (6:103). The one vision does not perceive is not a body falling under senses. When the Qur'an mentions "hand" it means power and spending ("Rather, both His hands are extended; He spends however He wills" 5:64), and when it mentions "face" it means essence and presence ("Wherever you turn, there is the face of God" 2:115). Sacred hadiths return us to the pagan conception of a deity as a material being occupying space. Attributing speech God did not say is among the greatest injustice: "And who is more unjust than one who invents about God a lie" (6:21).
Third: Grave Punishment and the Barzakh Concept (The Impossible Return)
It became entrenched in many minds that there is punishment in the grave immediately after death for criminals, and that the barzakh is a place where people sleep or are punished.
• Barzakh is a barrier, not a place: The word "barzakh" appears in the Qur'an three times: twice meaning the barrier between the two seas ("Between them is a barrier which they do not transgress" 55:20), and once in death ("And behind them is a barrier until the Day they are resurrected" 23:100). Barzakh in the Qur'an is not a third place, but a "barrier" and preventer separating the world of the living from the world of the Hereafter, preventing the dead from returning to the world.
• When does the dead ask to return? In Surah Al-Mu'minun: "Until, when death comes to one of them, he says, 'My Lord, send me back'" (23:99). The speaker here is a criminal asking to return immediately upon his death. This indicates he was resurrected alive immediately, became conscious, sees Hell before him, speaks and addresses his Lord begging to return to fix what he corrupted. The dead does not sleep in the grave but is recompensed immediately, either with the Garden of Refuge (like the believer) or with miserable life and Hell (like the criminal).
• The resting place and heedlessness: As for sleep ("Who has sent us from our resting place" 36:52), it does not mean sleeping in the grave, but a state affecting people after the first blast and before the second blast on Judgment Day. Criminals are punished immediately after death (Hell punishment, not grave), then are struck by the first blast and rest, then resurrected by the second blast for final Judgment Day reckoning.
In conclusion, presenting all heritage to the Qur'an alone is sufficient to purify our religion from these concepts formulated by humans and attributed to the Lord of humans.

Chapter Seven: The Qur'anic Tongue as Criterion for Reflecting on Narrations
First: From the Question of Source to the Question of Text
It is not enough in examining a narration to ask: Who transmitted it? Nor to ask: Does it agree with a verse in its general meaning? There is another question connected to the text's structure itself: Is the transmitted narration formulated in a tongue consistent with the Qur'anic tongue in meaning construction, word functions, and relationships? This is an important criterion in reflecting on narrations, requiring we not settle for receiving authentication and weakening rulings, but read the text carefully and test it in light of the Qur'an.
This view rests on a methodological premise: correct prophetic elucidation does not contradict the Qur'an in its indication nor separate from its linguistic system; the Prophet learned the Qur'an and conveyed it, so consistency with the Qur'anic tongue is an essential indicator when examining what was attributed to him. But this premise remains an inferential criterion needing clarification of its steps and testing, not presented as a direct Qur'anic text or historical ruling established by mere assertion.
Second: Clarifying Terms Before Judgment
We should differentiate between three often-confused usages: divine patterns (the patterns and laws by which creation runs); prophetic Sunnah (if meant the Prophet's guidance or practice and elucidation); and hadith narration (a report reaching us attributed to him through human transmission). Research in the first is research in existence laws, the second in the Prophet's function and guidance, the third in a historical document needing examination. No ruling should transfer from one level to another without proof.
Also, the term "Sunnah" when appearing in some books of this project as elucidation or second source needs precise definition: Is the intended the established prophetic guidance, or the total recorded in narration books? If the first, it does not necessitate authenticating every narration; if the second, the reason for granting a specific narrative corpus legislative authority must be explained. Clarifying this usage removes confusion between respecting the Prophet's status and accepting everything attributed to him.
Third: What is Meant by the Qur'anic Tongue
The Qur'anic tongue is not a list of vocabulary extracted from a dictionary then projected onto the text, but a network of usages and relationships formed by context, word repetition, its counterparts, its position in structure, its function in discourse, and its connection to other verses and topics. Reflection thus begins from the Qur'anic text and its induction, not making dictionary definition a final ruling on every instance.
This does not mean canceling Arabic or dispensing with dictionaries and grammar; they are witnesses and assisting tools. The intent is they not become a higher reference by which a Qur'anic meaning is decided from outside Qur'anic usage itself. Dictionary is used to suggest possibilities, then these possibilities are tested by context, network induction, and criterion, declaring what remains hypothesis or interpretation.
Fourth: Two Complementary Paths, Not One
Examining a narration proceeds on two distinct paths. The first is historical: What is the transmission route? When was it recorded? What are its word and route differences? Are there independent indications supporting its attribution? These are questions of report establishment, not answered by mere beauty of text or agreement with Qur'anic meaning.
The second is linguistic-semantic: What are the transmitted text's words? Their relationships in context? Do they yield meaning consistent with Qur'anic usage after inducing relevant instances? Does the proposed reading withstand counter-verses and reverse testing? This path examines text acceptability from indication, but does not alone establish the Prophet said those words.
This differentiation becomes more necessary if some reports were narrated by meaning; the text before us may reflect the transmitter's wording choice, not the prophetic wording letter by letter. Then Qur'anic tongue testing focuses first on transmitted formulation and meaning, and we may not attribute its exact formulation to the Prophet without independent evidence.
Fifth: Practical Steps for Reflecting on the Text
1. Establish the narration text as in its source, recording copy, route, and word differences without merging different formulations into one text.
2. Determine the narration's topic and claim precisely, separating its words from explanations added by transmitter, commentator, or researcher.
3. Induce central word instances in the Qur'an, studying their functions, contexts, and relationships, instead of settling for a similar word or close meaning in a dictionary.
4. Compare text indication with relevant Qur'anic structure, including texts that may oppose the initial reading. Do not cherry-pick supporting evidence and leave the counter; present inference to all contexts.
