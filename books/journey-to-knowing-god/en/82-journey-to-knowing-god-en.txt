II. Translator's Note — The Bridge of Meaning
This English edition is a condensed conceptual adaptation. It is not a word-for-word translation of the Arabic publication. Its purpose is to carry the architecture of the argument, the methodological safeguards, and the main Qur'anic distinctions into clear English for an international reader.
Some Arabic Qur'anic terms are retained in transliteration because a single English equivalent would erase distinctions that are central to the book. Thus Allah, ilah, rabb, al-Rahman, qawl, kalam, kalimah, wahy, amr, nutq, dhikr, tadabbur, ayah, yaqeen, iman, and aman are explained in context rather than flattened into one-word translations.
Short Qur'anic renderings in this edition are sense-based English renderings prepared for this adaptation. References are given by surah and verse. The Arabic Qur'an remains the governing text.
The adaptation also preserves the author's distinction between a textual finding and a model. When the Arabic book leaves a lexical core OPEN, this English edition does the same. When a proposed equation has been RESISTED by counter-evidence, it is not restored merely because it sounds elegant in English.
For the International Reader
No prior commitment to Islamic theology is assumed. The book asks a narrower and more testable question: how does the Qur'an itself structure the question of God? Instead of beginning with a completed doctrinal picture and then collecting verses that fit it, the method starts from the text, its recurring vocabulary, its contrasts, and the relations among terms.
The central research sequence is: text → observation → induction → relations → hypothesis → testing → law → model. A hypothesis is not promoted to a law before resistant cases are examined. Adjacency is not causality. Textual order is not automatically a metaphysical sequence. A grammatical form is not automatically a complete meaning. A branch of a root is not the root itself. A previous book is not evidence for a new corpus simply because the same author wrote it.
This method does not claim neutrality in the sense of having no commitments. It has a clear commitment: the Qur'anic text must remain higher than the researcher's model of it. The reader is therefore invited not merely to accept conclusions, but to examine how those conclusions were reached.

Chapter 1 — Why Seek God?
Knowledge Without Comprehension
The question “Who is God?” is not one religious question among many. It rearranges the others: Where does existence come from? What gives truth its reference point? Why is the human being responsible? Where does the journey end? Yet the question becomes distorted when the answer is assumed in advance and the Qur'an is then searched for confirming quotations. In that case, the text is no longer a source of knowledge; it has become a witness called to defend a prior image.
This book therefore begins elsewhere. It asks how the Qur'anic discourse identifies its divine referent and what limits it places on human knowledge of that referent. The first task is not to abolish reason but to prevent reason from turning its own limited categories into a measure of God.
The Qur'an gives a decisive methodological tension in 42:11: “Nothing is like Him, and He is the Hearing, the Seeing.” The verse rejects likeness and, in the same movement, gives meaningful description. This blocks two opposite reductions. Divine transcendence does not require descriptive silence; and divine description does not authorize human-like modality. We may know what God communicates about Himself without claiming that the manner of the divine attributes is comparable to created experience.
This distinction can be stated simply: knowledge is not the same as mental picturing. Knowledge may arise from communication, relation, sign, effect, and attribution. Mental picturing tries to produce a shape, mechanism, or humanly imaginable mode. The Qur'an opens the first path while refusing to make likeness the measure of the second.
The same discipline appears in the discussion of vision. In 6:103 the Qur'an says that the sights do not idrak Him, while He idrak the sights. The Arabic root d-r-k is also used for reaching, catching up, or overtaking—death reaching a person, drowning overtaking someone, a pursuer catching a group. It is therefore methodologically unsafe to replace idrak with ru'ya, “seeing,” and then build a broader conclusion than the wording itself permits.
The story of Moses in 7:143 sharpens the distinction. Moses asks to be shown so that he may look. The response includes “You will not see Me,” an instruction to look at the mountain, and then the statement that the Lord tajalla to the mountain. The verse brings together ru'ya (seeing), nazar (looking), and tajalli (manifestation). The book therefore refuses to collapse the three into one concept. What the passage establishes directly is that the requested vision did not occur for Moses in that scene. A universal metaphysical law about every possible mode or future state would require a wider corpus.
The Qur'anic field of basar also extends beyond the physical eye. It includes basa'ir—forms of insight, evidence, and clarity. This provides a positive language for knowledge without forcing it into an imagined form. One may know through the Qur'an's names, signs, relations, and guidance without claiming essential comprehension of the divine reality.
The chapter therefore arrives at a disciplined answer rather than a total definition: Allah is the divine referent by which the Qur'an identifies the One to whom lordship, the Most Beautiful Names, creation, guidance, mercy, judgment, and return are attributed, while likeness is denied. The book does not claim that this “contains” God. It claims only that this is how the Qur'an begins to teach the reader to know without assimilating God to created things.
Methodological outcome
Knowledge of God is possible through what the Qur'an communicates; essential comprehension is not claimed.
Transcendence does not equal descriptive silence.
Idrak, ru'ya, nazar, basar, and tajalli should not be treated as automatic synonyms.
The universal law of divine vision remains OPEN in the research architecture.
Chapter 2 — The Divine Names as a Network
Allah, Ilah, Rabb, al-Rahman, and the Most Beautiful Names
If the Qur'an names the divine referent in more than one way, does that mean the names are interchangeable? The corpus says no. The challenge is to preserve two truths at once: the unity of the referent and the differentiation of linguistic function.
The clearest example is 17:110: “Call upon Allah, or call upon al-Rahman; whichever you call, to Him belong the Most Beautiful Names.” The singular pronoun establishes one referent while the wording preserves more than one name. Shared referent does not mean shared lexical function.
In the tested corpus, Allah functions as the central referential name. The Qur'an can say, in effect, “That is Allah, your Rabb; there is no ilah except Him” (6:102). The sentence itself distinguishes the terms. Allah identifies the referent; rabb marks a relationship; ilah belongs to the field of worship, devotion, or the object taken as ultimate.
Ilah cannot simply be equated with Allah because the word can occur in false or contested claims. One may “take desire as an ilah” (25:43), and Pharaoh can claim an ilah-status for himself. Such uses do not validate the claim; they reveal that ilah can name the object or direction of deification, worship, allegiance, or ultimate orientation. “There is no ilah except Him” therefore does more than repeat the word Allah; it negates rival ultimacy.
Rabb provides another kind of evidence. In the Joseph narrative, rabb can refer contextually to a human master or king: “mention me to your rabb” and “return to your rabb.” This prevents us from treating rabb as an exclusive proper name for God. When the Qur'an says “Allah, Rabb of the worlds,” Allah names the divine referent while rabb marks a relation of sovereignty, reference, care, rule, or governance whose exact shade is determined by context.
Al-Rahman points consistently to the divine referent in the tested material, yet its function should not be reduced too quickly to “gentleness.” The Qur'an can speak of “a punishment from al-Rahman” (19:45), which resists sentimental reduction. Earlier writings by the author proposed a functional emphasis in which Allah appears more strongly in contexts of command, the unseen, and legislation, while al-Rahman appears more strongly in contexts of creation, order, and cosmic law. The new testing retained this only as a hypothesis of functional salience, not as an exclusive division. Allah is explicitly “Creator of all things,” while al-Rahman also appears with permission, the unseen, promise, and judgment.
The Most Beautiful Names should therefore be read as a network of relation, not merely a list to memorize. Qur'an 7:180 links the names directly with invocation: “To Allah belong the Most Beautiful Names, so call upon Him by them.” The point is not magical power attached to syllables. The methodological question is: what does each name do in its Qur'anic context? What relation does it open between the human being and God? What changes if one name is substituted for another?
This approach also clarifies the author's earlier interest in ayloolah—the field of return, destination, and final outcome. The Qur'an strongly supports the theme that things return to God and that the final destination is to Him. But this thematic truth does not prove that the historical etymology of the name Allah comes from the root meaning “to return.” The book preserves the theme while rejecting the unsupported etymological equation.
Thus divine unity does not require the erasure of linguistic difference. Nor does linguistic difference imply multiple gods. The names and relational terms converge upon one referent while opening distinct angles of recognition and responsibility.
Chapter 3 — How Meaning Reaches the Human Being
Qawl, Kalam, Wahy, Amr, Nutq, and Kalimah
Ordinary language often compresses “saying,” “speaking,” “speech,” “revelation,” “command,” and “articulation” into one broad category. The Qur'anic corpus resists that flattening. At the same time, it also resists a modern engineering fantasy in which the terms become fixed layers of a technical pipeline.
Qawl—saying—is not necessarily audible sound. The Qur'an speaks of people “saying within themselves” (58:8). Qawl can carry a proposition, command, judgment, position, or attributed content. A cautious working description is therefore: an issued or attributed saying-content. This remains a candidate rather than a final root law.
The root k-l-m must be separated by form. Kallama, Form II, repeatedly establishes directed discourse: God spoke to Moses; the king spoke with Joseph; people ask how they can speak to a child in the cradle. Takallama, Form V, often describes engaging in speech or speaking about a matter. This difference becomes especially visible in 78:38: they do not speak except one whom al-Rahman permits, and who says what is right. The act of speaking and the content of what is said are related but not identical.
The noun kalam is relatively limited and should therefore be handled close to its contexts. The Qur'an speaks of “hearing the kalam of Allah” and of people hearing it and then distorting it after understanding. At minimum, kalam in these verses is discourse attributed to God and capable of being heard and received. The text does not require the researcher to begin with a philosophical theory of “speech as an internal meaning.”
Nutq is also broader than vocal sound. A book “speaks with truth” in 45:29 and 23:62. Skins are made to speak in 41:21. Solomon is taught the “language/articulation of birds” in 27:16. A strong candidate is that nutq makes content manifest or communicatively accessible in a way that can function as statement, evidence, or testimony. Again, that is a research candidate, not an exhaustive root definition.
Wahy is wider than prophetic revelation in the narrow sense. The Lord “reveals/inspires” the bee (16:68), Zechariah communicates to his people by wahy-like indication (19:11), and hostile agents can “inspire one another with embellished qawl” (6:112). In the last example, qawl can be the payload while wahy names the act of conveying. In 42:51, wahy also appears as one mode within God's communication with a human being. This is enough to show that wahy is not identical with qawl or kalam.
Amr, command or affair, is likewise broader than the grammatical imperative. The Qur'an speaks of God's amr when He wills something, of deciding an amr, and of managing the amr. Command, will, saying, judgment, and realization can meet in one verse without becoming a universal metaphysical sequence.
An early model in the research compared these terms to decision, protocol, channel, payload, and display. It was useful for generating questions, but it did not survive as a lexical law. The Qur'an shows a graph of relations: wahy may carry qawl; wahy may be a mode within taklim; qawl and nutq may coexist; taklim may frame an exchange in which several qawls occur; amr and qawl may be linked in a given context. A network is therefore safer than a pipeline.
The practical lesson is as important as the linguistic one. If qawl can occur “within the self,” moral responsibility is not limited to public sound. If a written record can “speak with truth,” testimony is not restricted to the human voice. If revelation can take more than one form, divine communication should not be reduced to a single human sensory mechanism.
Chapter 4 — The Words of God Do Not Run Out
Kalimah, Kalimat, Command, Promise, and Realization
The Qur'an states that if the sea were ink for the words of the Lord, the sea would be exhausted before the words of the Lord were exhausted, even if another sea were brought as reinforcement (18:109). It also says that if all the trees were pens and the sea, reinforced by seven more seas, were ink, the words of God would not run out (31:27).
The textual claim is non-exhaustion. It is important not to replace that wording with a different mathematical or philosophical thesis. “The words of God do not run out” is Qur'anic. “The words of God are mathematically uncountable” would be a further inference and needs its own argument.
The singular and plural should also be kept distinct. In 6:115 the singular “word of your Lord” is described as complete in truth and justice, followed by the plural “His words” with the statement that none can alter them. In 7:137 the “good word of your Lord” is fulfilled upon the Children of Israel because of their patience. Elsewhere, the Qur'an denies replacement or alteration of God's words. These recurring features—completion, truth, justice, fulfillment, stability, non-exhaustion—form a network, not yet a single definition.
Kalimah itself is not confined to one domain. The Qur'an speaks of a good word and a corrupt word, a common word between communities, a word of unbelief, the “Word of God” in relation to Jesus, and “the word of Allah” contrasted with the word of those who disbelieve. These resistant cases prevent a simple definition such as “word = creative command.”
The author's earlier books explored a wider hypothesis: God's words may include revealed communication, the creative “Be,” knowledge, laws, and the structures by which creation is ordered. The current book preserves that proposal as a model under examination. It does not treat every created thing as lexically identical with a kalimah merely because creation can be read as an ayah.
The Qur'an does place amr, qawl, and realization together in verses such as 36:82 and 19:35: when God wills or decides an affair, He says “Be,” and it is. Other verses speak of a prior word, a word becoming due, or a word being fulfilled. These relations matter, but they do not authorize a universal production line “word → command → realization.” Adjacency is not yet causality, and textual order is not automatically a metaphysical chronology.
The deepest consequence is epistemic humility. Human ink, categories, and models are finite. The Qur'an's language of non-exhaustion does not abolish study; it explains why study cannot honestly end in the claim that the divine field has been exhausted by one human formulation.
Chapter 5 — Dhikr
Revelation, Reminding, and Recovered Presence
If the words of God do not run out, what happens on the human side of reception? The term dhikr becomes central because it can refer to revealed content, an act of mentioning or remembering, a reminder directed to another, and a response of recollection or attentiveness.
The Qur'an can call the revealed message dhikr: “We sent down the dhikr, and We will preserve it” (15:9), and “We sent down to you the dhikr so that you may clarify to people what was sent down to them” (16:44). Yet these uses do not justify the formula “dhikr = Qur'an” in every occurrence. The same root operates across nouns and verbs with different structures.
The distinction between reminding and remembering is particularly important. A reminder may be delivered without the hearer actually remembering. The Qur'an can describe people who are reminded but do not remember. That single pattern blocks any automatic causality from external reminder to internal response.
One of the author's major intuitions was that dhikr is recovered or renewed presence of guidance in consciousness, and that tadabbur—deep reflection on the text—may prepare that presence. Qur'an 38:29 provides strong local support: the blessed Book is sent so that its verses may be reflected upon and so that people of understanding may remember. The connection is real. But the conjunction does not prove that every act of remembrance must be preceded by tadabbur, nor that every tadabbur necessarily produces remembrance.
For this reason, “referential presence” remains a useful functional candidate in a cognitive branch of the root rather than a complete definition of dhikr. Dhikr may also designate revealed content, verbal mentioning, reminding, worship-related practice, or a contextually determined act of reference.
A modest educational model can still be useful: communication reaches the person; attention becomes possible; relations are examined; remembrance or renewed presence may arise; an action may follow. But every arrow in that model remains a question to test. The model helps a reader organize practice; it does not become a law of the Qur'an.
The chapter therefore preserves the author's central insight while narrowing its claim: reflection can be a strong Qur'anic path toward remembrance, and dhikr can involve the return of guidance into active awareness, but neither statement should be universalized beyond the evidence.
Chapter 6 — From Recitation to Reflection
Reception Does Not Guarantee Understanding
Recitation, listening, attention, understanding, reflection, remembrance, and action can occur in one human process, but the Qur'an does not treat them as synonyms. This chapter asks what each contributes and what fails when the terms are collapsed.
Recitation can deliver the Book without guaranteeing obedience. Listening can occur without fiqh, understanding. Qur'an 6:25 describes people who listen while failing to understand, and 10:42 separates listening from reasoning. Qur'an 2:75 goes further: people hear the kalam of God, understand it, and then distort it. Reception and even cognition do not mechanically produce integrity.
A positive path also exists. Qur'an 39:18 describes those who listen to what is said and follow the best of it. The verse demonstrates that listening can be followed by discrimination and action, but it does not say that every listener will make that transition.
Tadabbur is more than repetition. The Qur'an connects the Book with reflection on its verses (38:29), asks why people do not reflect upon the Qur'an (4:82), and links failure of reflection with locked hearts (47:24). In the author's methodology, tadabbur therefore means attending to relations, context, consequences, and internal structure rather than merely increasing the number of recitations.
Still, even tadabbur is not a magical cause. In 38:29 reflection and remembrance are both presented as aims, but that does not establish an unbreakable sequence. The terms aql, fiqh, lubb, dhikr, and tadabbur occupy overlapping but non-identical fields.
For teaching, one may use a provisional sequence: reception → attention → examination → possible understanding/remembrance → response. The Qur'an, however, remains free to describe different combinations. A model may help the student ask better questions; it must not be smuggled back into the text as if the text itself had stated the model.
Chapter 7 — Ayah
Signs in the Text, the World, and Events
The Qur'an uses ayah for revealed verses, for features of the created order, and for historical or experiential signs. This breadth invites a powerful but risky synthesis. The book accepts the breadth while refusing to make every use identical.
Revealed passages are called ayat. Night, day, sun, moon, and created patterns are also called ayat. Historical events can function as ayat. The shared word invites comparison, but context determines what kind of sign is being discussed, how it is encountered, and what inference is justified.
The author's wider project often speaks of reading the written book and the visible world together. This is retained as a reading model, not as a literal claim that the Qur'an calls the cosmos a second book identical in structure with scripture. The world can contain signs without every object becoming lexically equivalent to a Qur'anic verse.
A sign also does not coerce belief. The Qur'an can address signs to those who reason, know, understand, or seek certainty, while also describing people who encounter signs and refuse them. The presence of evidence and the human response to evidence must therefore be separated.
The methodological procedure is simple but demanding: identify the sign, identify its context, identify the conclusion proposed, and then ask whether the conclusion exceeds the sign. This protects the reader from turning wonder into arbitrary symbolism or from treating any observed pattern as a divine message with a predetermined meaning.
The chapter thus preserves the relationship between revelation, creation, and reflection without collapsing them into one semantic object. Ayah opens a field of indication; it does not cancel interpretation, responsibility, or the possibility of rejection.
Chapter 8 — Yaqeen
Strength of Knowledge and the Limits of Response
The root y-q-n occurs twenty-eight times in the corpus examined by the project. The small size of the set made it possible to map all occurrences rather than rely on a sample. That complete map sharpened the book's treatment of certainty.
Yaqeen does not appear as one psychological ladder in every verse. It can concern the Hereafter, meeting the Lord, signs, judgment, report, or a community described as people of certainty. One verse is particularly resistant to simplistic theories: Pharaoh and his people reject the signs even though their inner selves had become certain of them (27:14). Certainty and public acknowledgment are therefore not the same thing.
The Qur'an does connect signs with certainty in local passages. Abraham is shown the dominion of the heavens and the earth “so that he may be among those who have certainty” (6:75). Signs are detailed “so that you may be certain of meeting your Lord” (13:2). These verses establish a purposive relation, but they do not create a universal psychological law in which every sign necessarily produces certainty.
The famous expressions 'ilm al-yaqeen, 'ayn al-yaqeen, and haqq al-yaqeen are Qur'anic phrases. Yet the Qur'an does not place all three in one explicit staircase. A three-stage epistemic ladder may be a useful interpretive model, but it must be labeled as a model rather than presented as a direct sequence stated by the text.
The verse “Worship your Lord until yaqeen comes to you” (15:99) is likewise kept within its context rather than forced into a root-wide definition. The book's current lexical candidate is modest: yaqeen often marks strong cognitive settledness toward a subject or judgment, while some nominal uses require their own contextual interpretation.
The practical lesson is severe: knowledge can become strong without becoming obedience. The human moral problem is not solved merely by increasing information. Certainty may exist while pride, injustice, or self-interest determines response.
Chapter 9 — Iman, Aman, and the Responsibility to Give Safety
A Functional Thesis With Declared Limits
The author's central functional thesis in this chapter is: iman should be examined not only by what a person claims to believe, but also by the aman—safety, trustworthiness, and security—that person's presence makes possible for others. The thesis is deliberately practical. It asks what knowledge and faith do in human relationships.
The Qur'anic root a-m-n strongly contains the field of security, trust, entrustment, and faith. The project's expanded map found 879 occurrences across seventeen morphological forms. Yet the same map also prevented a simplistic lexical equation. In 106:4, God “gave them security from fear,” and the sense of granting safety is direct. But one cannot replace “believe in God” with “give God safety.” The grammar and relational structure differ.
This matters because the book does not want to lose either side. It refuses the claim that iman is lexically identical with 'granting safety' in every construction; that equation was RESISTED. At the same time, it refuses to cut iman away from the semantic field of security, trust, entrustment, and amanah.
Qur'an 6:82 gives a particularly strong local relation: those who believe and do not clothe their faith in injustice—“for them is security, and they are guided.” The verse does not make iman and aman the same word, but it makes it impossible to discuss this context while ignoring the relation between faith, injustice, and security.
The Qur'an also protects an inward dimension. In 49:14, faith has not yet entered the hearts of the addressed group. This blocks any purely external definition. At the same time, inwardness does not remove the question of social effect. A person may speak about truth while remaining unsafe in power, dishonest in trust, or manipulative in knowledge.
The author's functional model therefore asks: does my knowledge reduce deception or refine it? Does my authority make the vulnerable more secure or more afraid? Do I preserve what has been entrusted to me? Do I distinguish evidence from inference so that another person can choose intelligently? These questions do not pronounce judgment on anyone's ultimate faith. They operationalize the ethical burden of the thesis.
The chapter is explicit about status: 'iman = granting safety' is not a closed lexical law. The a-m-n field of security/trust is a strong candidate. The inner dimension is supported. The link between faith and security is supported locally. The social thesis is retained as the author's functional model and remains open to further testing.
Chapter 10 — The Balance of Reading
Responsible Inference and Cognitive Safety
The final chapter turns the method back upon the book itself. A study of God and revelation is not protected from error merely because its subject is sacred. The greater the claim, the larger the corpus and the stronger the testing required.
The book therefore separates layers of statement: Qur'anic text, textual observation, induction, proposed relation, hypothesis, tested finding, provisional law, and model. A number count is a map of the corpus; it is not a semantic conclusion. A local finding in one construction is not a law for the whole root. A useful metaphor is not a divine description.
Counter-testing matters because good theories are most informative where they are under pressure. If one claims that certainty necessarily produces acceptance, 27:14 is not an inconvenience to be explained away; it is a test that narrows the claim. If one proposes that listening guarantees understanding, 6:25 and 2:75 resist the claim. If one proposes that al-Rahman belongs only to the domain of creation, verses that connect the name with permission, the unseen, promise, and judgment must be included.
Reverse testing asks whether a relation has been illegitimately inverted. If a verse gives reflection and remembrance as aims of the Book, this does not automatically prove that every remembrance must be preceded by reflection. If signs can contribute to certainty, it does not follow that certainty proves a specific sign sequence. If faith is associated with security, the words do not become synonyms.
The author calls for cognitive safety in research: the reader should be able to identify the source, the boundary of the claim, the evidence, and the route by which an inference was made. Cognitive safety is not a promise that the book cannot err. It is the opposite: it creates conditions in which error can be located, corrected, and traced.
The project itself records corrections. Mixed references and overly wide inferences were revised rather than hidden. Earlier authorial writings are treated as a history of questions and hypotheses, not as infallible evidence. The Qur'an remains higher than the explanatory model built around it.
This is the book's final methodological ethic: knowledge of God should increase the discipline with which one speaks about God, not the confidence with which one exceeds the evidence.
Conclusion — Knowledge Expands; Responsibility Deepens
The journey began with a question about God and ended with a question about the human being who speaks about God. The two cannot be separated. It is possible to reject likeness without rejecting knowledge; to receive names without collapsing their functions; to study speech without reducing it to sound; to read signs without forcing them to produce one response; to seek certainty without equating it with obedience; and to speak of faith while asking what safety one's presence gives to others.
The book's deepest claim is not that it has finished the subject. It is that a disciplined beginning is possible. We can distinguish the Qur'anic wording from our explanation, our explanation from our model, and our model from a law. We can preserve earlier insights without allowing earlier books to become evidence merely because they are ours. We can say OPEN when the corpus has not closed the question.
The statement that the words of God do not run out becomes, in this context, an invitation to humility rather than intellectual paralysis. The horizon is larger than the researcher's formulation, yet the researcher remains responsible for every formulation offered.
Knowing God in this book is therefore neither the possession of a mental image nor the accumulation of sacred vocabulary. It is a disciplined relation to revelation, signs, names, responsibility, and return. The journey continues because the text remains richer than the model and because responsibility grows with knowledge.

ayah (آية)
A sign. The term applies to revealed verses, created phenomena, and events; context determines the mode of signification.
yaqeen (يقين)
Strong settled cognitive certainty toward a subject or judgment; not identical with public acknowledgment or obedience.
iman (إيمان)
Faith/belief in its Qur'anic constructions. The author's functional model relates it to aman, safety and trust, without claiming a direct lexical identity in every occurrence.
aman / amanah (أمن / أمانة)
Safety, security, trust, entrustment. Part of the broader a-m-n semantic field.

Back Cover Text
What does it mean to know God without reducing God to a human image? How does the Qur'an distinguish Allah, ilah, rabb, and al-Rahman? Are saying, speech, revelation, command, and word simply synonyms? What is dhikr? Do signs automatically produce certainty? Can faith be tested by the safety and trust it creates in human relationships?
Journey to Knowing God approaches these questions through a corpus-driven reading of the Qur'an. Rather than beginning with inherited definitions, Nasser Ibn Dawood follows the vocabulary of the text, tests proposed meanings against resistant cases, and carefully distinguishes observation, hypothesis, law, and model.
This condensed English adaptation introduces the international reader to a method the author calls Fiqh of the Qur'anic Tongue: a disciplined attempt to study not only what the Qur'an says, but how its language produces distinctions, relations, and responsibility.
The result is neither a conventional theology manual nor a mystical portrait. It is an invitation to read with humility: to know without claiming comprehension, to interpret without replacing the text, and to let greater knowledge produce greater responsibility.
