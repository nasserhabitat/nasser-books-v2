II. Translator’s Note: The Bridge of Meaning
This English edition is a condensed conceptual adaptation. It is not a word-for-word translation, but rather an "extraction of essence." It presents the core philosophical framework in accessible English, omitting the exhaustive linguistic debates and classical references found in the original Arabic text.
For the academic researcher: The original Arabic version remains the primary source for comprehensive linguistic analysis, detailed exegesis (Tafsir), and the complete bibliography.
This adaptation also preserves the methodological status of the Arabic edition. A claim marked OPEN remains open; a CANDIDATE remains a candidate; a RESISTED or STRONGLY RESISTED claim is not revived through simplification; and a CLOSED result remains closed only within its declared scope. The aim is clarity without scientific inflation.
Key Qur’anic terms are sometimes retained in transliteration—such as nafs, tawaffī, barzakh, and baʿth—when a single English equivalent would prematurely narrow the field under study.
The final example terms in the translator’s note are general illustrations of the author’s translation policy; they are not all central objects of this volume.
Editorial and citation conventions
In the chapters below, Qur’anic references are given primarily by sūrah name and verse: for example, Sūrat al-Nisāʾ 4:3. An English rendering of a verse is a concise interpretive paraphrase unless explicitly presented as an Arabic quotation. Transliteration is used to protect contested meanings rather than to imply that a lexical dispute has been resolved.
Source-status labels follow the Arabic book: [N] text (naṣṣ), [B] linguistic/contextual explication (bayān), [S] inference (istinbāṭ), [F] testable hypothesis (farḍiyyah), and [W] operational model (namūdhaj). The review statuses CLOSED, CANDIDATE, OPEN, and RESISTED are not interchangeable with these evidence categories.


A Reader’s Map
The seven chapters follow a single movement: from the inalienable dignity of the person to the ethics of a solemn covenant; from the scrutiny of marriage, plural marriage, and inherited structures of subordination to the protection of rights when a covenant breaks; and finally from family ethics to a wider social practice of justice. The sequence is an authorial architecture, not a claim that the Qur’an places these topics in this exact order.

The central thesis
The dignity of woman and man has an intrinsic foundation. Their specific duties and legal entitlements are described in different textual contexts; differentiation in a particular legal rule is not, by itself, a proof of higher or lower humanity. Conversely, affirming equal intrinsic dignity does not authorize a researcher to erase explicit Qur’anic numbers, periods, conditions, or distinctions. The book’s intellectual task is to keep both commitments in view.
What this edition deliberately does not do
It does not provide a verse-by-verse commentary, adjudicate all historical schools of Islamic law, reproduce the full Arabic corpus registers, or claim that the ethical appeal of a proposed interpretation proves its Arabic grammar. It condenses the author’s arguments, counter-tests, uncertainty labels, and practical models. References to the author’s other works identify intellectual context, not independent proof of a new claim.
Method: Reading Text, Testing Claims, Limiting Models
Five tiers of argument
The author’s Qur’anic Concept Research Operating System (QCROS) starts with the text in its own setting. A word is examined through syntax, morphology, surrounding verses, the sūrah’s topic, and other Qur’anic instances. A relation proposed between passages is then distinguished from an inference or hypothesis. The researcher must actively seek a “resisting witness”—a passage that narrows or contradicts the proposed generalization—before declaring a rule.
TierWhat the tier can establishWhat it cannot establish automatically[N] TextThe stated words, conditions and immediate contextA universal rule outside that context[B] ExplicationA viable grammatical, lexical or contextual readingA preferred explanation without competing tests[S] InferenceA bounded ethical or legal conclusion from examined evidenceA new explicit wording in the Qur’an[F] HypothesisA proposal that can be checked and resistedA CLOSED result merely because it is attractive[W] ModelA human tool for applying a principleA divinely prescribed institutional design
A counter-test is not an insult to an interpretation
If a passage is claimed to prove that all women are incapable of rational dispute, Qur’anic scenes of women arguing, deliberating and recommending policies resist the universal claim. Yet those counterexamples do not automatically prove a new, alternative grammatical referent for the disputed passage. Refuting one thesis and establishing another require different evidentiary steps. The same discipline governs polygyny, qiwāmah, milk al-yamīn, divorce, and remarriage.
Mathānī and functional non-synonymy
The System of Mathānī invites investigation of paired structures, letter patterns and relationships among terms. It can generate testable questions; it cannot, on its own, cancel ordinary Arabic grammar or a directly stated condition. Similarly, non-synonymy encourages readers to distinguish such terms as sakan, mawaddah and raḥmah, or ṭalāq, tasrīḥ and firāq. But difference must be established from usage rather than asserted as a universal law about every occurrence.
Status discipline
A result may be CLOSED for a narrow observation while a wider thesis remains OPEN. The numerical meaning of the Arabic forms mathnā, thulātha and rubāʿa is supported by cross-Qur’anic usage; what a contemporary state should require as evidence of fairness in every marriage case remains a legal and policy question. Similarly, verses that commend freeing captives or prohibit coercion do not by themselves prove that every historical form of slavery was abolished at one definitive moment. The book protects that gap instead of concealing it.
Chapter One — Dignity, the Right to Life, and the Freedom to Speak
1.1. The disputed verse must be read within its argument
The first chapter begins not by inventing a flattering answer to a difficult text but by examining the argument in which it appears. Sūrat al-Zukhruf 43:18 contains the disputed description of one “brought up in adornment” who is “not clear in dispute.” Its immediate environment (43:15–21) criticizes those who attribute daughters to God while preferring sons for themselves. The book asks whom the description addresses, how the relative construction works, and whether the rhetorical attack repeats a cultural stereotype in order to expose its inconsistency. These are questions of language and context, not matters decided by moral preference alone.
The Arabic study compares several explanatory trajectories: reference to females as imagined by the original opponents; reference to a male disputant whose reasoning fails; and a reported interpretation involving adorned idols. Each has different grammatical and contextual costs. The fact that the passage belongs to a polemic does not by itself select one interpretation. Equally, the presence of a feminine social stereotype in a discourse does not prove that the Qur’an establishes that stereotype as a universal natural law. Accordingly, the precise referent and a causal theory of adornment remain OPEN.
1.2. Women as speaking agents
The case against an intrinsic incapacity for female argument is constructed independently. In Sūrat al-Mujādilah 58:1, the woman who disputes with the Prophet and raises her complaint is heard. In Sūrat al-Naml 27:32–35, the Queen of Sheba consults others and offers a reasoned political assessment. In Sūrat al-Qaṣaṣ 28:26, a daughter recommends a worker on the grounds of strength and trustworthiness. These examples establish the presence of women’s speech, judgment and deliberation in the Qur’anic narrative. They are not proof that every utterance by a woman is correct; they resist the assertion that the female sex is by nature unable to articulate reasons.
From that evidentiary base the author infers an ethic of hearing and testing a claim on its merits. Practical opportunities for consultation, education and complaint are discussed as means of securing agency, but the procedures of a modern hearing, school or administrative appeal are human designs. They belong to the tier of the operational model, not to the verbatim content of the verses.
1.3. Al-mawʾūdah: the murdered child and the limits of metaphor
Sūrat al-Takwīr 81:8–9 invokes the female child who was buried alive and is asked for what sin she was killed. The book insists that the concrete killing must not be erased by a more convenient symbolic reading. Its connection to other passages on killing children is an ethical and thematic comparison, while the individual expressions retain their distinct scope and settings.
The author then offers “cognitive and social burial” (al-waʾd al-maʿrifī wa-l-ijtimāʿī) as a critical metaphor for suppressing a person’s learning, voice, informed choice or opportunity to challenge a decision. This is not presented as the dictionary meaning of al-mawʾūdah. The metaphor is legitimate as a research tool only when the alleged suppression is described concretely and distinguished from ordinary disagreement, warranted criticism and the physical crime addressed by the verse.
1.4. Result and boundary
The chapter supports a narrow, durable result: the Qur’anic text contains active female speakers and an unequivocal scene of accountability for the killing of a girl. The disputed referent of 43:18, an alleged causal pathway from upbringing to poor argument, and the predictive effectiveness of an institutional “anti-silencing” model remain questions for further investigation. Agency is not measured by verbal victory; assistance in expressing an intention may support an individual without removing that individual’s dignity.
Chapter Two — Fiqh al-Wijdān and the Solemn Covenant
2.1. Tranquility, affection, mercy: a constellation, not a formula
In Sūrat al-Rūm 30:21, the account of spouses speaks of sakan, mawaddah and raḥmah: repose or tranquility, affection, and mercy. The chapter studies them as a relational constellation. Sakan points toward a place of repose and settled life; mawaddah to a bond expressed through caring action; raḥmah to consideration and kindness when the other is vulnerable. This differentiation is offered as an interpretive synthesis governed by context, not as a rigid lexicon in which each word has only one function.
The presence of an intended end does not mean every marriage attains it. The Qur’an also speaks of alienation, disputes, harm and separation. A marriage that fails to produce tranquility cannot be declared tranquil merely because the word appears among its aims. The author’s expression “reducing existential noise” may help modern readers imagine the lived effects of repose, but it is explicitly a metaphor, not a Qur’anic definition of sakan.
2.2. A firm covenant must remain humane
Sūrat al-Nisāʾ 4:21 refers to the “solemn covenant” (mīthāq ghalīẓ) and to spouses’ having come into intimate relation with one another. The Arabic book connects the seriousness of this covenant with affection and mercy, while rejecting the inference that the contract licenses ownership of the other person. The ethical claim is two-sided: emotional warmth cannot invalidate due financial rights, and contractual rights cannot be used as an excuse for cruelty.
The mutually addressed garment imagery of Sūrat al-Baqarah 2:187 deepens this reading: “they are a garment for you and you are a garment for them,” in a concise English paraphrase. The figure supports mutual intimacy and protection; it does not prescribe identical functions in every context. A related argument defends the man’s dignity against the reduction of his person to earnings, while defending the woman’s dignity against reduction to reproductive or sexual utility. Both depend on mutual care, although both remain accountable for their specific duties.
2.3. Consent, generosity and the boundary against coercion
The issue of ṭīb al-nafs—willingness of the self or genuine contentment—is discussed in connection with Sūrat al-Nisāʾ 4:4 and the treatment of a spouse’s monetary entitlement. The text’s mention of a freely given concession cannot be equated with capitulation under fear, misinformation or unequal pressure. The author proposes three practical questions: Did the person understand the right and its extent? Could the person actually decline without retaliation? Was the concession free of manipulation or coercion? These questions form a useful operational check, not a new list of conditions quoted verbatim from the verse.
The book examines ʿaḍl—wrongful obstruction or prevention—alongside other restrictions on coerced marriage or forced concessions, especially Sūrat al-Nisāʾ 4:19 and Sūrat al-Baqarah 2:232. The right to refuse is distinguished from the moral evaluation of every refusal; not every chosen action is thereby wise or justified. Nor is every instance of care a disguised form of control. These distinctions defend both personal agency and ethical accountability.
2.4. Consultation and the scope of social design
The explicit pairing of mutual agreement and consultation concerning weaning in Sūrat al-Baqarah 2:233 provides a concrete textual witness to shared parental deliberation in that matter. Moving from it to a standing family council, private mediation service, or standardized agreement is a plausible institutional application, not a divine instruction describing a particular office. The same caution applies to qiwāmah: the presence of expenditure in Sūrat al-Nisāʾ 4:34 is part of the text; proposals about its cessation, transfer or legal enforcement in changed circumstances cannot be derived automatically from that one observation.
2.5. Status of this chapter
The textual anchors—tranquility and mercy, the solemn covenant, the mutual garment image, agreed consultation and resistance to coercion—are supported in their contexts. A wider thesis of “emotional dignity” is a strong candidate in the author’s broader research rather than an exhaustive revealed legal definition. The emotional and institutional models remain CANDIDATE or OPEN according to their scope. This chapter does not turn a theory of affect into a replacement for marriage law.
Chapter Three — Marriage, Polygyny, Equity and Justice
3.1. Sūrat al-Nisāʾ begins with vulnerable persons
The third chapter approaches Sūrat al-Nisāʾ 4:3 in the setting established by the sūrah’s opening concern for orphans, their property and fair treatment. The verse’s movement from fear of failing in justice toward orphans to marriage “two, three, or four,” and then to one upon fear of failing in justice, cannot be assessed by detaching its clauses. Yet the opening reference to orphans does not alone prove that every marriage envisaged is literally a guardianship arrangement. The relationship between the immediate setting and the rule’s wider application requires its own argument.
The Arabic edition considers interpretations that would recast the numerical forms as entirely non-numerical or treat nikāḥ as a general act of patronage. Its cross-tests resist that redefinition. In Sūrat Fāṭir 35:1, comparable forms describe wings “in twos, threes and fours.” Sūrat Sabaʾ 34:46 offers another numerical construction. Above all, the return to “one” inside Sūrat al-Nisāʾ 4:3 anchors a numerical contrast. The basic numerical sense is supported; that conclusion does not settle all legal questions about plural marriage.
3.2. Two verses on justice must be read together
Sūrat al-Nisāʾ 4:3 connects plurality to fear of failing in ʿadl, directing the reader toward one in that case. Verse 4:129 observes that complete equality among women cannot be attained, however earnestly desired, and forbids inclining so entirely as to leave a woman suspended. The book reads the two without nullifying either. The inability to make the human heart experience identical affection need not entail the impossibility of observing fairness in time, provision, non-injury or other observable treatment. Conversely, legal permission cannot be recast as permission to harm or suspend a spouse in uncertainty.
The phrase dhālika adnā allā taʿūlū at the close of 4:3 receives particular attention because the word’s explanation has been disputed. The chapter does not use that uncertainty to cancel the rule’s explicit conditional structure. It also does not claim that “one wife for everyone at all times” has been proved as an unconditional Qur’anic statement. A socially preferred baseline, judicial policy and the text’s stated conditions are separable kinds of proposition.
3.3. Beyond financial capacity
The study rejects the reduction of wives to possessions acquired through sufficient wealth. Money matters because contractual obligations and ordinary needs matter, but financial abundance cannot demonstrate justice of treatment, the absence of coercion, or the fulfillment of emotional and parental responsibilities. When a legal system designs safeguards for demonstrable harm, it should identify the source and jurisdiction of those safeguards rather than claiming the Qur’an itself names every document, court procedure or monetary threshold.
The author’s analysis of qiwāmah similarly refuses shortcuts. The verse’s inclusion of spending is textual. Whether authority lapses, transfers, persists or changes form when spending becomes impossible is a different dispute. Neither a modern proposal for equal administrative partnership nor an inherited institutional claim becomes textual merely by being placed beside the verse.
3.4. Counter-witness and outcome
A thesis that the forms mathnā, thulātha and rubāʿa possess no numerical meaning meets resisting witnesses in the language of other verses. A thesis that polygyny is an unrestricted privilege meets the explicit fear-of-injustice condition and the prohibition on leaving someone suspended. A thesis that the heart’s imperfect equality voids every possible practical standard meets the remaining command not to incline wholly. Thus the study supports the forms’ numerical sense and the force of the ethical limits, while recording wider juristic conclusions and institutional design as separate work.
Chapter Four — Milk al-Yamīn: History, Dependency and Human Dignity
4.1. The method begins with the full range of uses
This is the volume’s most sensitive historical and legal inquiry. The phrase mā malakat aymānukum, often rendered “what your right hands possess,” cannot be settled by choosing a modern ethical label in advance. Its occurrences involve different speakers, relationships and legal contexts. The book notes that related expressions are addressed to women as well as to men—for example, in Sūrat al-Nūr 24:31 and Sūrat al-Aḥzāb 33:55. That observation complicates any definition which treats the phrase as referring only to a man’s sexual relation. It does not, however, establish that the expression has one uniform meaning of contract, employment, kinship or guardianship wherever it appears.
Three kinds of material are carefully separated: the Qur’anic words themselves; historical institutions of enslavement and concubinage described in later sources; and a contemporary hypothesis that the Qur’an’s moral direction can be reconstructed as a “covenant of care and protection.” Evidence that a historical society practiced slavery cannot, without further argument, prove that the Qur’an created or endorsed each practice. Conversely, finding verses on liberation cannot by itself prove the immediate historical abolition of every form of slavery.
4.2. Nikāḥ, permission and integrity in Sūrat al-Nisāʾ
Sūrat al-Nisāʾ 4:25 discusses a specified marriage situation involving women under a condition of dependency and includes permission, a marital payment and iḥṣān. These elements matter. The text does not support translating that particular situation into unrestricted sexual access without attention to the conditions it states. Still, the existence of one conditioned form of nikāḥ does not show that every occurrence of milk al-yamīn has the same transactional and legal character. The identity of “their people” or “their household” in the permission clause also requires historical and interpretive examination; a modern paraphrase must not silently settle that question.
The author tests whether nikāḥ and zawāj have identical or contextually differentiated ranges. The lexical inquiry can uncover differences of emphasis, but a proposed distinction cannot automatically erase a marital legal situation in the verses. Likewise, a philosophical reading of yamin as pledge or responsibility is a candidate for investigation, not a universal replacement for the established historical and linguistic senses of possession, authority or dependency.
4.3. Manumission, non-coercion and release
Sūrat al-Nūr 24:32–33 contains distinct elements: encouragement to arrange marriage for eligible persons; a conditional direction concerning mukātabah, an emancipation agreement; and a prohibition on forcing dependent young women into prostitution when they seek protection. The condition “if you know good in them” belongs to the mukātabah passage and cannot be omitted when reconstructing its scope. The ethical resistance to sexual coercion is powerful; moving from the passage to a complete penal statute covering every contemporary form of trafficking requires other legal premises.
The theme of freeing a person from bondage appears elsewhere, including Sūrat al-Balad 90:13. Sūrat Muḥammad 47:4 discusses release by grace or ransom in a specific setting of war captives. These passages furnish evidence for emancipation and humane treatment in their respective domains. Their relation to the complete legal history of slavery is an inferential and historical problem, not an equation proved by two examples. The study rejects both the casual claim that every historical enslavement practice is a Qur’anic ideal and the claim that all forms of legal dependency ended universally at one clearly specified date on the basis of these verses alone.
4.4. Competing approaches retained as competing approaches
The book examines interpretations that identify milk al-yamīn primarily with historical bondage, with protection or care, or with non-slavery relationships such as dependent children and persons under guardianship. It tests each view against counter-passages. A pure “modern protection contract” reading has difficulty accounting for historical referents and the wording of certain verses; a pure “unrestricted male entitlement” reading must account for female addressees and explicit constraints on coercion, marriage and liberation. Neither discomfort with historical practices nor enthusiasm for a reformist model supplies an independent grammatical proof.
The proposed “covenant of care” (mīthāq al-riʿāyah) is explicitly an authorial explanatory model. It can inspire contemporary safeguards for vulnerable workers, displaced people or those dependent on institutions. But these modern protections must not be described as a transfer of the old possession category into employment or a revival of human ownership. Jurisdictions may criminalize trafficking and coercion under current law; the author’s moral inference is distinct from the text of those statutes.
4.5. A research result deliberately left open
CLOSED within scope: the identified passages contain the quoted words, and the conditions of their individual verses must be respected. SUPPORTED as an ethical inference: they offer substantial grounds for care, integrity, non-coercion and emancipation in the domains they address. OPEN: whether a single Qur’anic definition can encompass every use of milk al-yamīn; precisely how historical concubinage should be related to the text; the full legal consequences of the end of slavery; and the general validity of the “covenant of care” model. This chapter’s restraint is central to the integrity of the translation.
Chapter Five — Marital Conflict, Divorce, Waiting Periods and Rights
5.1. Nushūz is not an exclusively female label
Sūrat al-Nisāʾ 4:34 addresses fear of nushūz in relation to a wife; Sūrat al-Nisāʾ 4:128 mentions fear of nushūz or aversion by a husband. The term therefore cannot be presented as intrinsically and exclusively a female defect. This shared lexical presence does not make every response, procedure or sanction identical across the two contexts. The text also points, at 4:35, to the appointment of one arbiter from the husband’s family and one from the wife’s. Symmetry in the number of arbiters does not by itself specify complete equality of all legal powers, proceedings or results.
A responsible rendering does not resolve the controversial imperative wa-ḍribūhunna in 4:34 by citing the abstract root alone or by declaring that a general preference for kindness cancels difficult wording. The Arabic work records that lexical, exegetical and practical consequences remain to be examined, including the safeguarding of persons from actual harm. A condensed translation must not launder that OPEN debate into a falsely unanimous verdict.
5.2. Three expressions, connected but not identical
The chapter studies ṭalāq (divorce or release), tasrīḥ (letting go or releasing in a fitting manner), and firāq (separation) as related expressions with different settings. The author proposes that they help us see a process rather than imagine divorce as an unregulated utterance devoid of consequences. Yet the terms are not assigned universal, mechanically sequential stages applicable in exactly the same way to every case. The distinction is a contextual reading, with any procedural model assessed separately.
Sūrat al-Baqarah 2:228–232 and Sūrat al-Ṭalāq 65:1–7 contain substantive constraints on timing, residence, rights, the intention to repair a relationship, and protection against retaining a spouse to cause harm. Sūrat al-Baqarah 2:231 explicitly resists holding a woman merely to injure her. The author argues that neither a passing emotional impulse nor a desire to punish the other party is an adequate account of the ethical content of divorce.
5.3. ʿIddah is a legally structured period
The waiting period, ʿiddah, is not simply a psychological metaphor. Sūrat al-Baqarah 2:228 specifies three qurūʾ in the relevant situation, a term whose classical interpretation is disputed between menstrual periods and intervals of purity. Sūrat al-Ṭalāq 65:4 discusses other specified conditions, including pregnancy. Sūrat al-Aḥzāb 33:49 addresses divorce before marital contact, and Sūrat al-Baqarah 2:234 deals with a widow’s waiting period. These are different legal conditions with time-bound consequences. A single modern phrase such as “three cycles of emotional healing” would efface distinctions the Arabic volume expressly preserves.
The author’s “waiting-period laboratory” is thus a limited educational image: during a legally specified interval, parties may reflect, document obligations and avoid impulsive retaliation. This can be helpful as a human communication model. It cannot replace Qur’anic measures or establish that every case has exactly the same duration or purpose. The temporal, biological, financial and social dimensions must be kept separate enough to protect actual rights.
5.4. Housing, property and the difference between rights
Sūrat al-Ṭalāq 65:1 protects residence during the relevant waiting period and includes a stated exception. An accurate paraphrase therefore avoids an absolute formulation that deletes that exception. Sūrat al-Ṭalāq 65:6–7 addresses housing and provision in additional circumstances. Sūrat al-Baqarah 2:229, 2:232–233 and neighboring passages raise matters of marital gifts, financial release, support, breastfeeding and consultation. These are not all one undifferentiated financial entitlement. Each depends on its own facts, timing and conditions.
The study pays special attention to iftidāʾ—the language of compensation or release in Sūrat al-Baqarah 2:229—rather than projecting the later juristic term khulʿ back into the verse as though the two were identical in every legal detail. Likewise, legal documentation is a reasonable contemporary way to protect facts and rights, but its specific form and compulsory status belong to the law of the relevant society unless established by a separate textual argument.
5.5. Children must not become instruments of conflict
Sūrat al-Baqarah 2:233 combines attention to care, nursing, financial responsibilities and the injunction against harming either parent through a child. Where weaning is discussed, the verse expressly mentions mutual consent and consultation. The Arabic work draws from this a practical ethic of shielding children from retaliation and sustaining responsible care after separation. It proposes structured records of support, clear channels for consultation and independent means of resolving disputes. Those are operational proposals, not a Qur’anic family-court manual.
5.6. A bounded conclusion
The chapter establishes an interconnected network of textual obligations without turning every word into a free-standing law. It also distinguishes the moral duty to avoid harm from the separate judicial question of which remedy a particular court can order. A woman or man need not be portrayed as morally faultless to retain entitlements that still apply. A desire for reconciliation cannot justify indefinite detention within a harmful relationship; separation itself must not be romanticized as always desirable.
Chapter Six — Limits of Return and the Critique of Legal Evasion
6.1. The textual condition cannot be wished away
Sūrat al-Baqarah 2:229 states “divorce is twice” in the setting of retaining a spouse with recognized good conduct or releasing her graciously. Verse 2:230 then describes a later condition in which the woman does not become lawful to the former husband again until she marries another husband; the passage continues with possible return after the subsequent marriage ends and where the former couple judge that they can maintain God’s limits. These elements must remain visible in any ethically persuasive summary.
The Qur’anic passage does not itself contain the later technical label “major irrevocable divorce” (baynūnah kubrā), nor does it name “the intermediary husband” (muḥallil). Those labels belong to juristic discussion. The author reads the condition as a serious boundary against treating the covenant as an object of manipulation, while distinguishing his interpretation of its purpose from the direct rule that the verse articulates.
6.2. A true second marriage and the problem of sham arrangements
The Arabic study criticizes practices in which a person is recruited for a temporary, instrumental relationship solely to manufacture a route back to the former spouse. Such practices may humiliate both the woman and the man cast in the role of intermediary, especially where poverty, pressure or deception is involved. This is an ethical critique and an invitation to investigate the evidence of classical positions. It is not a claim that every juristic school throughout history agreed on one comprehensive rule about every contractual intention, consummation or judicial consequence.
The author therefore keeps apart three questions: What exactly is required by Sūrat al-Baqarah 2:230? What have jurists concluded from additional traditions and legal reasoning? What safeguards should modern systems require to establish informed consent and protect against sham arrangements? None can answer the others merely by sharing a moral concern. An interpretation that rightly condemns coercion must still face the scriptural condition; an interpretation that stresses a formal condition must still examine the reality of coercion and misuse.
6.3. The plural “they may return”
The phrase an yatarājaʿā directs attention to mutual action, and the context connects return to an expectation of observing God’s limits. It may support an ethical emphasis on the intentions and agency of both persons. It does not, in isolation, provide a complete civil-law code for remarriage. Likewise, the author’s expression “existential repositioning” is a model of how a relationship may sometimes end and only later be reconsidered under changed conditions. It is not the lexical meaning of nikāḥ or a way to reduce marriage to a technical circuit.
6.4. Status and counter-test
The scriptural threshold is fixed in its own context. The classifications of particular marriage arrangements, intentions, evidence and judicial validity remain matters of specialized jurisprudence and applicable law. The ethical rejection of exploiting another person is strong; it does not authorize an unsupported historical consensus. The proposed existential model is permitted as a limited analogy and may not be used to apply the numerical rules of divorce to companies, research partnerships or public institutions.
Chapter Seven — From Relational Ethics to Social Practice
7.1. Against the stigma of separation
The final chapter turns from analysis of legal language to the culture surrounding those who enter, sustain or leave a marriage. Divorce can be accompanied by shame, public judgment, financial threat or the weaponization of children. In some environments these pressures fall more severely on women, yet men also can be degraded by caricatures that deny vulnerability or treat a person’s economic worth as the whole measure of his humanity. The book’s proposed remedy is not the celebration of divorce but the recognition that an orderly separation with preserved rights need not be a moral disgrace.
Tasrīḥ bi-iḥsān, gracious release, offers the chapter a central ethical orientation. It should not become a command for an injured person to remain in danger or waive what is legally due. Neither should language of kindness obscure the facts of harm. The author seeks a balance in which attempts at repair are not coerced, and separation does not license revenge, humiliation or invasion of privacy.
7.2. The proposed family-equity indicators
The Arabic text sketches four domains for assessment in social services and institutions. These are suggested measures, not a set of indicators directly numbered by revelation. They can help a practitioner ask whether formal procedures actually serve the persons involved.

Their enforcement mechanisms depend on local law, professional ethics and demonstrable benefit. To test an indicator one would need suitable cases, voluntary and privacy-preserving data, and attention to unwanted burdens imposed by the intervention. Without such evaluation, the instrument remains a candidate model.
7.3. Existential repositioning beyond marriage
“Existential repositioning” (iʿādat al-tamawḍuʿ al-wujūdī) is an authorial metaphor describing the possibility of withdrawing from a destructive arrangement without denying the legitimacy of earlier cooperation, and perhaps entering a new relationship under better conditions. The book suggests that this may illuminate institutional associations, professional arrangements and community initiatives. But the analogy is limited: Qur’anic rules about spouses, divorce counts, waiting periods or remarriage are not transferable as legal conditions to a business agreement or a research consortium.
A sensible non-marital application might involve a transparent way to end a collaboration, settle outstanding obligations, protect vulnerable participants and offer impartial review. Its success must be assessed with ordinary institutional evidence. The Qur’an offers moral bearings; human organizations must supply practical design, revise errors and comply with the governing legal framework.
7.4. From personal ethics to just institutions
Sūrat al-Ḥadīd 57:25 places qist (equity or just dealing) within a broad account of revelation and human responsibility. The author uses this to extend the discussion from household fairness to community conduct without erasing the differences among legal domains. Justice, recognized good dealing (maʿrūf) and mercy should be distinguishable yet mutually supportive aspirations. A law or institution should be evaluated for its effects on consent, coercion, poverty, the care of children and the protection of private life, not judged solely by its declared good intentions.
The conclusion is deliberately neither a detailed judicial code nor a free-floating spirituality of relationships. It is a call to keep human dignity and actual duties visible together: enough legal clarity to prevent arbitrary injury, enough moral imagination to support compassion, and enough intellectual restraint not to confuse a contemporary regulatory model with divine speech.
Synthesis — Dignity Without Erasing Difference
Part I of this research separated intrinsic human worth from particular functions, capacities and responsibilities. Part II applies that distinction to marriage, intimacy, dependency, conflict and separation. The argument does not require women and men to have identical experiences or identical obligations in every cited passage. It requires that a particular legal difference not be silently converted into a judgment of intrinsic human superiority or inferiority.
The same balance constrains the reform-minded interpreter. Where the Qur’an articulates a number, a period, a condition, an exception or a protected entitlement, the text cannot be dissolved into an abstract metaphor for human flourishing. Conversely, where custom has attached an additional hierarchy, denial of agency or institutional practice to a verse, the burden of proof rests on the one making the attachment. Context, independent corroboration and resisting witnesses matter more than the polemical attractiveness of a conclusion.
In the volume’s relational vision, the woman cannot be reduced to an object of adornment, a possession or an instrument for another person’s legal maneuver. The man cannot be reduced to salary, force or biological appetite. Both may need care, exercise reason, undertake duties, cause harm or suffer harm. Ethical maturity lies neither in removing all restrictions nor in treating unequal power as automatically legitimate; it lies in a disciplined accounting of who holds which right, on what basis, and with what protection against coercion.
Qur’anic verses concerning sakan, mawaddah, raḥmah, the solemn covenant, consultation, the conditions of marital justice and the protection of children form a powerful field of inquiry. That field remains intellectually honest only when it records its open problems. An unresolved referent in a verse does not disappear when the writer’s moral purpose is sound. An emerging social model does not become compulsory merely because it cites the Qur’an. A clear verse retains its own wording even when a broader modern policy is being considered.

Guide for the Researcher and the Reader
Questions for a responsible continuation
Further investigation should begin with the Arabic text and the precise limits of each passage. For al-Zukhruf 43:18, test all proposed antecedents rather than choosing the ethically preferred one. For al-Nisāʾ 4:3 and 4:34, separate numbers and grammatical conditions from broader theories of family roles. For milk al-yamīn, distinguish the Qur’anic corpus, later records of slavery and modern models of dependency. For qurūʾ, reconstruct the competing readings before proposing a policy based on time or bodily cycles. For al-Baqarah 2:230, distinguish the stated condition from the later technical discussion of marriage intention and sham procedures.
The Arabic edition includes a fuller register of verses, historical references, comparative tables and proposed practical cards. A researcher who intends to cite a specific linguistic interpretation, classical view or legal ruling should consult the Arabic R7 text and locate the primary source, edition and page independently. The present work is intended to open a line of inquiry, not to disguise the absence of that final reference check.

Closing reflection
The goal of the study is a balance in which dignity does not erase responsibility and responsibility does not swallow dignity. Every person should be heard as an accountable agent; every specific Qur’anic statement should be treated according to its evidence and context; and every proposed institution should be tested for whether it genuinely reduces harm. The work invites the reader to continue the investigation, challenge its inferences and allow the evidence—not the desired conclusion—to fix the status of each claim.
