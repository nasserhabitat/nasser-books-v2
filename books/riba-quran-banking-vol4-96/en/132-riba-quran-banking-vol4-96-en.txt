II. Translator’s Note: The Bridge of Meaning
This English edition is a condensed conceptual adaptation. It is not a word-for-word translation, but rather an “extraction of essence.” It presents the core philosophical framework in accessible English, omitting the exhaustive linguistic debates and classical references found in the original Arabic text.
For the academic researcher, the original Arabic version remains the primary source for comprehensive linguistic analysis, detailed exegesis (tafsir), and the complete bibliography.
This adaptation also preserves the methodological status of the Arabic edition. A claim marked OPEN remains open; a CANDIDATE remains a candidate; a RESISTED or STRONGLY RESISTED claim is not revived through simplification; and a CLOSED result remains closed only within its declared scope. The aim is clarity without scientific inflation.
Key Qur’anic terms are sometimes retained in transliteration—such as nafs, tawaffī, barzakh, and baʿth—when a single English equivalent would prematurely narrow the field under study.
Scope of this volume
Volume Four completes the product audits and methodological framework in Chapters 87–99. Chapters 87–93 examine credit cards, deposits, investment accounts, rescheduling, late-payment claims, debtor states, and sukuk. Chapters 94–98 develop the Unified Transaction Variable Matrix (UTVM) and its component matrices. Chapter 99 sets out how an independent researcher can repeat the tests. The summaries condense the argument and do not reproduce all source tables, claim cards, regulatory references, or case evidence.
Governing boundary
The Qur’anic corpus remains the evidence base for investigating the meaning of riba. Contemporary contracts, product pages, regulations, accounting standards, and financial records describe applications; they do not define the Qur’anic term. RIBA-QTAM and UTVM organize evidence and questions, but neither is an automatic classifier.
The final definition of riba remains NOT SEALED. Classification readiness remains OPEN / NOT PASSED. E*-R2—the ground of entitlement together with the bilateral-right boundary—remains a STRONG AUDIT CANDIDATE / OPEN, not a completed semantic rule. The volume’s matrices make the analysis more consistent and reproducible; they do not close the underlying question.
Part IX — Completing the financial product laboratory (Chapters 87–93)
Chapter 87 — Credit cards
The credit-card audit follows events, balances, and claims rather than treating “credit card” as one uniform transaction. It distinguishes the payment instrument from credit extended to the customer and maps the account’s different balance categories, statement cycle, payment timing, grace period, payment allocation, fees, rates, and post-due claims.
Rules and standards from contemporary regulators, payment networks, and standard-setting bodies are used as application sources, not as Qur’anic authorities. A real case requires the card agreement, fee schedule, term-cost disclosures, statements, transaction logs, balance-bucket data, and payment-allocation rules. The chapter does not infer a classification from the card label, the presence of a balance, or a particular charge alone. Its findings remain specific to the case structure and governing documentation examined.
Chapter 88 — Accounts and deposits
This chapter examines bank accounts and deposits through the customer’s claim and the movement of funds. It asks what kind of account is involved, who owes what to whom, how balances are recorded, and whether a deposit functions as a repayable claim, a service relationship, or another arrangement. Similar account names may conceal different terms and operational features.
The accounting description of a balance as a liability can help reconstruct the contemporary structure; it does not establish that “deposit equals debt” as a Qur’anic rule. A real audit needs the account agreement, statements, rate and fee schedules, applicable protections, and the relevant transaction history. The chapter keeps product description separate from semantic definition.
Chapter 89 — Investment accounts and profit-sharing investment accounts
The chapter audits investment accounts, including profit-sharing investment accounts (PSIA), by tracing the pool, mandate, investment activity, return calculation, loss exposure, and any reserves or smoothing mechanisms. It distinguishes an investment account from an ordinary deposit account and asks how the investor’s claim changes with the underlying pool and its performance.
Profit and loss sharing, exposure to loss, and pool governance are application variables, not Qur’anic classifiers. Contemporary accounting and standardization sources may help reconstruct the product framework, but they do not define riba. A specific audit requires the investor agreement, pool mandate, mudaraba or wakala terms, profit formula, loss-allocation rules, reserve arrangements, and reports about actual performance.
Chapter 90 — Rescheduling and restructuring
This chapter examines how a claim changes when repayment is rescheduled or a debt is restructured. It distinguishes the original contract, payment history, outstanding balance, revised schedule, added amounts, fees, and any new agreement. The key question is whether a change in timing also changes the claim and, if so, what ground is given for that change.
The chapter uses historical and contemporary legal or standardization material as comparative sources, not as a direct Qur’anic definition. Hardship and forbearance are treated as an adjacent obligation layer; they do not automatically define riba. Case analysis requires the original contract, full payment history, original and revised schedules, balance calculation, costs, modification agreement, and evidence about the debtor’s circumstances.
Chapter 91 — Late-payment charges and post-default claims
The chapter separates claims arising after a due date: contractual charges, collection costs, compensation, charitable allocations, or other amounts. It asks when each claim arose, who receives it, how it is calculated, and whether it is tied to actual cost, delay, enforcement, or another asserted basis.
Regulatory default, procrastination, and insolvency are distinct categories. Contemporary consumer-protection and standardization sources help describe the relevant arrangements but do not define the Qur’anic term. A real audit requires the contract, due-date history, statements, collection records, cost evidence, and allocation of any late-payment amount. The existence of a late charge does not by itself settle the semantic or case classification.
Chapter 92 — Default, distress, insolvency, and the debtor’s state
This chapter distinguishes default as a regulatory or contractual status from financial distress, insolvency, bankruptcy, and deliberate procrastination. These states may affect how an obligation is enforced or restructured, but they are not synonyms and must not be collapsed into one description of the debtor.
The audit records the debtor’s state as solvent, insolvent, or unknown only where the evidence supports that description. Payment history, income, assets, liabilities, regulatory records, disputes, and restructuring documents may all matter. Debtor hardship is not promoted into a necessary condition or definition of riba. Thresholds and procedures vary and must be checked for the specific jurisdiction and date of a future case.
Chapter 93 — Sukuk, special-purpose vehicles, and multi-contract structures
The chapter closes the product laboratory by examining sukuk as potentially multi-contract structures involving an issuer, investors, an asset or project, a special-purpose vehicle (SPV), servicing arrangements, and claims or undertakings across several documents. The audit asks what asset or right is held, what was transferred, who bears performance and asset risks, how returns arise, and how the structure operates if the project or obligor fails.
The label sukuk does not itself establish ownership, asset transfer, risk allocation, or a Qur’anic result. Accounting and governance sources support reconstruction of contemporary structures; they do not define riba. A real case requires the prospectus, SPV or trust documents, asset-transfer agreements, underlying contracts, servicing agreements, purchase undertakings, guarantees, and relevant event records. The door closes the sequence of product laboratories within the stated scope, not the semantic inquiry.
Part X — The final matrix and independent re-testing (Chapters 94–99)
Chapter 94 — Building the Unified Transaction Variable Matrix
The chapter formalizes UTVM v1.0, the Unified Transaction Variable Matrix. Its purpose is to provide a shared language for describing transactions across their parties, assets, ownership, claims, timing, transfers, outcomes, evidence, and claim status. A variable may appear in more than one type of transaction, but its role and function may differ from case to case.
The matrix is a common audit language, not a common judgment machine. Standardizing the questions helps analysts see where transactions are genuinely similar or different; it cannot create the definition or justify a conclusion before the evidence is complete. The version is an operational framework, not a semantic closure.
Chapter 95 — Asset, ownership, possession, control, and transfer of rights
This chapter separates the asset from ownership, possession, control, benefit, and the claim connected to it. These may yield different answers within one transaction: one party may hold legal title, another may possess or use the asset, and a third may control disposition or hold a related claim.
The matrix therefore asks: What is the asset? Who legally owns it? Who benefits from it? Who possesses it? Who controls its disposition? Who holds the claim attached to it? Transfer of an asset is not identical to transfer of a claim, and a transferred claim does not erase its origin. The chapter supplies a distinct audit matrix for these variables rather than collapsing them into one ownership label.
Chapter 96 — Debt, time, and the additional element
This chapter maps debt, term, payment timing, and any additional amount as separate variables. It asks how the debt arose, when a claim became due, whether time changed the claim, and what ground is offered for an additional element. The matrix prevents “debt + term + increase” from being turned into a rule before the reason for the entitlement and its boundary have been examined.
Amount and timing matter to the case description, but they do not alone establish why a claim is due. The analysis therefore moves from the quantity of debt, time, and Δ toward the entitlement ground and the boundary of the right.
Chapter 97 — Ground of the claim and the bilateral-right boundary
This chapter brings the ground of entitlement together with the boundary of reciprocal rights. The ground asks why the claim arose; the boundary asks how far that claim extends and what corresponding right of the other party constrains it. The boundary may concern amount, duration, a ceiling, or a reciprocal claim, depending on the structure and evidence.
The resulting E*-R2 framework is described as a STRONG AUDIT CANDIDATE / OPEN. Its usefulness as an audit matrix does not prove that it is the final semantic law of riba. A claim cannot be understood from its amount, timing, or name alone; it must be examined through its ground, its limits, and the corresponding right. The candidate remains open until further testing supports more.
Chapter 98 — Outcome, evidence, and claim rank
The chapter distinguishes an outcome from its cause and from the ground of entitlement. A product-level observation, an institutional finding, and a Qur’anic finding belong to different evidentiary layers. A result about profitability, loss, social effect, or a customer’s cash position cannot silently become an explanation of why a claim was due.
The outcome matrix links each result to its evidence, source layer, and claim rank. It asks not only “What did we find?” but also “What kind of finding is it, what evidence supports it, and may it be raised to a stronger level?” Comparative superiority, a beneficial alternative, or an adverse outcome does not by itself prove a prohibited structure or a semantic definition.
Chapter 99 — How can another researcher repeat the test?
The final chapter defines reproducibility as a transparent process another researcher can follow: state the claim and scope; identify the source layer and evidence; apply the declared test; record the result and status; preserve counterevidence; and show how the conclusion follows. The protocol also asks the re-tester to examine structure before restoring institutional or product labels, reducing the risk that a familiar name determines the result.
An independent researcher is not required to reach the same result. A different result is useful if the documents, method, and point of disagreement are recorded. The protocol allows outcomes such as agreement, partial agreement, disagreement, or insufficient evidence; it does not force closure to preserve the original conclusion. Reproducibility strengthens review and correction, not the authority of an untested claim.
The chapter closes the methodological architecture of the book within its declared scope. It does not close the definition of riba. The final result remains NOT SEALED, and readiness to classify remains OPEN / NOT PASSED.
Consolidated status and limits
• Semantic result: the final definition of riba remains NOT SEALED; classification readiness remains OPEN / NOT PASSED.
• Open candidate: E*-R2 remains a STRONG AUDIT CANDIDATE / OPEN, not a sealed definition.
• Operational tools: RIBA-QTAM and UTVM v1.0 organize transaction evidence and variables; neither issues an automatic Qur’anic judgment.
• Application evidence: regulations, standards, product pages, and financial records describe contemporary products and cases. They do not define the Qur’anic term.
• Case scope: actual conclusions require the relevant executed contracts and event-level records. Current rules, rates, fees, and product terms must be rechecked for the jurisdiction and date being studied.
• Re-testing: another researcher may reach a different result; the method requires transparent evidence and reasoning, not forced agreement.
RESISTED applies to the specified claim and test, not to every possible role of a variable. OPEN is not a finding, and CLOSED remains bounded by its declared scope. The matrices improve description and review while preserving the unresolved semantic question.
Closing
Volume Four completes the current sequence of product audits and assembles their variables into a common matrix. Its final safeguard is reproducibility: another researcher should be able to inspect the evidence, repeat the stated test, and record agreement or disagreement without being compelled to reproduce a predetermined verdict. The tools make the inquiry more reviewable; they do not replace the evidence or seal what remains open.
