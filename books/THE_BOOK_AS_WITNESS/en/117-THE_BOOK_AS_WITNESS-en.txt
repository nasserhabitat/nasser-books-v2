II. Translator’s Note: The Bridge of Meaning
This English edition is a condensed conceptual adaptation. It is not a word-for-word translation, but rather an "extraction of essence." It presents the core philosophical framework in accessible English, omitting the exhaustive linguistic debates and classical references found in the original Arabic text.
For the academic researcher: The original Arabic version remains the primary source for comprehensive linguistic analysis, detailed exegesis (Tafsir), and the complete bibliography.
This adaptation also preserves the methodological status of the Arabic edition. A claim marked OPEN remains open; a CANDIDATE remains a candidate; a RESISTED or STRONGLY RESISTED claim is not revived through simplification; and a CLOSED result remains closed only within its declared scope. The aim is clarity without scientific inflation.
Key Qur’anic terms are sometimes retained in transliteration—such as nafs, tawaffī, barzakh, and baʿth—when a single English equivalent would prematurely narrow the field under study.
Book Summary
This book asks a question that its title deliberately does not answer in advance: how does revelation appear within the Qur’anic network of accountability and evidence, especially in eschatological scenes? Does the text permit us to say that the Qur’an, or al-kitāb, functions as a formal judicial witness on the Day of Judgment? Or does the Qur’an present a more complex architecture in which revelation, messengers, witnesses, deeds-records, bodily testimony, and divine judgment remain distinct even when they appear in the same scene?
The phrase “The Book as Witness” is therefore an analytical title, not a claim that the Qur’an literally names itself “the witness,” nor a claim that the revealed Book is identical with the record of deeds. The book begins by establishing an epistemic guard: shared wording does not prove shared referent, co-occurrence does not prove causation, a local relation does not automatically become a root-wide law, and a model does not become a textual fact merely because it explains the data elegantly.
The study then moves through eight chapters. It first establishes the QCROS research path and the Mathānī method as a discovery tool rather than an independent proof. It distinguishes among kitāb, Qurʾān, dhikr, and furqān without forcing either absolute synonymy or absolute separation. It then examines the architecture of waḥy, messenger authority, prophetic clarification, and the question of whether any of these establish a second independent preserved legislative corpus. From there, it turns to preservation, continuity, confirmation, hegemony, religious unity, plurality of shirʿah, the difference between ikhtilāf and tafarruq, the eschatological network of evidence, and finally the analytical fields of functional displacement, authority displacement, laghw, ṭāghūt, and legislation without divine authorization.
Several results are stable within their tested scope. Shared Lexeme ≠ Shared Referent is a governing methodological rule. The identification of the revealed Book with deeds-records is not established. The Qur’an as a formal eschatological court witness is not established. Messenger authority and prophetic clarification are established as functions, but they do not by themselves prove a second independently preserved corpus. Confirmation does not entail full legal identity. The relation of hegemony is textually present, while its complete mechanism remains open. The coexistence of one dīn with plural shirʿah is closed within the tested network. The final definition of minhāj remains open. “Authority displacement” and “functional displacement” remain analytical models rather than Qur’anic technical terms.
The chief value of the study lies not in the number of conclusions it seals, but in the discipline with which it limits claims. OPEN is not FALSE. NOT ESTABLISHED is not NONE. CANDIDATE is not CLOSED. MODEL is not TEXTUAL FACT. The reader is therefore given not only conclusions, but also the means to challenge, narrow, reopen, or continue them.
General Introduction
From a Strong Title to a Testable Question
A title such as “The Book as Witness” can easily become a hidden conclusion. A researcher may enter the text already convinced that al-kitāb in an eschatological scene is the Qur’an, that the Book itself bears courtroom testimony, or that every occurrence of the same lexical form points to the same object. QCROS reverses that movement. The title organizes an inquiry; it does not settle the referent.
The central task is therefore to ask, at every stage: What does the verse explicitly state? What is merely observed? What relation can be induced? Which part is hypothesis? Which part survives resistance testing? What is the current scientific status? This discipline prevents the architecture of a theory from outrunning the architecture of the text.
The Book’s Two Functions
The work has a substantive function and a methodological function. Substantively, it maps revelation across reception, delivery, preservation, reference, confirmation, hegemony, law, accountability, deeds-records, witness-bearing, abandonment, and rival centers of authority. Methodologically, it makes the path from text to claim visible. The reader should be able to identify not only what the book concludes, but why the conclusion has exactly that rank and no higher.
QCROS and the Claim Ceiling
The governing sequence is: TEXT → OBSERVATION → INDUCTION → RELATIONS → HYPOTHESIS → TEST → STATUS → LAW / MODEL. The sequence is not merely a writing convention. It functions as a claim ceiling. A statement cannot become a law simply because it is coherent, traditional, useful, or repeated. It must survive the relevant corpus, reverse testing, resistant passages, and scope control.
The Mathānī method is used within this discipline as a discovery instrument. It can draw attention to pairing, contrast, recurrence, functional complementarity, and transformed repetition. Yet Mathānī Discovery ≠ Proof. A striking binary, symmetry, or structural diagram cannot create a relation that the textual corpus has not established.
The Resistant Passage
A resistant passage is not an inconvenience to be explained away. It is evidence that disciplines the hypothesis. Individual books of deeds resist the claim that every “book” in an eschatological context is the revealed Book. The varied uses of ḥikmah resist its reduction to a uniquely Muhammad-specific second corpus. The context of Qur’an 59:7 resists using a single command formula as an automatic documentary proof. Non-textual uses of furqān resist identifying it exclusively with one physical or textual entity. Resistance therefore refines scope, identity, and function.
Limits Are Part of the Result
This book does not establish that the Qur’an is a formal judicial witness in the complete legal sense. It does not establish that the revealed Book is the deeds-record. It does not finally seal the referent of al-kitāb in Qur’an 39:69. It does not close the final map of kitāb / Qurʾān / dhikr / furqān. It does not prove a second independently preserved legislative corpus merely from messenger authority, bayān, or ḥikmah. Nor does failure to prove such a corpus amount to a denial of every situational instruction given during the Prophet’s lifetime.
These limits are not editorial weakness. They are scientific output. A disciplined theory knows where to stop.
How to Read This Condensed Edition
This edition should not be read only for its final propositions. Each chapter is designed as a traceable chain of claims. A useful reading path is: READ → COLLECT → COMPARE → HYPOTHESIZE → TEST → JUDGE → CONTINUE.
1. Begin with the text, not with the title. Ask what the verse actually names and what it does not name.
2. Distinguish lexical form from referent. Shared Lexeme ≠ Shared Referent.
3. Read status words as part of the claim. OPEN, CANDIDATE, CLOSED, MODEL, and NOT ESTABLISHED are not decorative labels.
4. Do not treat co-occurrence as identity or causation. Two elements may share a scene without sharing a function.
5. Ask where the result was established: one verse, one context, one tested network, or the root as a whole?
6. Look for resistant evidence before accepting a model. The strongest hypothesis is the one that survives relevant resistance without expanding beyond it.
7. Use reverse testing. If A → B is proposed, search for A without B and B without A.
8. Keep function separate from documentary identity. Messenger authority does not, by itself, identify the documentary form that survives after the messenger.
9. Keep representation separate from proof. Tables, arrows, and conceptual maps summarize results; they do not generate them.
10. When historical or institutional applications are discussed, distinguish Qur’anic findings from analytical application.

Chapter One — Epistemological Foundation
Why Method Comes Before the Conclusion
The first chapter refuses to begin by “proving” the book’s title. Instead, it establishes the conditions under which the question can be asked without circularity. The Qur’anic corpus is the governing evidence. Earlier volumes, inherited theology, conceptual elegance, and historical consensus may supply questions or comparative material, but they cannot substitute for the new corpus under examination.
This is expressed in a crucial rule: Previous Volume ≠ Evidence. Earlier work can provide tools, not automatic conclusions. The same rule applies internally: Research File ≠ Book Chapter. A research register may record uncertainty, failed tests, and provisional claims; the literary chapter must preserve those ranks rather than smoothing them into certainty.
Mathānī as Discovery, Not Proof
The Qur’an describes the Book in Qur’an 39:23 using the expression “mutashābihan mathāni,” and speaks in 15:87 of “seven of the mathāni.” These resources justify treating mathāni as a serious Qur’anic clue, but they do not, by themselves, close a universal operational definition. The method therefore uses pairing and structural recurrence heuristically. A discovered relation must still survive context, counter-example, reverse test, and scope control.
Levels Must Not Be Collapsed
The chapter also uses muḥkam / mutashābih as an example of level discipline. A whole-book description and an internal classification of verses need not operate at the same analytical level. SAME ROOT ≠ SAME FUNCTIONAL LEVEL. This prevents lexical similarity from collapsing structural distinctions.
The Accountability Network
Eschatological passages contain multiple evidentiary elements: messengers, witnesses, books, deeds, bodily testimony, and divine judgment. Their coexistence is significant, but it does not erase their identities. Qur’an 16:89 can place a witnessing messenger and a revealed Book in the same verse without proving that the Book itself performs the messenger’s witness-function. Qur’an 25:30 can place the Qur’an inside an eschatological complaint without identifying it with the deeds-record. Qur’an 45:28–29 can describe a book tied to what people did without calling it revealed scripture. Qur’an 39:69 can place “the Book” alongside prophets and witnesses without naming that Book as the Qur’an.
The chapter’s strongest current formulation is therefore: Revelation as a Normative Accountability Frame = CANDIDATE / STRONGLY SUPPORTED NETWORK. Qur’an as Formal Eschatological Court Witness = NOT ESTABLISHED.
Chapter Two — Kitāb, Qurʾān, Dhikr, and Furqān
From Word to Referent
The second chapter dismantles two opposite shortcuts. The first treats kitāb, Qurʾān, dhikr, and furqān as interchangeable names everywhere. The second turns them into absolutely separate ontological objects. The Qur’anic corpus resists both simplifications. The correct question is contextual: what is the referent here, what is the function here, and what relation is actually established?
Kitāb and the Problem of Multiple Referents
The word kitāb appears in materially different accountability settings. Qur’an 17:13–14 speaks of a book an individual encounters and is told to read. Other passages describe a person receiving “his book.” Qur’an 45:28–29 speaks of every community being called to its book and then of “Our book” speaking truthfully about what people used to do. Qur’an 18:49 presents a book that leaves nothing small or great uncounted. These contexts strongly support the existence of deeds-related records.
Accordingly, the project closes the methodological rule that lexical identity does not prove referential identity. MULTIPLE KITĀB REFERENTS IN ESCHATOLOGICAL CONTEXTS = CANDIDATE TO CLOSED. REVEALED BOOK = DEEDS BOOK = NOT ESTABLISHED.
The Qurʾān as Recited and Operative Revelation
The chapter does not offer a root-wide final definition of Qurʾān. It does, however, observe a strong connection with recitation, public proclamation, reception, and operative encounter. Qur’an 75:17–18 relates Qurʾān to the act of recitation; Qur’an 25:30 presents “this Qur’an” as the object of the messenger’s complaint that his people had taken it as abandoned. The working formulation Qurʾān as Recited / Operative Revelation remains a hypothesis, not a sealed definition.
Dhikr
Dhikr is linked with descent and preservation in Qur’an 15:9, with explanatory communication in Qur’an 16:44, and with the Qur’an in other contexts. This supports functions of remembrance, recall, and preservation. Yet Dhikr as Preserved / Recalled Content remains a working hypothesis until a dedicated root-wide corpus is completed.
Furqān
A separate scan of furqān resists reducing it to a synonym for one textual object. Qur’an 8:29 speaks of God granting a furqān, while 8:41 speaks of the Day of Furqān. The strongest current formulation is Furqān as Decisive Distinction / Criterion = CANDIDATE TO CLOSED. Furqān = Legislative Mechanism Only = NOT ESTABLISHED ROOT-WIDE. Furqān as Eschatological Judicial Tool = NOT ESTABLISHED.
The Four-Term Map Remains Open
A useful provisional map says: kitāb as stabilized reference, Qurʾān as recited or operative revelation, dhikr as remembered or preserved content, and furqān as decisive distinction. But the book refuses to promote this map into a final law. BOOK–QURʾĀN–DHIKR–FURQĀN FINAL MAP = OPEN / REQUIRES DEDICATED CORPORA.
Chapter Three — The Architecture of Revelation
Waḥy Is Wider Than Legislation
The Qur’anic field of waḥy is broader than public religious legislation. Revelation is addressed not only to prophets and messengers but also, in different modes, to the mother of Moses, to the bee, to angels, and even appears in descriptions of deceptive whispering among devils. This alone blocks the equation WAḤY = PUBLIC LEGISLATION. Multiple Forms of Waḥy = OBSERVED / STRONGLY SUPPORTED. Multiple Forms of Waḥy → Multiple Preserved Legislative Sources does not follow.
When Does Revelation Become Public Norm?
The chapter therefore turns to delivery, following, and judgment. Qur’an 5:67 links messenger-delivery to what was sent down. Qur’an 7:3 connects normative following to what was sent down from the Lord. Qur’an 5:49 links judgment to what God sent down. Qur’an 6:19 explicitly links the Qur’an with public warning. These form a strong network in which descent, messenger, communication, following, and judgment converge without answering every documentary question.
Messenger Authority
The messenger is not merely a vocal conduit. The Qur’an assigns him functions of delivery, warning, judgment, clarification, and obedience. Messenger Authority = CLOSED as an established function. Yet Obedience to the Messenger ≠ Proof of a Second Preserved Corpus. A human agent’s authority and the documentary structure through which normativity survives are separate questions.
“What the Messenger Gives You”
Qur’an 59:7 occurs within the distribution of fayʾ. QCROS therefore rejects two shortcuts: restricting the verse to money without testing the breadth of its formulation, and extracting it from its context as a self-sufficient proof of a complete second corpus. Messenger Command Authority = ESTABLISHED. Second Independent Revelatory Corpus by 59:7 Alone = NOT ESTABLISHED.
Prophetic Bayān
Qur’an 16:44 establishes a prophetic function of bayān in relation to what was sent down to people. The function is stable; the documentary mechanism is not thereby defined. Bayān may include clarification, teaching, application, judgment, and practical disclosure. Prophetic Bayān = CLOSED AS FUNCTION. Bayān = Second Independent Revelation = NOT ESTABLISHED / OPEN ON MECHANISM.
Ḥikmah and the Second-Corpus Hypothesis
The pairing “Book and wisdom” is an important test case. Yet a full scan shows ḥikmah being given, taught, sent down, associated with earlier prophets, and embedded in Qur’anic instruction. Qur’an 17:39 even describes preceding commands as “from the wisdom” revealed to the Prophet. This weakens the claim that ḥikmah is the exclusive name of a second independent corpus outside the Qur’an. Ḥikmah as Independent Second Preserved Corpus = NOT ESTABLISHED AFTER FULL SCAN.
The Post-Messenger Question
Qur’an 4:59 establishes reference to God and the messenger during the messenger’s historical presence. The mechanism by which “return to the messenger” functions after that historical presence is a separate question. The chapter refuses two automatic answers: that it must mean only the Qur’an, or that it necessarily means a later independent ḥadīth corpus. Post-Messenger Referential Mechanism = OPEN.
Chapter Four — The Continuing Corpus
Agent and Documentary Vessel
The chapter moves from the living messenger to the question of what remains as a stable public reference. Messenger and corpus are not competing concepts: one is an agent within the revelatory mission, the other is a documentary form. The study therefore asks where preservation, continuity, public reach, and normative reference converge.
Kitāb as a Discovery Model of Stabilization
The chapter allows a discovery model in which kitāb is associated with gathering, fixing, recording, and making reference possible. This is not promoted into a final etymological truth. Mathānī Decomposition ≠ Proof. The value of the model lies in what it helps the researcher test, not in its elegance.
Preservation and Its Limits
Qur’an 15:9 strongly supports preservation in relation to dhikr. Yet preservation by itself does not establish every claim about exclusivity, interpretation, or the absence of any other historical instruction. Likewise, completeness statements do not mean that every conceptual detail is instantly available without human study. Preservation secures a textual reality; interpretation remains a disciplined task.
Extended Address
Qur’an 6:19 links the Qur’an with warning “you and whoever it reaches.” This makes public reach beyond the first audience an important part of the continuity question. The chapter treats this as strong evidence for the Qur’an’s trans-generational public function while avoiding claims larger than the wording can carry.
Preservation Is Not Response
A key distinction is that material preservation does not equal functional response. A text can remain present while listening, reflection, following, judgment, or norm-governed action weakens. This distinction becomes essential in the final chapter, where “functional abandonment” and “authority displacement” are tested as analytical models.
Bridge to Earlier Revelation
Once the continuing corpus is identified as a live normative reference, the next question becomes relational: how does the present Book stand toward what was revealed before it? This opens the fields of confirmation and hegemony without assuming either total continuity or total cancellation.
Chapter Five — “What Was Revealed Before You”
Continuity Without Legal Identity
The Qur’an situates the Muhammadic message within a history of revelation, but continuity of source does not require identity of every legal detail. Qur’an 3:50 is especially important: Jesus is presented as confirming the Torah while making lawful some of what had been forbidden. Within this tested network, strict confirmation cannot mean complete legal identity.
The resulting formulation is narrower and stronger: confirmation preserves continuity with prior truth while allowing legal differentiation. This does not close the root-wide definition of ṣ-d-q, but it defeats a specific overgeneralization.
Hegemony
Qur’an 5:48 places confirmation and hegemony together. The conjunction matters: if hegemony were merely another word for confirmation, the double relation would be difficult to explain. The text therefore establishes a governing or supervisory relation of the later Book toward what precedes it. The relation is real; its complete historical and textual mechanism is not closed.
Hegemony = “correction of every historical textual corruption” is therefore NOT ESTABLISHED without a dedicated corpus of distortion, concealment, alteration, forgetting, and scripture. The chapter preserves hegemony as a relational fact without inflating it into a ready-made theory of textual history.
One Source, More Than One Shirʿah
Qur’an 42:13 evokes continuity in dīn across prophetic history, while Qur’an 5:48 speaks of a shirʿah and a minhāj for each community. These passages allow unity and plurality to coexist. The book therefore rejects the assumption that one divine source requires identical legal detail at every historical stage.
Earlier Revelation and Present Obligation
Belief in earlier revelation does not by itself make every earlier ruling automatically binding on the present community. Present legal obligation requires a bridge within the current address: a reaffirmation, command, incorporation, referral, or other normative mechanism. This does not nullify earlier revelation; it distinguishes historical truth from present legal binding force.

Chapter Six — Difference, Dīn, Shirʿah, Sharīʿah, and Minhāj
Ikhtilāf Is Not Tafarruq
Qur’an 11:118 describes human beings as continuing to differ. Qur’an 3:105 warns against people who divided and differed after clear signs came to them. These two uses prevent opposite reductions: not every difference is divinely approved, and not every difference is condemned division. IKHTILĀF ≠ TAFARRUQ = CLOSED as a structural distinction. The moral value of a particular difference remains contextual.
Authorized Diversity Is Not Religious Fragmentation
Qur’an 5:48 presents a shirʿah and a minhāj for different communities within a divine framework of testing, while Qur’an 42:13 prohibits division in dīn. The book therefore distinguishes authorized diversity from blameworthy fragmentation without extending “authorized diversity” to every historical dispute.
Dīn and Millah
The attractive formula “dīn is divine, millah is human” does not survive the corpus. Dīn can be attributed to communities other than true believers, while millah appears in patterns of following and attribution that include Abraham and ancestral communities. The combination of dīn and millat Ibrāhīm in Qur’an 4:125 supports non-synonymy but does not provide final exhaustive definitions. Dīn ≠ Millah as Total Synonyms = CANDIDATE TO CLOSED. Final functional definitions of both remain OPEN.
One Dīn, Plural Shirʿah
The tested network permits a stable conclusion: UNITARY DĪN + PLURAL SHIRʿAH = CLOSED within the tested network. This does not imply a complete hierarchy of all legal vocabulary. The description of shirʿah as an “implementation layer” remains MODEL / ANALOGY.
Sharīʿah and Amr
Qur’an 45:18 establishes a relation between sharīʿah and amr and commands following that sharīʿah. But the preposition min does not automatically prove a part-whole ontology in which sharīʿah is literally a subcomponent of amr. Sharīʿah ↔ Amr Relation = ESTABLISHED. Sharīʿah = Part of Amr = NOT ESTABLISHED / OPEN.
Ḥukm and Minhāj
The root ḥ-k-m is broader than “executing sharīʿah”: it includes judging, arbitration, wisdom, and related functions. In dispute contexts, adjudication is closed locally; Ḥukm = Sharīʿah Execution as a root-wide definition is NOT ESTABLISHED. Minhāj occurs lexically in a highly limited corpus, so its final definition remains OPEN. The discipline of leaving a term open is itself a result.

Chapter Seven — The Architecture of Witnessing
The Afterlife Is Not Simply a Human Court Enlarged
Eschatological discourse contains judgment, records, testimony, messengers, witnesses, bodies, and divine knowledge. It is tempting to translate this whole network into a familiar courtroom model. The chapter resists that simplification. A courtroom analogy may be useful, but the Qur’anic network must first be allowed to preserve its own distinctions.
The Performance Record
Several passages establish records tied to human action: individual books that are read, community books related to deeds, and a record that leaves nothing uncounted. These resources support a deeds-record family with evidentiary functions. They do not identify that family with revealed scripture.
When the Human Being Loses Narrative Monopoly
The Qur’an also depicts bodily testimony and the disclosure of what persons did. The human subject is no longer the sole narrator of his or her own history. This evidentiary plurality is important: accountability is not built on one documentary element alone.
Sh-h-d Is Wider Than Courtroom Witnessing
The root sh-h-d has a broader Qur’anic field than formal judicial testimony. Therefore, the presence of shahīd / shuhadāʾ in an eschatological scene cannot by itself impose a modern courtroom ontology on every other element in that scene.
Qur’an 39:69
Qur’an 39:69 presents “the Book” together with prophets, witnesses, and judgment in truth. Yet the verse does not name that Book as the Qur’an. The deeds-record referent is strongly plausible in the wider context, but the project preserves its status: al-kitāb in 39:69 as deeds-record referent = CANDIDATE TO CLOSED, not an explicit textual fact.
Where Does Revealed Scripture Enter?
The revealed Book enters the accountability architecture through normative and missionary relations: what was delivered, what was followed or abandoned, how the messenger was answered, and how the Qur’an could become the object of an eschatological complaint. This strongly supports revelation as part of the accountability frame without making it identical to the record of deeds or a formal court witness.
The Messenger’s Complaint
Qur’an 25:30 is decisive for one limited point: “this Qur’an” is explicitly the object of the messenger’s complaint that his people took it as abandoned. The verse gives the Qur’an an eschatological presence within the accountability network. It does not call the Qur’an the deeds-record, nor does it by itself define the full root of h-j-r.
Calibration as a Model
The chapter allows an analytical calibration model: revelation functions normatively; deeds-records preserve performance; judgment relates deed to norm. This can explain why revelation matters to accountability without collapsing the documents into one identity. The calibration model remains MODEL / ANALOGY, not a Qur’anic description of a mechanical judicial procedure.

Chapter Eight — The Architecture of Authority Displacement
From Abandonment to Functional Displacement
The final chapter asks whether a text can remain materially present while its governing function is weakened. Qur’an 25:30 establishes abandonment of “this Qur’an” in the messenger’s complaint, but it does not close a complete root-wide theory of h-j-r. The project therefore uses Functional Displacement as an analytical term for patterns in which hearing, reflection, following, or judgment is disabled while the text may remain publicly present.
Response Network
The Qur’an presents listening, attentive silence, reflection, following, and judgment as distinct response functions. Their disruption need not be identical. A community might preserve recitation while weakening reflection; it might preserve symbolic reverence while shifting effective authority elsewhere. These are hypotheses to be tested, not automatic judgments on historical communities.
Laghw and the Disruption of Reception
Qur’an 41:26 presents an unusually clear local case: opponents urge one another not to listen to this Qur’an and to engage in laghw concerning it in order to prevail. Within this context, laghw functions as disruption of reception. Laghw as a root-wide final definition remains open. The historical analogy of “epistemic noise” is a model, not the lexical definition.
Ṭāghūt: From Transgression to Rival Alignment
The chapter distinguishes the root-level motion of transgression from the noun ṭāghūt as it functions in networks of judgment, allegiance, rejection, and path. It rejects the reduction of ṭāghūt to “legislation” alone. A stronger candidate model is a rival center of allegiance or reference. Yet the root-wide final ontology of ṭāghūt remains subject to dedicated corpus testing.
Judgment and Path
Qur’an 4:60 presents tension around seeking judgment from ṭāghūt despite a command to reject it. Qur’an 4:76 extends the contrast into the language of path: the way of God versus the way of ṭāghūt. The chapter therefore tests whether displacement can move from a judicial point of reference to a broader alignment structure. It does not claim that every institutional authority is thereby ṭāghūt.
Legislation Without Authorization
Qur’an 42:21 criticizes partners who prescribe as dīn what God has not authorized. This supports a strong distinction between divine normativity and unauthorized religious obligation. Yet Qur’an 4:59 functions as a resistant resource against demonizing human authority as such: obedience and ordered authority appear within a divinely framed structure. The analytical target is unauthorized rival normativity, not the mere existence of institutions.
The “Parallel Religion” Model
“Parallel religion” is an internal analytical model describing a system in which binding religious authority is generated, stabilized, or obeyed outside the authorization structure established by the Qur’anic corpus. It is not a Qur’anic term and must not be used as a shortcut for condemning a school, community, or historical period without separate evidence.
What Remains Open
The chapter closes several local relations while leaving root-wide definitions and historical applications open. Functional displacement, authority displacement, and parallel religion are useful models; they are not substitutes for lexical corpus work. The final lesson is therefore methodological: displacement cannot be defined from one word, one verse, or one historical analogy.

Appendix II — Open Research Agenda
• Complete dedicated corpora for Qurʾān and dhikr before attempting a final four-term map with kitāb and furqān.
• Complete a dedicated root corpus for h-j-r to determine the range and limits of “functional abandonment.”
• Complete a dedicated ṭ-gh-y / ṭāghūt corpus to separate lexical motion, named referents, and analytical models of rival allegiance.
• Complete a full ḥ-k-m corpus before proposing any root-wide definition centered on legal execution.
• Investigate the post-messenger referential mechanism without importing either exclusivist answer in advance.
• Test the referent of al-kitāb in Qur’an 39:69 against the entire eschatological book/record network.
• Distinguish historical application from Qur’anic closure whenever authority-displacement models are applied to institutions or traditions.

Closing Note
This condensed edition does not claim to replace the Arabic original. Its purpose is to give the international reader access to the architecture of the inquiry: the questions, distinctions, tests, scientific statuses, and open research paths. Where the Arabic edition leaves a matter open, this edition leaves it open. Where a model remains analytical, it remains analytical. Where the corpus resists a claim, condensation does not erase the resistance.
The governing principle remains simple: text before model, evidence before representation, context before projection, and limits as part of the result.
