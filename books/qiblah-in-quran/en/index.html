II. Translator’s Note: The Bridge of Meaning

This English edition is a condensed conceptual adaptation. It is not a word-for-word translation, but rather an "extraction of essence." It presents the core philosophical framework in accessible English, omitting the exhaustive linguistic debates and classical references found in the original Arabic text.

For the academic researcher: The original Arabic version remains the primary source for comprehensive linguistic analysis, detailed exegesis (Tafsir), and the complete bibliography.

How to Read This Edition

This edition is designed for a reader who may not know Arabic and may not be familiar with the classical vocabulary of Qur’anic studies. It therefore preserves the architecture of the argument rather than the full volume of linguistic documentation. Qur’anic terms are introduced in transliteration, followed by the Arabic term and a short contextual explanation when needed. Technical statuses such as OPEN, CLOSED, CANDIDATE, and NOT ESTABLISHED are kept because they are part of the book’s method, not decorative labels.

The central discipline of the original work is also preserved: evidence is not the same as interpretation; interpretation is not the same as hypothesis; and a hypothesis is not a law simply because it is attractive or comprehensive. This condensed edition therefore aims to remain intellectually open while still giving the foreign reader a coherent view of the book’s main discovery.

Introduction — Why Reopen the Question of Qiblah?

Qiblah (القبلة) is usually presented as one of the clearest concepts in Islamic religious consciousness: the direction toward which Muslims turn in prayer. This is true as far as it goes. The Qur’an itself repeatedly commands the turning of the face toward al-Masjid al-Haram (المسجد الحرام). Yet the book asks whether this spatial description exhausts the Qur’anic function of Qiblah.

The question arises because the Qur’anic passages do more than identify a direction. In the surrounding textual network, Qiblah appears beside the face, turning, transformation, al-Masjid al-Haram, the house, east and west, righteousness, guidance, following, trial, the middle community, witnessing, and prayer. The existence of such a network does not prove that all these ideas belong to the definition of Qiblah. But it does require the researcher to explain why they appear where they do and what kind of relationship each one has to the concept.

The book therefore changes the order of inquiry. It does not begin with a definition and then collect confirming verses. It begins with the textual corpus, records observations, maps relationships, formulates several competing hypotheses, and then subjects those hypotheses to falsification and resistant cases. The guiding question is not merely, “Where does one turn?” but, “How does orientation function in the Qur’anic text?”

This approach rejects two symmetrical reductions. The first is material reduction: treating spatial direction as the whole concept. The second is abstract reduction: dissolving the concrete direction into a purely symbolic, spiritual, or civilizational metaphor. The working principle is that a Qur’anic concept may have a real embodied form and, at the same time, perform functions that cannot be reduced to that form alone.

The problem is not materiality. The problem begins when the part is made equal to the whole, or when the embodiment is mistaken for the entire concept.

The project calls its wider research horizon the “architecture of orientation.” This is not claimed to be a Qur’anic expression. It is an analytical model whose legitimacy depends on testing. If the relationships are stable and explanatory, the model may be useful. If they are not, it must remain only a hypothesis.
