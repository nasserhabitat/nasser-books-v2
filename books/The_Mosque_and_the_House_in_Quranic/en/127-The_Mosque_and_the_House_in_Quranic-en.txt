II. Translator’s Note: The Bridge of Meaning
This English edition is a condensed conceptual adaptation. It is not a word-for-word translation, but an extraction of the book’s central argument. It presents the main framework in accessible English and omits much of the Arabic edition’s detailed linguistic discussion and source-by-source documentation.
For academic research, the Arabic manuscript remains the primary source for the full linguistic analysis, detailed discussion, and complete bibliography.
This adaptation preserves the methodological status assigned to claims in the Arabic edition. A claim marked OPEN remains open. A CANDIDATE is a provisional result. A RESISTED or STRONGLY RESISTED claim is not revived by simplification. A CLOSED result is closed only within its declared scope. The aim is clarity without inflating the evidence.
Key Qur’anic terms are sometimes retained in transliteration—such as nafs, tawaffī, barzakh, and baʿth—when a single English equivalent would narrow the question prematurely. Qur’anic references are given by surah and verse. English renderings of Qur’anic wording in this edition are working glosses; they do not replace the Arabic text.
How to read this book
The study distinguishes five levels:
● Text: the Qur’anic wording, its form, and its location.
● Explanation: a linguistic, grammatical, or historical account that should identify its basis.
● Inference: a conclusion drawn from a set of verses, their contexts, and their relations.
● Hypothesis: a possible extension that needs further testing.
● Model: a practical framework for examining a hypothesis; it does not claim to be the verse’s exclusive meaning.
The tags used below mark these levels: [T] text, [E] explanation, [I] inference, [H] hypothesis, and [M] model. A symbolic reading is not presented as a Qur’anic definition merely because it is suggestive or spiritually useful.
The book uses QCROS as a governance protocol for knowledge claims: Qur’anic corpus, context, relations, opposing evidence, and status. The method begins with a claim, gathers the relevant wording, reads its setting, compares related uses, tests contrary evidence, and records what the evidence can currently support.
1. The question and the scope
This study examines the Qur’anic network of masjid/masājid and bayt/buyūt—mosque and mosques, house and houses—and their connections with prostration, direction, remembrance, retreat, pilgrimage, construction, purification, security, dwelling, and entry.
The central question is whether this network allows us to read a place both as a location and through the function the Qur’anic context assigns to it, and whether that relationship can open a disciplined reflection on human awareness. The answer must preserve both sides of the question. The Qur’an addresses people with guidance; its named places are not abstract coordinates without purpose. Yet it does name places and does not thereby turn every place into a symbol of the mind.
The book therefore rejects the unqualified statement, “The Qur’an does not specify geographical places; it specifies functions.” The Qur’an names Bakkah, the Sacred House, the valley without cultivation, ʿArafāt, and the Sacred Monument, among other places. In the same passages, these places are joined to worship, security, provision, remembrance, or pilgrimage. The more accurate thesis is this: the Qur’anic address guides the human being, while place in the passages studied has a presence and a context-defined function. The relationship may invite reflection on the human interior, but such reflection must not erase the place.
For example, the House is described as a place of return and security and is associated with those who circumambulate, devote themselves, bow, and prostrate (2:125). Abraham’s prayer recalls a valley without cultivation beside the Sacred House and asks for prayer, the inclination of people’s hearts, and provision (14:37). Place and function occur together; neither has to replace the other.
The study does not attempt a history of mosque architecture, settle detailed legal rulings, or present an empirical theory of consciousness. It asks what the Qur’anic wording and its network support, then marks the limits of further interpretation.
2. Reading in layers and the Mithani method
The Qur’an speaks of clear foundational verses and others described as mutashābih (3:7), and it describes the Book as mutashābih mathānī (39:23). This study takes such passages as a warrant for reading verses in relation to one another. It does not infer that every verse contains a fixed number of hidden meanings or that every inward reading is valid.
The author’s Mithani system is used as a method for exploring paired, structural, linguistic, and—in some of the author’s work—numerical relations. A proposed pairing is a starting question. It must be tested: Does it respect the word’s forms? Does it recur across relevant contexts? Does it explain contrary examples? Can another reader reproduce the reasoning? A pattern that cannot pass those tests remains a proposal.
The principle of functional non-synonymy is central here. Two words may occur together without becoming interchangeable. Bayt and masjid can be linked in a passage while retaining different roles. The noun masjid is related morphologically to the root of sujūd (prostration), but the range of the verb does not automatically transfer to every use of the noun.
A provisional pairing: house and mosque
The Qur’an associates the House with return, security, purification, circumambulation, and the raising of foundations (2:125–127; 3:96–97; 22:26–29). It places mosques in contexts of direction, retreat during fasting, remembrance, construction, and the contrast between piety and harm (2:144–150, 187; 9:17–18, 107–110). These passages allow a provisional structural question: might the House be considered in relation to return and shelter, and the mosque in relation to orientation and worship?
This is an [M] exploratory model, not a Qur’anic binary. It does not establish that the House always means stability or that the mosque always means mental movement. A second proposed pairing—purifying the House and building or maintaining mosques—can invite reflection on preparation and cultivation. The text does not, by itself, define purification as removing psychological idols or mosque construction as building an intellectual system. Those are further interpretations [H].
The reverse test is decisive: can this pairing respect the House of the Spider (29:41), the House frequented (52:4), and homes used for dwelling, as well as the different Qur’anic mosque contexts? If it must force all of them into one inward meaning, it fails and must be narrowed.
3. Mosque, prostration, and miḥrāb
Prostration does not define every use of “mosque”
The Qur’an describes prostration in varied settings: the angels prostrate to Adam (2:34); Joseph’s family prostrates before him in the dream’s fulfillment (12:100); celestial bodies appear in Joseph’s dream (12:4); and creatures in the heavens and earth, including the stars and trees, prostrate (16:49; 22:18; 55:6). It also commands prostration in worship (22:77; 96:19). The contexts do not reduce to one bodily posture, but neither do they all become metaphors for inward submission. Each use must be read in its own setting.
The range of sujūd therefore does not prove that a Qur’anic mosque is any site where people make objects “submit” to natural laws. Research centers, factories, universities, and hospitals may serve human flourishing and civilization. Calling them “mosques” can work as a contemporary metaphor; the evidence reviewed here does not establish that the Qur’an uses masājid to name such institutions.
The singular and plural forms likewise mark number or category according to context. The plural masājid does not by itself extend the word to every institution with a beneficial function. Nor does the singular masjid automatically encode a unique project or symbolic center.
The mosque in its Qur’anic contexts
The command to face the Sacred Mosque in 2:144, 149, and 150 establishes a spatial direction. Reflection on orientation and obedience may follow, but it cannot cancel the spatial reference. Other passages connect the Sacred Mosque with pilgrimage, obstruction, security, treaty, and conflict. Those contexts should not be flattened into one generic “spiritual center.”
Verses 9:17–18 relate the maintenance of God’s mosques to faith in God and the Last Day, prayer, almsgiving, and reverence. Verse 9:19 places watering pilgrims and maintaining the Sacred Mosque in a comparison in which these acts alone do not equal faith and righteous action. The passages resist reducing maintenance to stonework or to a spiritual slogan. They do not define mosque maintenance as scientific research or industrial production.
Verses 9:107–110 describe a mosque established for harm, disbelief, division, and surveillance, and contrast it with a foundation established on reverence. The name of a place does not settle the moral status of its purpose. A modern institution may be examined by analogy for its aims and consequences, but the verses do not directly name a university, laboratory, or factory as a mosque.
In 7:31, the address “Children of Adam” is followed by a command to take adornment at every mosque, to eat and drink, and not to be excessive. The broad address alone does not prove that the verse assigns the word “mosque” to every religious tradition’s worship site. The mention of eating and drinking also does not prove that eating was a regular practice inside a prayer building. The verse cannot, by itself, be used either to deny a spatial sense or to redefine the mosque as a general-purpose social institution.
In 2:187, the command concerning those who are ʿākifūn (devoted in retreat) in mosques appears within the setting of fasting, nights, marital intimacy, and the completion of the fast. The immediate wording supports a devotional practice associated with mosques. Applying the value of focused devotion to doctors in hospitals or researchers in laboratories may be a useful contemporary analogy [H], but it is not the direct meaning of the verse. The same caution applies to reading “night” as concealment or as a reference to ambiguous verses.
In 17:1, the Sacred Mosque and the Farthest Mosque are named in the account of the Night Journey, with the area around the latter described as blessed. The passage names places. A symbolic extension to the farthest reaches of awareness must remain additional to, and cannot replace, that reading.
The Qur’an does not provide a full architectural definition of a mosque—its walls, roof, minaret, or prayer niche. Many of its uses nevertheless concern a place associated with worship, pilgrimage, direction, remembrance, retreat, or communal maintenance. The balanced conclusion is limited: the Qur’an names places and assigns them functions in context; it does not give a complete architectural specification, and the evidence here does not support calling every useful institution a mosque in Qur’anic usage.
The miḥrāb is not a fixed inward symbol
The Qur’anic forms miḥrāb and maḥārīb occur in accounts involving Zachariah, Mary, David, and Solomon (3:37, 39; 19:11; 38:21; 34:13). In 3:37, Zachariah enters upon Mary in the miḥrāb, where he finds provision; the next verse describes him standing in prayer there. In 19:11, he comes out from the miḥrāb to his people. In 38:21, disputants come over the wall to David in the miḥrāb. In 34:13, maḥārīb are among the works made for Solomon.
These different contexts establish a designated space or spaces, but not a single psychological meaning or one fixed architectural form. Linguistic sources record multiple senses for miḥrāb; the root alone cannot prove that it means “inner struggle,” “self-confrontation,” or a laboratory for reviewing one’s beliefs. Such an extension remains a reflective model [H/M]. The distinction among bayt, masjid, miḥrāb, dār (domain or abode), and sakan (dwelling or repose) must be tested in context rather than turned into a universal symbolic chart.
Script, number, and symbolic claims
The singular and plural forms should be examined in a specified Qur’anic text, distinguishing the written consonantal form from later marks and digital encoding. A script shape can establish how a word appears in the chosen edition. It does not, on its own, prove that the plural encodes unity of reference or that a singular form encodes a distinct spiritual project. Such claims require a declared method and independent contextual evidence.
The definite article in al-bayt does not make every occurrence refer to one physical center. The Qur’an mentions the House frequented in 52:4 and the House of the Spider in 29:41, as well as the Sacred House in pilgrimage contexts. Reference is determined by the description and context, not by the article alone. A single “House of Truth” as a conceptual center may be an authorial model [M], not a grammatical result.
4. The house and houses: dwelling, center, and parable
The Qur’anic corpus includes homes for dwelling and family life, the Sacred House, a house whose foundations Abraham raises, the House of the Spider, the House frequented, and houses appearing in passages about entry, privacy, and habitation. One definition cannot cover all of them. The word’s connection with what “abides overnight” may inspire reflection on settled convictions, but this is a wordplay or conceptual analogy, not a demonstrated lexical law that bayt means the contents of consciousness.
The Sacred House
In 2:125–127, the House is associated with return and security, the station of Abraham, purification, circumambulation, devotion, bowing, prostration, and the raising of its foundations. In 3:96–97, the first House established for people is located at Bakkah and described as blessed and guidance, with clear signs and the station of Abraham; pilgrimage is due to those able to make the journey. In 22:26–29, the House is connected with purification, circumambulation, standing, bowing, prostration, and fulfillment of vows.
These passages establish [T] a spatial center with devotional and guiding functions. They support the inference [I] that place in Qur’anic discourse is linked to action and purpose. The further claim that the House is a “center of knowledge” or that it represents the believer’s heart is an interpretive extension [H]. It should be developed without displacing the place named in the verses.
Homes, entry, and privacy
The Qur’an gives instructions about entering homes and seeking permission (24:27–29; 24:58–59). In 2:189, it rejects entering houses from their backs and says that righteousness lies in reverence, then instructs people to enter houses by their doors. The immediate context concerns a practice connected with pilgrimage and a correction of a mistaken route to the house. The verse does not directly define houses as verses, their backs as surface meanings, or their doors as scholarly sources.
It is possible to draw a methodological analogy: knowledge should be approached through its proper entry points and sound procedures [H/M]. That analogy may be useful, but it must be identified as an application. It cannot replace the contextual reading of houses and doors in the verse.
The Spider’s House and the House frequented
In 29:41, those who take protectors besides God are likened to the spider that takes a house, and the spider’s house is described as the frailest of houses. This is a Qur’anic parable about the weakness of the reliance in view. Applying it to a fragile intellectual refuge may prompt self-examination [H], but labeling a particular inherited belief as a “spider’s house” requires evidence; it cannot be used as an insult or shortcut.
In 52:4, the Qur’an swears by the House frequented. The passage places it within a sequence of solemn references, including the raised roof and the surging sea (52:1–6). The Arabic text names the House frequented; this study does not settle its unseen referent through a symbolic reconstruction of the sequence. The idea that it represents the most populated or most fully inhabited center of the cosmos remains an interpretation whose evidence must be stated.
Houses in which God’s name is remembered
In 24:36, houses are described as places God has permitted to be raised and in which His name is remembered. The verses that follow speak of people whom trade does not distract from remembrance, prayer, and giving (24:37). These verses support a relationship between places of remembrance and the conduct of those who frequent them. They do not directly say that the houses are the hearts of believers. “Houses of the heart” may serve as a spiritual metaphor [H], provided it does not erase the houses named by the passage.
5. The inward house, the mosque of orientation, and inner circumambulation
The author’s inward vocabulary asks what settles in a person’s awareness and what directs that person’s submission. “House of awareness” names, as a model, the convictions and reference points to which a person repeatedly returns. “Mosque of orientation” names, as a metaphor, the direction of a person’s attention and deepest allegiance. Neither phrase is a Qur’anic name for the heart or for a modern institution.
These models become useful only when they lead to examination. What is the source of a conviction? Does it accept correction when evidence changes? What authority governs the person’s choices? Does that orientation lead to remembrance, justice, and responsibility, or to the protection of a human idea from all challenge? The model is not validated by the metaphor; it must be tested against the text, conduct, and contrary evidence.
The circumambulation around the House and the inward analogy
The Qur’an explicitly connects circumambulation with the House. In 2:125 and 22:26, the House is to be purified for those who circumambulate, devote themselves, bow, and prostrate. In 22:29, the command is to circumambulate the Ancient House. These passages establish ritual circumambulation in its spatial and devotional setting. In 2:158, circumambulation concerns al-Ṣafā and al-Marwah; this difference in object is a reminder to read each occurrence by its construction and context. The verb’s root alone cannot make every occurrence a single practice or metaphor.
The author proposes “inner circumambulation” as a contemplative procedure: return repeatedly to a center of guidance and moral measure, inspect one’s convictions from more than one side, and correct them when the evidence requires it. The image is inspired by movement toward a center and return. The Qur’an does not define circumambulation as inward research, call the heart a house in these passages, or reduce the rite to a symbol. The inward extension is an [H/M] proposal; it does not replace the rite or produce a legal ruling.
The model must pass a reverse test. What is the center? What distinguishes honest review from repetitive thinking? What would cause the reader to revise a conclusion? If the “center” is a human idea that cannot be questioned, the model has become a way to protect a belief, not a way to test it. The passages in 55:43–44, where wrongdoers move between Hell and scalding water, also warn against assuming that every form from the root ṭ-w-f means ritual movement around a center. The contexts differ.
Numerical claims about seven circuits, completeness, energy centers, or the reprogramming of consciousness are not established by the cited verses of circumambulation. They require their own stated evidence and method. In this study, the ritual act is text; “inner circumambulation” remains an announced analogy.
6. The witnessed cosmos and the human field of awareness
The Qur’an directs attention to signs in the horizons and within people (41:53; 51:20–21). The author’s distinction between the witnessed cosmos and the human cosmos is an analytical way to consider these two fields. It does not claim that there are two separate material universes. The stars remain physical signs used for wayfinding in darkness (6:97; 16:16); they should not be equated with verses of guidance. The analogy is functional: as stars guide a traveler in a setting described by the Qur’an, divine signs may guide human understanding through uncertainty [H].
Heaven, earth, and what lies between them
The Qur’an describes the earth as a resting place, a spread, or a settled ground and the heaven as a structure or protected roof (2:22; 21:32; 78:6–7). It also repeatedly speaks of the heavens and earth and what is between them in contexts of creation and divine lordship. These are descriptions of the created order [T]. They are not displaced by an inward interpretation.
The author’s model proposes that “heaven” may invite reflection on elevation, command, or a source of guidance; “earth” on stability, nurture, or the ground of action; and “what lies between them” on the field of choice and transformation. These are structural analogies [H/M], not dictionary definitions. The verses do not equate heaven with higher consciousness, earth with psychological stability, or the space between them with an inner zone of transformation.
The methodological balance is therefore: respect the outward account and contemplate the inward application. The earth’s being made traversable and the instruction to walk through it seeking provision (67:15) preserve a material and practical dimension. The address also places human activity within a wider horizon of gratitude and return. Neither dimension needs to erase the other.
The malakūt and the “unfolded book”
In 6:75, God shows Abraham the malakūt of the heavens and earth so that he may be among those of certainty. The surrounding passage presents Abraham’s reasoning with his people and his reflection on celestial signs (6:74–79). The context supports a connection between contemplating signs and certainty [I]. Defining malakūt as a hidden layer of causes and scientific laws is a further proposal [H]; the verse does not provide that technical definition.
The author also proposes a relation between the written Book and the “unfolded book” of creation. The Qur’an swears by a written Book on an unrolled parchment (52:2–3), and elsewhere speaks of signs in the horizons and in human beings. “The universe as an unfolded book” is a reading model [M], not a Qur’anic phrase. The created world may be observed for signs and patterns, but a scientific theory cannot be attributed to the Qur’an without textual and independent evidential support.
The human being as a small cosmos
The author’s image of the human being as a “small cosmos” gathers several analogies: the person’s falak (orbit) is what turns in thought; the stars of guidance are divine signs; and inner night and day describe concealment and disclosure in understanding. These images may help a reader examine how ideas circulate and how guidance becomes visible [H/M]. The Qur’an does not literally name consciousness a falak, nor does it equate its signs with stars. The metaphor should not be converted into a claim about astronomy, neuroscience, or a hidden physical structure.
The same care applies to the author’s wider critique of contemporary cosmology in some source material. This condensed book adopts the question of how reflection on creation relates to faith and accountability. Broad claims that modern space science is fabricated or coordinated deception are empirical and political claims; the Qur’anic passages cited here do not establish them. They require independent evidence and are not adopted as findings in this book.
7. Inherited belief, discernment, and purification
The Qur’an records people appealing to what they found their ancestors practicing (2:170; 5:104; 31:21; 43:22–24). The criticism is not that every inherited belief is false. The problem is treating inheritance alone as proof and refusing examination when a claim is challenged by evidence.
“Purifying the house of awareness” is therefore proposed as a practice of review: identify where a conviction came from, distinguish direct evidence from repeated transmission, and revise what fails examination. It is not a license to call everything inherited misguided. Inherited knowledge may include truth, and rejecting tradition is not automatically a virtue.
The Qur’an describes the heart as a site associated with understanding, reflection, disease, and moral condition (22:46; 47:24). It speaks of the “sound heart” in 26:88–89. These passages can inform an ethical standard for inquiry: willingness to hear truth, fairness toward a dissenter, and scrutiny of one’s own motives. They do not explicitly identify the heart as a house, and “sound heart” does not mean a heart emptied of all inherited knowledge.
The Qur’anic descriptions of coverings, rust, and sealing must also remain distinct. “Coverings” occur in contexts of turning away from signs and listening (6:25; 17:46); “rust” is associated with what people have earned (83:14); and “sealing” appears in 2:7 in the description of those who reject faith. These passages allow comparison among forms of resistance or closure [I]. They do not establish one inevitable chronology in which every inherited belief produces a covering, then rust, then a seal. Nor do they authorize diagnosing an individual or group with one of these conditions without evidence.
The “earthquake of awareness” is a contemporary metaphor for the disruption of settled assumptions. It may be connected as a reflection to the command to purify the House (2:125), but that verse does not say the House is the intellect or that purification means removing conceptual idols. This transfer remains [H]. The decisive test is reflexive: does the researcher apply the same evidential standard to a cherished interpretation that is applied to inherited views or opposing readings?
8. A cognitive and civilizational model
The study can be summarized in three levels:
1. Place and the witnessed world. Houses, mosques, and cosmic signs have a presence in the Qur’anic text.
2. Contextual function. The surrounding passage relates them to security, direction, remembrance, retreat, worship, construction, harm, or another purpose.
3. Human reflection. The reader may ask what these functions reveal about convictions, allegiance, and conduct. This third level is a contemplative extension and must be labelled as such.
One level does not prove every step in the next. The model helps the reader ask questions; it does not supply the answers in advance.
Universities, laboratories, hospitals, and factories can serve knowledge, health, and human flourishing. An author may call them “mosques of knowledge” metaphorically. Three different claims must not be confused: a comparison (“like a mosque in this function”), an openly literary metaphor (“a mosque of knowledge”), and an exegetical claim (“the Qur’anic word masājid means universities”). The first two are contemporary uses; the third requires stronger Qur’anic evidence than is available in this study. A useful institution may also serve injustice or harm; its function and consequences require evaluation.
Questions for the reader
Before adopting an inward interpretation, ask:
● What exactly is the claim, and where did it come from?
● Is it Qur’anic wording, explanation, inference, hypothesis, or model?
● What is the immediate context, and which other passages share a relevant function?
● What is the strongest supporting evidence and the strongest opposing evidence?
● Has a root been used to assign a meaning the actual noun does not carry in context?
● Does the interpretation preserve the place or narrative in the verse, or erase it?
● What evidence could make me change my mind?
● Do I apply this standard to my own view as well as to an opponent’s?
These questions operationalize QCROS. They are a research tool, not a list quoted from the Qur’an.
9. Findings, status, and questions still open
Findings supported within this study
1. The Qur’anic noun masjid occurs in singular and plural forms across contexts involving direction, the Sacred Mosque, pilgrimage, remembrance, retreat, maintenance, harm, and reverence. A complete numerical inventory still requires final corpus verification. [T/CANDIDATE]
2. The Qur’an uses the root of prostration for different agents and settings; the uses should not be collapsed into one bodily or purely metaphorical sense. [T/CLOSED within the examined contexts]
3. The Qur’anic references to houses include different spatial, social, devotional, and parabolic uses. A single inward definition does not cover them. [T/I/CANDIDATE pending a complete form-by-form inventory]
4. The Sacred House is linked to purification, circumambulation, standing, bowing, prostration, and the raising of its foundations (2:125–127; 22:26–29). [T/CLOSED within these cited passages]
5. The contrast in 9:107–110 makes the purpose and moral direction of a mosque relevant to its evaluation. [T/I/CLOSED within the passage]
6. The passages studied present places with functions. This supports a contextual, functional reading of place; it does not support denying geography or reducing places to mental symbols. [I/CLOSED within this study’s scope]
Useful hypotheses that remain open
● The house as an image for convictions in which awareness takes refuge. [H/OPEN]
● The mosque as an image for the orientation and allegiance of awareness. [H/OPEN]
● The heart as a “house of light.” [H/OPEN; not an explicit equation in the cited verses]
● A university or laboratory as analogous to a mosque in a knowledge-related function. [H/M/OPEN; not an established Qur’anic name]
● Entering houses by their doors as an analogy for approaching knowledge by sound methods. [H/M/OPEN; the contextual meaning remains about houses and entry]
● The House frequented as part of a cosmic order or hierarchy. [H/OPEN]
● The witnessed cosmos and human experience as complementary fields for reflection. [I/M/OPEN as a terminology choice]
● Heaven, earth, and what is between them as analogies for elevation, stability, and action. [H/OPEN]
● Malakūt as a way of speaking about insight into governing patterns, and creation as an “unfolded book.” [H/M/OPEN]
● The human being as a small cosmos whose “orbit” is thought. [H/OPEN]
● Inner circumambulation as a method of returning convictions to guidance and moral measure. [H/M/OPEN]
Claims resisted in their present form
● “Every prostration is non-bodily submission.” The contexts vary.
● “In the Qur’an, mosques mean laboratories, factories, and universities.” Direct evidence has not been established.
● “Retreat in mosques means the work of doctors or researchers.” This is a contemporary analogy, not the direct sense of 2:187.
● “The houses in 24:36 are the hearts of believers.” Possible as metaphor; not established as the verse’s referent.
● “Entering houses from their backs” in 2:189 rejects surface readings and commands esoteric interpretation. This reading needs independent evidence and cannot be presented as settled.
● The absence of a command phrased “build a mosque” proves that the Qur’an does not recognize mosque buildings. Argument from silence is insufficient.
● Those who do not study cosmology lose their way to God. The cited verses do not support this universal claim.
● Space science as a whole is false or controlled by a single conspiracy. This requires independent empirical evidence.
● The Qur’an defines heaven as higher consciousness, earth as psychological stability, or awareness as an orbit. These remain proposed analogies, not direct definitions.
● Seven as a Qur’anic code for completeness or cognitive reprogramming in the circumambulation passages. The cited verses do not establish this claim.
The answer to the central question
The Qur’anic network allows the mosque and the house to be considered through place, function, and human awareness, but it does not merge these levels or provide a universal symbolic dictionary. The strongest finding is that named places are related to purposes and actions, and that a name alone does not exempt those actions from moral evaluation. “House of awareness” and “mosque of orientation” may be useful contemplative images if they remain declared hypotheses and are tested against the full range of passages.

The card does not make the hypothesis true. It makes the claim, evidence, limits, and possible failure visible so another reader can review it.

Summary
This book studies masjid/masājid and bayt/buyūt in their Qur’anic contexts and traces their relations to prostration, direction, remembrance, retreat, pilgrimage, construction, dwelling, security, purification, and entry. The words do not collapse into one definition. A mosque appears in several spatial and devotional contexts; the house appears as dwelling, sacred center, parable, and other references. Context identifies function, while functional non-synonymy prevents distinct words and places from being treated as interchangeable.
The book’s main argument is that the Qur’anic address guides the human being and presents places with functions that their contexts make clear. A material reading is necessary where the text names a place or action, but it may not exhaust the guidance opened by the passage. At the same time, inward reflection may not erase the spatial or ritual reference.
The book therefore offers “house of awareness,” “mosque of orientation,” “inner circumambulation,” and the relation between the witnessed cosmos and human awareness as paths for reflection. Their status remains visible: some are network-based inferences; others are hypotheses or models. The text alone does not establish that every house is the heart, that circumambulation means inward research, or that heaven and earth are fixed psychological levels. Their value lies in the questions they raise and their openness to testing, not in presenting them as ready-made Qur’anic definitions.
QCROS keeps this inquiry accountable: gather the evidence, read the context and relations, confront the claim with opposing material, and state its status. The reader moves from wording to context, from context to the wider network, and then to the human question the passage may open—returning at every step to the Qur’anic measure.
Conclusion
This study reads Qur’anic place in relation to the human being: as a place with presence, as a setting for functions the discourse identifies, and as a possible opening for reflection on guidance, civilization, and awareness. Inward and symbolic models remain human efforts subject to revision. They are measured by their fidelity to the wording and context, their treatment of opposing evidence, and their ability to withstand a reverse test.
The method joins an outward meaning that is respected with an inward reflection that is tested. It invites a wider reading of the verses while keeping clear the difference between what the Qur’an says and what the reader proposes.
