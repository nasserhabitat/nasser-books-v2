II. Translator’s Note: The Bridge of Meaning
This English edition is a condensed conceptual adaptation. It is not a word-for-word translation, but rather an "extraction of essence." It presents the core philosophical framework in accessible English, omitting the exhaustive linguistic debates and classical references found in the original Arabic text.
For the academic researcher: The original Arabic version remains the primary source for comprehensive linguistic analysis, detailed exegesis (Tafsir), and the complete bibliography.
This adaptation also preserves the methodological status of the Arabic edition. A claim marked OPEN remains open; a CANDIDATE remains a candidate; a RESISTED or STRONGLY RESISTED claim is not revived through simplification; and a CLOSED result remains closed only within its declared scope. The aim is clarity without scientific inflation.
Key Qur’anic terms are sometimes retained in transliteration—such as nafs, tawaffī, barzakh, and baʿth—when a single English equivalent would prematurely narrow the field under study.

Reader’s Guide and Research Charter
This volume is designed for readers who wish to continue the research, not merely receive its conclusion. Each chapter begins with explicit Qur’anic statements and proceeds to observations, relationships, candidate explanations, and counter-tests. It is possible to hold an interesting model without presenting it as the only permissible reading.
READ → COLLECT → COMPARE → HYPOTHESIZE → TEST → JUDGE → CONTINUE.
In the QCROS research sequence, “TEXT” means a claim directly grounded in its cited passage; “OBSERVED” means a feature evident within that passage; “CANDIDATE” is a proposed reading with reasons to investigate; “OPEN” indicates that a decisive test has not yet been completed; “NOT ESTABLISHED” marks an unsupported inference. “CLOSED” applies only to the specified proposition and scope, never automatically to every use of the root.
• The Qur’an is the controlling primary corpus. Earlier volumes by the author are not independent Qur’anic evidence.
• A shared audience, moral accountability, speech, or practical work does not prove identical biological origin.
• Adjacent statements do not, without more evidence, establish a causal mechanism.
• The order in which verses are presented is not automatically the chronology of their events.
• A metaphorical model cannot silently replace a word’s primary contextual sense.
• The Mathānī method may generate hypotheses from lexical and letter relationships; it must also identify and count resistant examples.


Introduction — From Fear Narratives to Testable Questions
Jinn and devils occupy a large place in popular storytelling. Often they are presented as shapeless beings, owners of unlimited secret power, or permanent explanations for human fear. This volume does not accept those depictions merely because they have been repeated. Neither, however, does it assume that exposing a folkloric exaggeration proves that the Qur’anic jinn must be ordinary humans, modern surveillance agencies, or psychological impulses. The rejection of one unproved picture is not proof of the opposite picture.
The research begins by listening to the terms as the Qur’an deploys them. It studies concealment in the root j-n-n, mutual address to jinn and ins, accounts of first creation, the description of Iblīs and his offspring, the workers in Solomon’s kingdom, the listening community in Sūrat al-Jinn, and the language of flame, molten substances, angels, remembrance, and moral responsibility. The same discipline governs the last chapters: modern data systems can help us imagine a functional form of hidden agency but do not identify a Qur’anic creature by analogy.
A central distinction shapes the argument. The Qur’an makes claims about creation, agency, hearing, responsibility, and judgment. The researcher may construct conceptual models of those claims; but those models must preserve the evidence they are trying to explain. This is especially important where the Arabic manuscript explores an original thesis: jinn and humans might represent one broader human stock, differentiated by concealment or social function. The hypothesis is articulated here because it belongs to the author’s research, not because the presently examined texts have proved it.

1  |  The Root J-N-N and the Problem of Reference
The lexical field of concealment
The Arabic consonantal root j-n-n is associated across several derivatives with covering, screening, or concealment. A fetus is hidden in the womb; a garden shelters what grows inside it; a protective shield covers its bearer; the night covers what daylight had made visible. Such relationships provide a genuine lexical starting point. They caution against taking a modern horror image as the inherent meaning of a Qur’anic word.
Nevertheless, a root’s semantic field does not tell us the biological identity of every referent named by a derivative. The word for an infant in the womb, for example, does not become a name for every covered object. A defensible research path therefore runs from root observation to the immediate grammar of the verse, and only then to a hypothesis about the referent. In the Arabic manuscript, “concealment” is a strongly motivated lexical observation, while “jinn are merely people concealed from public view” remains a candidate social reading.
The serpent comparison: movement or named creature?
In Moses’ staff narratives, the staff becomes a moving serpent, and in other passages it shakes “as though it were jān(n)” (al-Naml 27:10; al-Qaṣaṣ 28:31). One possible observation is the vivid suggestion of sudden motion. Yet the simile does not prove that jān(n) is nothing but a verb-like description of movement. A comparison may place a visible thing alongside another creature known for its movement. The account therefore invites a lexical test; it does not eliminate the creation passages in al-Ḥijr and al-Raḥmān.
This is an exemplary reverse test: if a definition of jān(n) based on movement alone cannot interpret “He created the jān(n)” (al-Raḥmān 55:15), that definition is not ready for universal application. Conversely, if a particular theory of a fully invisible body cannot explain why the staff is described by that simile, it must address the simile rather than omit it.
The hearing community and the social proposal
Sūrat al-Jinn 72:1–2 portrays a group listening to the Qur’an, recognizing guidance, and responding in faith. The chapter also depicts distinctions among them, including different moral stances (72:11, 14). In al-Aḥqāf 46:29–32 a group listens and returns to warn its own people. Such passages establish meaningful reception and communication. They provide a legitimate basis for discussing the social and cognitive roles of those described as jinn.
The manuscript’s sociological proposal interprets some uses through distance, unfamiliarity, or concealment from the general public. But neither the hearing nor the act of returning to a community is equivalent to a DNA test, and the text does not specify that the listeners were a previously unknown human tribe near Mecca. Responsible analysis recognizes communicative capacity while declining to assert what the verses leave unsaid.

2  |  Shared Address, Shared Responsibility, and the Creation Texts
The force and limits of a common address
The Qur’an addresses “the company of jinn and humans” in al-Raḥmān 55:33 and al-Anʿām 6:130. In al-Anʿām, the discourse extends across messengers, response, warning, culpability, and recompense (6:128–133). The two groups are thus not sealed away from a shared moral universe. The text depicts interaction, answerability, and forms of association. An inquiry into shared social space and responsibility is well supported.
But the noun maʿshar, a group or company in an address, is not an explicit statement of common ancestry. Likewise, “messengers from among you” (6:130) requires careful study of the audience and pronoun; it cannot be used alone to prove that every member of both named groups is a biological human. The discussion of descendants of former peoples (6:133) is especially tempting as a proof of common lineage. Yet its force depends upon precise pronoun reference and cannot bypass the question of which addressees are included at each stage of the speech.
Clay and fire are not automatically social metaphors
Two striking pairs appear in the creation corpus. Al-Ḥijr 15:26–27 places the creation of the human from clay alongside the earlier creation of the jān(n) from the fire of samūm. Al-Raḥmān 55:14–15 similarly juxtaposes the human from clay like pottery and the jān(n) from a mārij of fire. The parallel construction is positive evidence that the text differentiates origins or descriptions of origination. It resists any interpretation that dismisses “fire” as merely an emotional temperament without explanation.
The manuscript explores whether clay can suggest visible settlement, density, or stability while fire suggests motion, intensity, and hidden influence. Such associations can enrich an ethical or literary model. They do not independently prove that “from fire” really means “an elite with an intense temperament.” The Qur’an also says that the human was created “of haste” (al-Anbiyāʾ 21:37), but a psychological expression in one verse does not force the two creation narratives to be psychological as well. Context is decisive.
Accountability does not settle anatomy
The listeners in Sūrat al-Jinn can understand, judge, and make moral decisions. In Solomon’s story, the assigned workers build and dive. These activities make it unjustifiable to describe all jinn in the Qur’an as mere mental suggestions with no agency. Still, their presence does not entail that they share all human anatomical structures. The creation corpus and the address corpus must be read together without allowing either to silence the other.
The volume preserves three live positions: H1, distinct creation with shared accountability; H2, a common human biological stock with functional differentiation; and H3, contextual plurality in the meanings of jinn, jān(n), and shayṭān. The present evidence does not establish final victory for any position.

3  |  “Before”: The Boundaries of Pre-Adamic History
A relative statement of precedence
The phrase “min qablu,” meaning “from before” or “earlier,” occurs in al-Ḥijr 15:27, where the jān(n) is said to have been created earlier than the human named in the preceding verse. Within that construction, chronological precedence is a serious and direct observation. It is not, however, a complete history of ancient Earth. The phrase does not specify a geographical location, population system, genetic continuity, the dates of a civilization, or a political relationship to Adam.
The author’s historical proposal asks whether the text can be read as alluding to a rational form of life prior to the Adamic stage, perhaps even an earlier human phase. That question can be investigated, but the leap from “created before” to “a human civilization before Adam” requires other passages that identify place, descent, and kind. Those necessary connections have not yet been secured.
What did the angels know?
In al-Baqarah 2:30, the angels ask about the placing of a khalīfah on Earth and the prospect of corruption and bloodshed. One explanation supposes prior knowledge of a population whose behavior provided such an example. The text itself does not state that this is the source of their expectation. The dialogue therefore cannot yet establish an earlier human civilization, still less that the jinn of other passages were its inhabitants.
Another proposal distinguishes Adam as the beginning of an especially appointed phase of responsible knowledge rather than the first biological human. The Arabic manuscript examines it alongside the notions of election and vicegerency. Yet the proposed distinction is an interpretive reconstruction, not a plainly stated historical proposition in al-Baqarah 2:30. The student of the text should record what the dialogue says, then mark as open whatever historical explanation is supplied beyond it.
Why this restraint matters
Prehistory invites imaginative reconstructions, and modern science can supply independent information about the human past. But those data must not be covertly attributed to a Qur’anic statement that does not contain them. The Qur’an’s earlier-creation claim remains part of the textual corpus; the species identity and historical narrative of that earlier creature remain the subject of further investigation.

4  |  Resistant Verses and the Three Hypotheses
An attractive thesis becomes a serious research hypothesis only when it survives its strongest objections. The Arabic manuscript therefore devotes its fourth chapter to passages that trouble quick explanations. The following cases are presented as a structured examination, not a catalogue intended to win a debate by accumulation.
Iblīs, his company, and unequal vision
Al-Kahf 18:50 explicitly states that Iblīs “was of the jinn,” and the verse also names his descendants. Al-Aʿrāf 7:27 speaks of the devil and his company seeing the human audience from where they are not seen. These claims cannot simply be erased by describing evil as institutional manipulation. A functional account of temptation is welcome; substituting a human institution for the named actor requires separate textual evidence. Nor does the reference to Iblīs’ descendants say that every jinn is necessarily descended from him. Both universal biological conclusions would exceed the immediate text.
The unequal visibility in al-Aʿrāf is likewise specific. One may take it as an example of asymmetric observation, but the verse does not describe infrared sensors, digital platforms, holograms, or a physical formula for the beings’ bodies. A theory that makes it purely social concealment and a theory that extrapolates a complete anatomy from the phrase must each answer a different part of the textual question.
Solomon: action, construction, and the ignorance of the unseen
Solomon’s assigned jinn and devils perform work in Sabaʾ 34:12–13, al-Anbiyāʾ 21:82, and Ṣād 38:37–38. The texts speak of building, diving, and a group held in fetters. These are agency claims within a special divine arrangement. They resist the assertion that jinn in those passages were only human thoughts. But material labor is no direct demonstration that the workers were biologically ordinary humans. The text also does not say that their fetters were necessarily made from a particular modern metal, or that their diving was exclusively for pearls, copper, or minerals.
The death of Solomon is disclosed only after the earth-creature consumes his staff (Sabaʾ 34:14). The passage explicitly denies the workers the hidden knowledge that would have spared them a humiliating situation. This is a decisive antidote to unlimited claims about jinn knowing the unseen. It does not, however, provide an anatomical identification of those workers. Knowledge-limitation and species-identification are different propositions.
The ʿifrīt and the throne
In al-Naml 27:38–40 an ʿifrīt from the jinn offers to bring a queen’s throne within a stated interval, and a second speaker associated with knowledge of the Book presents a different proposal. The episode relates claims of capability, authority, and knowledge. A hypothesis must face those features without simply renaming the ʿifrīt a human foreman or supplying invented transport technology. The exact physical mechanism remains outside the evidence presently available.
Hearing, guarded heaven, and shihāb
Sūrat al-Jinn 72:8–10 reports a group’s speech about reaching the heaven, finding guards and shuhub (fiery phenomena), and previously occupying places for hearing. Al-Ḥijr 15:16–18 and al-Ṣāffāt 37:6–10 speak about celestial adornment, protection, and pursuit. Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:210–223 distinguishes the Qur’anic revelation from the descent and hearing attributed to devils in the domain of deception.
The author has experimented with an information-security analogy: listening without permission, obstacles to access, and false transmission. This analogy helps generate questions about authority over knowledge. But it cannot replace the celestial language with “radio interception” or “media disinformation” by fiat. The identity of the speaker, the point of view of each passage, and the precise kind of “hearing” must be evaluated before merging them into one modern process. The claim that all the jinn in the Qur’an are human intelligence operatives is not established by these texts.
The people of al-Jinn and al-Aḥqāf
Sūrat al-Jinn describes groups with moral difference, speakers who listen, and “men from humans” seeking refuge with “men from jinn” (72:6). Al-Aḥqāf 46:29–32 describes listeners turning to their people as warners. These verses display patterns of personhood, communication, and group life. They need not be reduced to isolated psychological metaphors. Yet they are not sufficient to infer common chromosomes, common descent, or an exact social identity in Arabian history.

5  |  Flame, Nuḥās, and Qiṭr: Materiality, Function, and Context
The sequence in Sūrat al-Raḥmān
Al-Raḥmān 55:33 addresses the two groups, jinn and humans, and challenges them with the prospect of passing beyond the regions of the heavens and earth, subject to sulṭān. Verse 35 follows with a warning involving shuwāẓ of fire and nuḥās. Reading the warning in isolation has led to imaginative mechanical descriptions, including seals across passages or a special energy barrier directed at jinn bodies. The larger sequence allows one to identify a challenge, a condition, and a warning. It does not specify the machinery of a technological confinement system.
Sulṭān is a crucial concept in the manuscript’s wider inquiry into knowledge, warrant, and authority. Its appearance does invite philosophical reflection on what makes transgression or passage possible. But “sulṭān” in this verse cannot be fixed as spacecraft propulsion, sovereign permission, scientific knowledge, or a mental password without further textual work. The same applies to describing “heavens” and “earth” exclusively as a person’s beliefs and mental reference frames. Those are possible allegorical extensions, not compulsory replacements for the Qur’anic setting.
Does nuḥās mean copper?
Nuḥās in al-Raḥmān 55:35 has been understood by exegetical sources as smoke and also as brass or copper-like metal (ṣufr). This competing lexical evidence matters. If it is smoke, a claim about molten copper sealing biological pores collapses at its first premise. If a metallic referent is intended, the Qur’an still does not provide a chemical mechanism or identify specific deficiencies in the bodies of the threatened groups. Thus neither interpretation licenses a theory of copper deficiency among early jinn or the suppression of a species by metallurgical means.
The book also explores the consonantal cluster n-ḥ-s, comparing the words nuḥās, naḥs (ill-fortune or harmful days), and related lexicographical material. A semantic relation is worth testing. Yet the proposal that n-ḥ-s literally encodes hidden thermal penetration and the closing of pores is a candidate letter-model, not an attested universal root definition. Resistant examples include the “ill-fated days” and violent wind in Fuṣṣilat 41:16 and al-Qamar 54:19. Literal heat is not expressly the governing feature in each instance.
Qiṭr in two constructive settings
Sabaʾ 34:12 speaks of an eye or spring of qiṭr made to flow for Solomon. Al-Kahf 18:96 uses qiṭr in the command to pour it on a barrier associated with pieces of iron. A molten metal reading, frequently molten copper, is supported by major lexicographical and exegetical discussions; some interpretations explore other molten substances. The Qur’anic verbs emphasize flowing and pouring. Those contexts differ substantially from the warning in al-Raḥmān.
This is the legitimate center of the comparison. Qiṭr appears in settings of pouring and building; nuḥās appears in a passage of warning. The distribution is contextual, not proof of an eternal opposition whereby qiṭr means “blessing” and nuḥās means “curse.” Nor does the proximity of Solomon’s jinn workers and the flowing qiṭr make the metal a necessary means of restraining them.
Letter-pairs as hypotheses rather than physical instruments
The Arabic appendices test n-ḥ-s beside n-kh-s and n-j-s while using kh-n-s as a control for reordered consonants. The differences in Qur’anic attestation are essential: n-ḥ-s is represented by the nuḥās and naḥs forms examined in three passages; n-j-s appears in al-Tawbah 9:28; n-kh-s is invoked from Arabic lexicography rather than as a direct Qur’anic attestation. Shared letters do not independently establish shared physics. A valid test must announce a letter’s meaning before selecting examples and must count contrary cases. Otherwise the proposed code is being read into the evidence and then “confirmed” by itself.
The chapter therefore retains a two-level result: differences of verse-context are observed; totalizing etymologies and material mechanisms remain unproved. This distinction is one way in which Mathānī can strengthen research: it is a disciplined discovery tool only when it also exposes its own possible errors.

6  |  Concealment and Agency in the Digital Age
Hiddenness has degrees
Modern life shows how effective an unseen operation can be without being metaphysically invisible. Satellites, remote sensors, data infrastructure, public and private surveillance systems, and digital recommendation engines can operate at distances that ordinary sight does not overcome. We often see a system’s output without seeing the people, devices, or models that generated it. This gives readers a new analogy for relative concealment and unequal access to information.
The distinction is necessary. Intelligence organizations and space agencies perform different kinds of work and are subject to different public mandates and practices. The mere fact of concealed procedures does not make a specific institution evil, nor demonstrate that it secretly watches a particular person. Scientific observation, legitimate security work, invasive surveillance, and deliberate manipulation require separate evidence and ethical judgment.
What analogy may and may not do
The unequal visibility mentioned in al-Aʿrāf 7:27 can prompt reflection on cameras and sensors, since one party can observe another without reciprocal detection. But it remains a statement about the devil and his company; no modern observation instrument establishes their biological identity. Similarly, al-Raḥmān 55:33 can prompt questions about power, knowledge, and the limits of travel, yet the verse is not thereby converted into a forecast of particular satellites or aerospace programs.
The terms “hearing” and “guarding” in Sūrat al-Jinn can be compared with issues of access control, interception, and information integrity. Yet a functional resemblance does not make the celestial narrative merely a parable of communications networks. The shuhub in the verse are not proven to be cryptographic countermeasures, and claims about space-based systems cannot determine the meaning of the Qur’anic word.
The modern “companion” and the circulation of messages
Al-Zukhruf 43:36–37 describes a person turning away from remembrance and receiving a satanic qarīn who obstructs the way while a sense of being rightly guided may persist. Digital systems can reinforce information patterns by promoting material resembling what users previously consumed. This modern phenomenon provides an illustrative analogy for repeated influence and selective exposure. It does not establish that algorithms are the Qur’anic qarīn, or that online participation proves a person’s religious condition.
Al-Anʿām 6:112 speaks of adorned or embellished deceptive speech associated with devils among humans and jinn. The notion that falsehood can be packaged attractively has a clear textual basis. However, applying the ethical warning to particular platforms or individuals must be grounded in their acts and evidence, not in the label “jinn” or an unsupported accusation about hidden power.
A discipline of privacy and knowledge
Al-Ḥujurāt 49:12 warns against spying, while al-Isrāʾ 17:36 forbids following claims without knowledge. These verses help frame the ethical questions of the age: What information should be collected? Who controls it? How can the claim be checked? What separates protective investigation from intrusion? What does a reader really know about a hidden institution, and what is only conjecture? The relevant Qur’anic teaching is not that everything secret is satanic, but that knowledge, judgment, and personal dignity impose obligations.
The chapter thus shifts attention from dramatic appearances to the discipline of identifying agency. It is possible to observe effects without identifying causes, identify some causes without grasping the whole mechanism, and master a technology without possessing knowledge of the unseen in the divine sense. Sabaʾ 34:14 shows the jinn workers’ ignorance of a crucial hidden event; al-Naml 27:65 maintains the distinct status of divine knowledge of the unseen.

7  |  From Fragmented Reading to Systemic Reflection
A single discourse, different responses
In al-Baqarah 2:26 the Qur’an says that the same example leads many in different directions; the verse also specifies the context of the divine parable and those described as fāsiqūn. Al-Isrāʾ 17:82 describes the Qur’an as healing and mercy for believers while others incur loss. These passages provide an important observation: response and reception matter. They do not establish that every reader who disagrees with our interpretation is corrupt, shallow, or psychologically deficient.
A researcher committed to reflection must also be capable of interrogating his own preferred metaphors. A superficially literal reading may ignore context; yet a vividly symbolic reading can do equal violence to the text when it replaces the verse with the interpreter’s invented mechanism. “Depth” is not the same as obscurity. Sometimes fidelity requires retaining a concrete account and refusing to transform it into an allegory.
Tadabbur and Mathānī
The Qur’an asks its audience to reflect on the text (al-Nisāʾ 4:82; Ṣād 38:29) and describes itself with the term mathānī (al-Zumar 39:23). These passages support the value of careful reading and cross-textual attention. They do not by themselves authorize every contemporary reconstruction of letter-pairs as a divinely programmed physical code. The book uses Mathānī for comparison, generating propositions that must still withstand contextual and lexical testing.
A complete interpretive cycle should include the strongest passage against one’s view. If a reading of “jān(n)” as pure movement cannot face a verse about creation, it remains unsealed. If a theory of common human descent cannot explain the contrast of origins, it remains unsealed. If a literalist ontology supplies anatomical details the Qur’an does not give, it too crosses the boundary of evidence. This mutual discipline gives the research its scientific integrity.
Striving with the Qur’an and human dignity
Al-Furqān 25:52 speaks of a major striving “with it,” understood by many exegetes as striving with the Qur’an through argument and proclamation. The passage affirms the significance of intellectual and moral engagement, but it does not reduce every use of jihād to debate or deny that other passages refer to armed conflict in their own contexts. Al-Baqarah 2:256 declares that there is no coercion in religion. Ethical inquiry must resist coercive distortion while also studying each legal or historical discourse in its proper textual network.
Al-Rūm 30:21 presents tranquility, affection, and mercy in the marital relationship; al-Isrāʾ 17:70 speaks of the dignity granted to the children of Adam. These are foundational considerations for human relations. But a concern for dignity does not permit the interpreter to avoid difficult words or declare contested terms such as qiwāmah fully defined without a dedicated corpus. Values and philological scrutiny should support, not replace, one another.
The inheritance of stories and the responsibility to verify
Popular accounts, historical traditions, and later religious writings have long influenced how readers picture jinn and end-time themes. This volume asks for a sharp boundary: a report in a tradition is not, for that reason alone, a statement of the Qur’an. Conversely, the absence of a name from the Qur’an does not by itself demonstrate that every report bearing it is historically false. The question “What does this Qur’anic passage say?” differs from “What does this later source claim, and how could it be assessed?” Both questions can be discussed, but their evidentiary standards must not be confused.
The same discipline applies to this book. A novel proposal should receive no automatic privilege because it rejects inherited imagery. Readers should ask whether the writer has presented the full contextual passage, tested its grammar, recognized counterevidence, and named the limits of his claim. The intellectual virtue of the method is not that it guarantees an exciting new answer; it is that it makes the path toward any answer inspectable.

Conclusion  |  A Map of Knowledge, Not a Forced Verdict
This volume has traced a Qur’anic network that is more intricate than the ordinary opposition between “visible humans” and “invisible spirits.” The text describes creation, prior existence, shared address, moral differentiation, listening, work, unequal vision, temptation, and the limits of knowledge. The Qur’an also distinguishes names and contexts in ways that resist simple identification with contemporary institutions or with a single psychological process.
What can be stated confidently within the examined passages is limited but substantial. The jān(n) is said to have been created earlier in al-Ḥijr; distinct creation descriptions are present in al-Raḥmān and al-Ḥijr; Iblīs is described as of the jinn and as having descendants; some jinn hear and communicate; Solomon’s assigned jinn undertake work; the workers do not possess the hidden knowledge attributed to them in the popular imagination; and human and jinn audiences share forms of warning and accountability.
What has not been closed is equally important. The present tests do not demonstrate that jinn and humans are biologically one species, that the jān(n) names pre-Adamic human civilization, that fire is merely a psychological temperament, or that nuḥās is a special copper-based restraint on jinn. Nor do the texts make satellites, intelligence personnel, or digital algorithms the definitive referents of Qur’anic jinn. The candor of these open results protects the text from being made to serve a conclusion the author has not proved.
The goal is thus not to replace old mythology with a new technological mythology. It is to restore an accountable form of reading: to locate each verse, distinguish what is observed from what is imagined, test relationships against resistant cases, and make room for a reader to pursue the unfinished question. In this sense, a well-formed open question is a stronger scholarly achievement than a false closure.

Appendix C. The companion and the descending angels
The Arabic ninth appendix develops a paired study of Fuṣṣilat 41:30–31 and al-Zukhruf 43:36–39. In the first, those who declare the Lord and remain upright receive an angelic message of reassurance and good news, and a statement of guardianship extending to this life and the next. In the second, turning away from remembrance is linked to the assignment of a satanic companion and to misleading self-assurance. These are two meaningful textual relationships, not a proof that the agents share a biological identity or that they produce measurable electromagnetic effects in the brain.
The imperfect verb tatanazzalu, “the angels descend,” can support inquiry into recurrence. Its grammar alone does not establish continuous descent every instant. Maryam 19:64, which associates angelic descent with the Lord’s command, and al-Qadr 97:4, whose context specifies a particular occasion of descent, are crucial controls. The statement of guardianship in worldly life is not identical to a statement of the frequency, timing, or mechanism of every descent. The author’s independent work on angels suggests a functional “execution of divine command”; that formulation remains a comparative research model, not a definitive statement that angels are physical laws or neural impulses.
A further contrast places the “good life” promised to those who believe and act rightly (al-Naḥl 16:97) beside the constricted life described for turning from remembrance (Ṭā Hā 20:123–124). The passages do not explicitly say that every good life is directly caused by angelic descent or every constricted life by the qarīn. Nor may the verses be used to label ordinary sadness, illness, or poverty as evidence that someone has turned from God. Ethical and causal boundaries must be preserved.

Appendix D. A controlled test of letter-pair models
The manuscripts on nuḥās and the root n-ḥ-s propose motions for individual consonants and overlapping pairs. A scholarly test has to establish these definitions before selecting favorable roots. It must then seek resistant examples, compare attested and unattested Qur’anic roots, and distinguish root changes from letter reversals. The proposed comparison among n-ḥ-s, n-kh-s, n-j-s, and kh-n-s illustrates the problem: one root appears in three studied Qur’anic cases, one appears in a single verse, and another is invoked as an external lexical comparison. They cannot be assigned equal evidentiary weight.
The sound-based proposal may remain fruitful as a heuristic, but a string of consonants does not prove a “universal thermal penetration” rule, a mechanism of bodily closure, or a particle-level concept of ritual impurity. The decisive question is whether the model makes accurate predictions for previously unused examples without being redefined after every failure.
