### Introduction: Why This Book?
In contemporary Islamic thought, the question of "wine" ( *khamr* ) has become more than a minor jurisprudential debate about a specific drink. It has turned into a window revealing a much deeper crisis: a crisis in the very method of reading the Quran itself.
Historically, Islamic consciousness has almost completely identified *khamr* with intoxicating beverages. This understanding, formed over centuries, moved from the realm of scholarly interpretation ( *ijtihad* ) to that of unquestioned cultural certainty. However, a direct return to the Quran reveals a far more complex picture. The Quran does not present the concept of wine in a single, isolated verse. Instead, it distributes the term across multiple, interconnected contexts: alongside gambling ( *maysir* ), in the context of obstructed perception, as a description of Paradise, within the dream of Prophet Joseph, and in discussions about "drunken" eyesight.
This book argues that the inherited crisis is not that the tradition offered a specific understanding, but that this understanding turned into a "closed system," preventing any re-evaluation of the concept's original semantic structure. The goal is not to demolish tradition or to legalize the prohibited. Rather, it is to reopen the methodological question: **How do we read the Quranic concept?** Is a late juristic term enough to understand the original structure of the concept within the Quranic language itself?
Thus, the question of wine becomes a question about the nature of consciousness itself: how does one lose awareness? How is perception "covered"? And how does a human being transform from a reflective creature into a consumer of ready-made meanings?
---
### Chapter 1: The Central Problem – Drink or Function?
The book's central problem stems from a simple observation: the Islamic debate on "wine" transformed from an attempt to understand a complex Quranic concept into a jurisprudential debate centered on a specific substance. While this produced a coherent legal system, it also pushed deeper Quranic questions to the margins.
The book poses its essential question: **When the Quran spoke of "wine," was it establishing the prohibition of a specific substance, or was it revealing a functional structure related to the covering (veiling) of perception and the disabling of consciousness?**
This question does not aim to deny the physical dimension of wine, nor to reduce the concept to pure symbolism. Rather, it seeks to re-examine the relationship between substance, effect, and function within the Quranic system. The Quran often moves not by the logic of rigid physical classification, but by the logic of functions and outcomes—the logic of a thing's effect on the human being, consciousness, society, and civilization.
Therefore, the research goes beyond the traditional question, "Is drinking wine forbidden?" to a deeper one: **What makes something "wine" ( *khamr* ) in the Quranic perspective in the first place?**
---
### Chapter 2: The Linguistic Root (Kh-M-R): From Material to Function
The root (Kh-M-R) in Arabic fundamentally revolves around **covering, veiling, and concealing**. From this root, we get:
- *Khamara*: to cover or hide something.
- *Khimar*: a head cover or veil.
- *Khamira* (yeast): the agent that mixes with dough, changing it internally beneath a cover.
- *Khamara al-'aql*: something that mixes with the mind and covers its clarity.
This linguistic analysis suggests that "wine" ( *khamr* ) in the Quran may be closer to a **functional name** than a chemical or physical one. It is not just "what is made from grapes," but rather **"whatever performs the function of covering (veiling) perception."**
The book carefully distinguishes between:
- **The Physical Substance:** The historical and material reality of an intoxicating drink.
- **The Functional Structure:** The effect of this substance in disabling conscious discrimination ( *Furqan* ).
The Quran appears more concerned with the effect on consciousness than with the abstract physical classification. This explains why the Quran consistently links wine to:
- Turning away from the remembrance of God ( *dhikr* ).
- Disabling prayer.
- Enmity and hatred.
The central problem is not the "liquid" itself, but **what it does to human perception**.
---
### Chapter 3: Prohibition vs. Avoidance (Ijtinab)
The final and most decisive verse on wine is in Chapter 5 (Al-Ma'idah, 90-91):
> *"O you who have believed, indeed, intoxicants (khamr), gambling (maysir), [sacrifices on] stone altars [to idols] (ansab), and divining arrows (azlam) are but defilement (rijs) from the work of Satan, so avoid (fajtanibuhu) it that you may be successful. Satan only wants to cause between you enmity and hatred through intoxicants and gambling and to avert you from the remembrance of Allah and from prayer. So will you not desist?"*
This verse is the cornerstone of the book's argument. The command is not just "do not drink" (prohibition), but **"avoid" (ijtinab)** . The book argues that *ijtinab* is a higher, more comprehensive level of prohibition. It does not just forbid the direct act; it establishes a **"buffer zone"** around the entire system. It forbids the substance, its production, its trade, its social context, and everything that leads to it. It is a systemic quarantine against perceptual corruption.
Critically, the verse links four elements together as a single "matrix of enslavement":
1.  **Wine (Khamr):** The system of covering perception.
2.  **Gambling (Maysir):** The system of random gain that abolishes the law of cause and effect and human effort.
3.  **Stone Altars (Ansab):** The system of establishing rigid, unquestionable authorities and idols (physical or intellectual).
4.  **Divining Arrows (Azlam):** The system of abolishing personal responsibility and rational decision-making by outsourcing choice to chance or external authorities.
The Quran treats these four not as separate sins, but as a **single operational system** that produces a shared destructive outcome: enmity, hatred, and being veiled from divine guidance.

### Chapter 4: Drunkenness (Sukr) as a Disruption of Consciousness
The book conducts a structural analysis of the root (S-K-R), deconstructing it into two dynamic components:
- **(Sakka):** Complete closure, sealing off, and isolation of the receiver.
- **(Kar-ra):** Repetitive, circular motion and re-circulation of data in a closed loop.
Thus, **drunkenness (sukr) is not merely a biological state caused by alcohol.** It is the human cognitive processor entering a **"closed, repetitive, feedback loop of perceptual blockage."** The mind closes its receptive channels (sakka) and then recycles its own confused or delusional data in a circular motion (kar-ra), preventing any new insight or connection with reality.
The book identifies multiple levels of this functional drunkenness:
1.  **Physical (Chemical) Drunkenness:** Temporary disablement by a substance.
2.  **Psychological Drunkenness:** Being overwhelmed by intense emotion (fear, panic, obsessive love) that paralyzes rational judgment.
3.  **Ideological Drunkenness:** Adopting a closed ideological system that isolates the person from seeing universal laws and realities.
4.  **Civilizational Drunkenness:** A backward civilization being mesmerized by a fluid, consumerist model, losing its value system and productive energy.
This is confirmed by the Quranic description of the Day of Judgment: *"And you will see the people [appearing] as if they are drunk (sukara), while they are not drunk; but the punishment of Allah is severe"* (Al-Hajj, 2). Here, the state of perceptual disorientation is produced not by wine, but by the overwhelming shock of confronting divine laws. **The outcome is the same: the failure of the cognitive processor.**
---
### Chapter 5: The Wine of This World vs. The Wine of Paradise
One of the most powerful arguments for the book's functional thesis is the Quran's description of the wine of Paradise (e.g., As-Saffat, 45-47):
> *"A cup [of wine] from a flowing spring will be passed around to them, white and delicious to the drinkers. **No 'ghawl' will they suffer therefrom, nor will they be 'yunzafun' (intoxicated/losing their minds).** "*
The key terms are:
- **Ghawl:** Silent, sudden assassination or destruction (here, of the mind/perception).
- **Yunzafun:** To have one's mind drained or consumed; to lose consciousness.
The wine of Paradise is praised precisely for the **absence** of the negative perceptual effects that define earthly wine. It offers pleasure and delight (which are not inherently bad), but without the "covering" and "disabling" of consciousness.
This leads to the book's crucial distinction:
- **The Pleasure of this World's Wine (Absence/Escape):** A temporary, escapist pleasure that requires the **numbing of consciousness** and withdrawal from painful reality. Pleasure is dependent on "disabling."
- **The Pleasure of Paradise's Wine (Presence/Witnessing):** A higher, existential pleasure achieved through **intensifying presence and expanding clear consciousness**. The drinker does not drink to forget, but becomes more aware, more capable of glorification, praise, and spiritual transformation. Pleasure is dependent on "connection."
This proves that the Quranic legislation is not against pleasure or delight itself. It is against the **"assassination of consciousness" (ghawl)** and the **"draining of the mind" (nafz)** . The prohibition is tied to the *function* (perceptual covering), not the *substance* (liquid).

### Chapter 6: Toward a New Jurisprudence of Consciousness
The book concludes by calling for a fundamental epistemological shift in the Islamic mind: from a "Jurisprudence of Substances and Physical Objects" ( *Fiqh al-A'yan* ) to a **"Jurisprudence of Functions and Universal Laws" ( *Fiqh al-Sunan* ).**
**What the Quran Forbids:**
It forbids the operational intersection between a material input and a perceptual output. It forbids the substance (alcohol) because it inevitably leads (by its vital nature) to perceptual corruption. The substance is the inevitable means, and corruption is the destructive end. The Quranic prohibition closes the circle from its beginning to protect the system.
**The Redefined Concept of "Khamr" (Wine):**
*Khamr* is every factor (physical or systemic) that, by its nature and operation, induces a state of **"perceptual absence and closure"** that disables the mind's function of discrimination ( *Furqan* ) and prevents it from practicing moral and cognitive distinction. The alcoholic substance is the clearest, most striking applied model of this concept in the physical world.
**The Three Levels of Transformation:**
1.  **Natural Fermentation:** A chemical transformation in food (cheese, bread, vinegar) with no psychological or mental effect. This remains within the original permission of creation.
2.  **Intoxication (Iskār):** Partial disablement of consciousness, the beginning of behavioral instability. This is a "point of danger" and a prelude to prohibition.
3.  **Perceptual Disablement (Taghyīb Idrākī):** Complete loss of the ability to reason and discern; reaching the state of "not knowing what one says." This is the **definitive legal cause (manāt)** for the highest level of prohibition: avoidance ( *ijtinab* ).

### Conclusion: Awakening from Civilizational Drunkenness
The book ends with a powerful diagnosis of the contemporary Muslim condition. By fixating on the material "drink" and ignoring the functional "covering," traditional jurisprudence protected the individual from a bottle of wine but left the entire civilization vulnerable to more dangerous forms of "intoxication":
- **Paternal Wine (Khamr Aba'i):** The blind veneration of inherited tradition as a sacred, unquestionable authority.
- **Intellectual Wine:** The fermentation of human knowledge in closed, airless chambers of tradition, producing a solid, heavy, intoxicating ideology.
- **Media Wine:** The engineering of public perception through echo chambers, algorithmic filtering, and the manufacturing of consent.
The Quran came to be a "discernment" ( *Furqan* ) and "insight" ( *Basira* ), to uncover coverings and give sight sharpness to see universal laws. If the Muslim mind regains its methodological tools and frees itself from the drunkenness of blind imitation, it can become worthy of its role: to witness, to build civilization ( *'imara* ), and to prepare for the clear, transparent consciousness of Paradise, where no "ghawl" assassinates the mind, and no "nafz" drains its energy.
**This book is not an invitation to abstract theorizing, but a "protocol for perceptual liberation."** It argues that the human being entrusted with stewardship ( *khalifa* ) is required to maintain his cognitive processor free from all kinds of "wine"—material, traditional, and ideological—to remain qualified for building civilization and connecting with the divine.
