II. Translator’s Note: The Bridge of Meaning
This English edition is a condensed conceptual adaptation. It is not a word-for-word translation, but rather an "extraction of essence." It presents the core philosophical framework in accessible English, omitting the exhaustive linguistic debates and classical references found in the original Arabic text.
For the academic researcher: The original Arabic version remains the primary source for comprehensive linguistic analysis, detailed exegesis (Tafsir), and the complete bibliography.
This adaptation also preserves the methodological status of the Arabic edition. A claim marked OPEN remains open; a CANDIDATE remains a candidate; a RESISTED or STRONGLY RESISTED claim is not revived through simplification; and a CLOSED result remains closed only within its declared scope. The aim is clarity without scientific inflation.
Key Qur’anic terms are sometimes retained in transliteration—such as nafs, tawaffī, barzakh, and baʿth—when a single English equivalent would prematurely narrow the field under study.
Editorial application to this volume
In this book, hajj, bayt, tawaf, sha’a’ir, taqwa, Safa and Marwah are retained when a single English rendering would turn an unresolved lexical question into a purported conclusion. Surah references are maintained by name; English descriptions of Qur’anic wording are contextual paraphrases. The Arabic edition’s claim-rank system governs every chapter of this adaptation.

Reader's Orientation
A book of inquiry, not a replacement rite
This book asks what may be learned from the Qur'anic discourse on hajj beyond a bare inventory of physical actions, without erasing the concrete rite to which that discourse refers. Its point of departure is a tension that is also a source of intellectual energy. The Qur'an associates the House with pilgrimage, access, times, ritual actions and named locations. It also speaks of remembrance, mindfulness of God, safety, food for those in need, and benefits that pilgrims are to witness. The author asks how these strands belong together and whether their ethical structure may inspire responsible work in education, research and public life.
The answer advanced here has carefully limited layers. At the textual level, hajj is tied to the House and its particular Qur'anic setting. At the level of relationships, its verses connect action with remembrance, piety, protection and benefit. At the level of a contemporary model, a researcher may use the pattern of orientation, evidence, repeated review, open communication and service as a training framework. This third layer is an analogy designed by the author, not a newly discovered ritual or a Qur'anic synonym for publishing research.
The Arabic volume deliberately stages disagreements with the author's own earlier proposals. It neither dismisses an unusual interpretation because it is unconventional nor accepts it because it is inspiring. Claims must be named according to their support: direct wording, grammatical observation, bounded inference, practical model, or hypothesis awaiting a resisting test. This condensed English edition preserves those boundaries even where it omits extended root inventories, exhaustive lexical debates and the technical records that accompany the Arabic text.
For readers unfamiliar with hajj
Hajj is the pilgrimage associated in the Qur'an with the House in Bakkah, the ability to reach it, its times and acts, and references to places such as Arafat and al-Mash'ar al-Haram. The Qur'an describes that setting through commands and relationships: an announcement of pilgrimage; people coming from distant routes; remembrance during known days; material offerings and feeding; and acts connected with the House. The book does not set out to reconstruct a complete historical manual of pilgrimage. It focuses on what the cited Qur'anic passages directly establish, what they leave unspoken, and how they may inform ethical reflection.
The distinction between explaining a verse and drawing an analogy from it is essential. To say that the House is a concrete destination in the pilgrimage discourse and that a knowledge project also needs a reliable point of reference is not contradictory. It becomes misleading only if the second statement is promoted into the claim that the Qur'anic House is *nothing but* an idea, a laboratory or a library. Similarly, a beneficial research project may be conducted by someone unable to travel; this observation does not prove that the project fulfils the duty of pilgrimage.
How to read the evidence labels
The Arabic research uses a graded register rather than a binary true/false stamp. Its principal labels are [ن] direct text, [ب] linguistic or grammatical description, [ا] induction within a stated corpus, [س] contextual inference, [و] operational model, [ف] open hypothesis, [خ] resisting test, [ح] limit of validity, and [ر] revision. Labels identify the *kind and present status* of a claim. They are not guarantees of infallibility. A result may be supported within a narrow passage while a root-wide generalisation remains OPEN.
The brief formula for the reading journey is: Read → Gather → Compare → Hypothesise → Test → Judge → Continue. Its last step returns the reader to the first. New evidence is not an embarrassment to be hidden but a reason to revise the record.
Part One — A Charter for Open Qur'anic Reflection
Text first, model second
The Qur'an is the governing textual corpus of this study. The author's earlier encyclopaedia of hajj, his writing on letter meanings, and the Mathani system provide questions and analytical instruments, but their conclusions cannot serve as independent evidence that the same conclusions are correct in a new investigation. This is the book's first safeguard against circularity. The text is gathered, the use of words is observed, relationships are proposed, and only then is an explanation tested against passages capable of limiting it.
Two methodological anchors are the call to ponder revelation (Surah Sad 29) and the challenge to reflect on its coherence (Surah al-Nisa 82). The author also considers the description of the Book as *mutashabihan mathani* in Surah al-Zumar 23 alongside the distinction between clear and other verses in Surah Al Imran 7. These expressions must not be collapsed into one unchecked theory. They motivate comparison and inquiry, not a licence to discard the plain constraints of a passage whenever an alternative symbolism appears attractive.
The method treats context as a control on inventive readings. A word is considered in its sentence, syntactic relations, passage, surah and Qur'anic network. One root can support several uses. A recurring word-pair may suggest a hypothesis, but adjacency is not proof of causation, and a sequence of expressions in the text is not automatically a sequence of stages for a contemporary institution. A metaphor that makes teaching memorable is not thereby the lexical meaning of a Qur'anic term.
QCROS: the research cycle
QCROS proceeds from TEXT to OBSERVATION, INDUCTION, RELATIONS, HYPOTHESIS, TEST, STATUS and, where evidence warrants it, LAW or MODEL. In ordinary language, the reader first identifies exactly what was said and where. Next come a description that is not yet an interpretation, a collection of relevant occurrences, and comparison of similarities, grammatical forms, objects and contexts. A testable explanation is then offered. The strongest contrary passages are sought deliberately. Finally, the claim is assigned a status and validity range, not merely declared victorious.
A closed determination is closed *only for the question tested*. If one passage locates a pilgrimage act at the House, it does not follow that all figurative uses of 'house' elsewhere have been exhausted. Conversely, the possibility of symbolic uses elsewhere does not erase the physical references in that passage. This scope discipline allows the book to sustain a lively conversation while refusing to turn a preferred hypothesis into a universal law before the relevant corpus and counterexamples are examined.
The author introduces Mathani (paired or mutually illuminating relationships) and proposed dynamics of letters as discovery tools. Decomposing a root into overlapping letter-pairs can prompt new questions about movement, containment, relation or transition. But the semantic value of a letter or pair must still be independently tested across occurrences, including resistant cases. The Arabic work records the wider ambitions and limitations of that programme; the present adaptation does not present its still-open mappings as proven etymology.
A discipline of multiple insights
Open reflection does not entail that all possible interpretations are equally strong. Different readers may notice different relationships or apply a text fruitfully in different fields, yet a direct grammatical statement, a plausible contextual inference, and a modern ethical analogy bear different burdens of proof. The book is therefore hospitable to competing proposals without making the evidence negotiable. A revision of judgement is a sign of intellectual responsibility when a stronger observation arrives.
The author places special emphasis on spiritual motivation without making inward sincerity a substitute for public argument. Piety and humility matter to the practice of research; they do not make every inference reached by a pious researcher correct. The phrase about none touching the protected Book except the purified (Surah al-Waqi'ah 79) may provoke spiritual reflection, but using it to claim a certain hidden interpretation of hajj requires independent contextual demonstration. Equally, a renewed human understanding of revelation is not to be described as new legislation sent down to the interpreter.
Five safeguards for functional reading
A responsible transfer to contemporary life observes five tests. First, related words are inventoried without assuming every derivative means the same thing. Second, individual citations are read in the network that limits them. Third, any functional application declares whether it is inferred from the wording or designed for present needs. Fourth, the surrounding passage may not be silently cropped to hide a geographical, temporal or ritual connection. Fifth, every ambitious claim must name an observation that could narrow or defeat it. If no conceivable counterexample is allowed, the statement remains meditation or rhetoric, not a demonstrated general law.
Part Two — Hajj, Purpose, Argument and Capacity
Pilgrimage and the semantic family of ḥ-j-j
Surah Al Imran 97 associates the duty with hajj al-bayt, pilgrimage to the House, and with being able to find a way to it. Surah al-Hajj 27 contains the call and the arrival of people from distant routes. These are not incidental ornaments around the word hajj. They establish a concrete textual setting that any competing definition must account for. The same root family also contains words for proof and argument, as in the argument provided to Abraham (Surah al-An'am 83), and a separate use of *hijaj* for years in a work agreement (Surah al-Qasas 27). A related root, j-d-l, concerns disputation and has its own distribution and contexts.
This variety invites the author's working triad: purposeful orientation, evidence presented as *hujjah*, and fair testing through argumentation. The triad is useful in a research project: set a question, build reasons, expose them to challenge. But it remains [و] MODEL. The shared spelling history does not prove that every instance of pilgrimage, proof and years has one interchangeable definition. The passage about an invalid or 'defeated' argument (Surah al-Shura 16) also cautions that the mere existence of a claim or a dispute does not demonstrate its truth.
Need is a motive, not an established etymology
The Arabic study once placed emphasis on connecting hajj to human *need*. The psychological point is compelling: inquiry often starts when a person becomes aware of ignorance, injury or some unmet requirement. Such need can give a research project its ethical purpose. Linguistically, however, an attractive sound resemblance between *hajj* and *hajah* (need) is not by itself enough to identify their roots or to define the ritual. This edition therefore describes need as the proposed opening of an educational journey, not the Qur'an's settled lexical definition of pilgrimage.
Capacity: to reach the House, and to publish responsibly
In Al Imran 97 the relevant capacity is specified by its object: the ability to find a way to it. The pronoun and its contextual referent matter. A modern scholar may borrow the idea of 'capacity' to design criteria for publication—competence to identify a question, gather evidence, challenge conclusions and state limitations. This is *intellectual readiness* in the model, not a replacement meaning for the capacity associated with pilgrimage.
The practical difference matters for inclusion. A housebound student, a farmer, or a reader with few resources can participate in the model of careful inquiry and public benefit. Their ability to do so is a good reason to support open knowledge. It is not a textual argument that visiting the House is optional for those whom the verse addresses in its own domain, or that people who are not professional scholars lack the capacity for hajj.
Announcement, arrival, and public access
The command to announce the pilgrimage in Surah al-Hajj 27 and the response of arrivals 'on foot and on every lean mount' present a call and a movement toward it. A proposed reinterpretation of 'on foot' as 'established scholars' or of the lean mount as the thinker's inner struggle must confront the arrival language, distance and means of transport. These are resistant observations. The book does not discard the imaginative readings, but refuses to let them monopolise the phrase or cancel its setting.
The positive analogy is responsible disclosure. Research should leave a private notebook when the question, evidence and uncertainty can be explained to others; outsiders should be able to examine the work. Announcement does not guarantee correctness, and publishing a paper is not identical to the Qur'anic call to hajj. What transfers is an ethical lesson about accountable communication rather than a legal identity between two acts.
The value and the danger of 'benefits'
The invitation that people 'witness benefits for themselves' (Surah al-Hajj 28) motivates an expansive inquiry into religious, social and material effects. The same passage speaks of remembrance, named days, livestock and feeding. Its text does not prescribe a modern research congress, a cattle market or a particular economic institution as the exclusive form of those benefits. Nor can the word *benefit* alone certify every claimed outcome: Surah al-Baqarah 219 speaks of both benefits and grave harm in another context, a counterexample to equating mere usefulness with overall goodness.
From this evidence the author develops a principle for research and civic work: seek benefit, ask who receives it, investigate hidden costs, and test whether the result lasts. A publication that spreads widely may still mislead, while a modest finding may correct a consequential practice. These standards are [و] an operational evaluation framework. They are not reported by the Qur'an as numeric performance metrics for the pilgrimage itself.
Part Three — The Major Concepts and Their Resistant Contexts
The House, the Ka'bah, refuge and social 'standing'
Surah al-Baqarah 125 describes the House as a place of return and safety; related verses speak of purification, foundations and Abraham's prayer. Surah al-Ma'idah 97 speaks of the Ka'bah, the Sacred House, as a support or standing for people, alongside the sacred month, offerings and garlanded animals. Other passages identify the House in connection with Bakkah, the pilgrimage and the rites. Together they prevent a reading in which 'the House' here is only a mental state or academic institute.
The author nevertheless proposes a limited functional analogy. A research community needs a stable reference, a place to return when claims conflict; it needs safety for criticism, rules that protect participants, and accountability for public outcomes. In that model the 'house of knowledge' is a metaphor for a documented reference system, not the referent of *al-bayt al-haram* in the ritual verse. The text describes a protected locus with connected actions. The model describes an ethical institution inspired by selected relations.
The distinction also protects the wider Qur'anic lexicon. Houses of dwelling, the spider's house, and expressions of plotting during the night do not all turn into intellectual laboratories merely because they share part of a lexical family. The word's complement and context determine the admissible range of interpretation. The original Arabic book works through this problem more extensively than the English adaptation can.
Tawaf: circulating, returning and attending
The command to perform tawaf at the Ancient House (Surah al-Hajj 29) belongs to the pilgrimage discourse. The Qur'an also uses related forms for people repeatedly passing among one another (Surah al-Nur 58), and for movement between places in Surah al-Rahman 44. A *ta'ifah* can denote a party or group rather than a body physically rotating around a fixed centre. The author's corpus notes 41 occurrences within 35 verses across these grouped forms; such totals are an inventory for further testing, not a one-word definition.
The practical analogy is repeated examination of evidence. A scholar 'circles back' to the original texts, the counterarguments and their own assumptions; the circuit must produce correction or a better account of uncertainty rather than a ritualised repetition of a favoured view. Yet this epistemic tawaf remains a constructed image. It neither proves that every form of t-w-f involves an intellectual centre nor turns a discussion group into a pilgrimage rite.
Safa and Marwah: from names to a disputed educational pair
Surah al-Baqarah 158 names al-Safa and al-Marwah among the signs or rites of God and connects moving between them with hajj or 'umrah. The Arabic encyclopaedia had proposed a paired intellectual function: *safa* as purification of evidentiary material, *marwah* as the skilled narration of a result. A different possibility in the research material associated the names with animal-related rites because livestock occurs elsewhere in the same broad discourse. Neither proposal is established by simple juxtaposition.
The English edition preserves the potential teaching exercise without promoting it to original vocabulary. 'Test the material, then report it clearly' is an excellent research habit. It does not follow that al-Marwah derives from the Arabic term for skilled narration, or that al-Safa in this verse literally means data filtering. Both lexical assignments remain OPEN and require evidence beyond a convenient lesson. The direct textual layer is the named pair of rites and the act linked to them; the educational loop is [و] MODEL, with [ف] open linguistic hypotheses.
Sa'y, signs and the purpose of acts
A person's *sa'y* can express striving or effort in diverse Qur'anic contexts (including Surah al-Najm 39 and al-Layl 4). The study uses this range to ask how purposeful effort can become accountable action. It does not use those passages as proof that every mention of sa'y is the same ritual act. Similarly, *sha'a'ir*—signs or rites—cannot be reduced either to empty motions or to free-floating abstractions. Their force depends on the material and relational setting in which the Qur'an names them.
This matters for the bodily dimension of worship. Acts of giving, remembrance or sacrifice can carry inward meaning and social consequences without ceasing to be the acts specified in a passage. The author resists two opposite reductions: treating a rite as physical movement with no ethical horizon, and treating its ethical horizon as authority to rewrite every concrete object into a psychological symbol.
Months, crescents and the boundary of 'making known'
Surah al-Baqarah 189 makes the new crescents markers of time for people and hajj; 197 states that pilgrimage belongs to known months and speaks of undertaking it within them. Surah al-Hajj 28 refers to known days; Surah al-Tawbah 36 gives a counted set of months. The language of time remains visible across this network. The Arabic corpus also distinguishes 'ma'lum' (known) in several contexts from the plural 'ma'lumat' in the two cited hajj-time passages. Similar wording does not make all referents into published scholarly information.
The author's alternative proposal is a social analogy: meaningful knowledge should be made known at agreed, visible points rather than withheld indefinitely. Research teams can schedule opportunities for teaching, announcing results and publicly testing them. But an 'announcement calendar' is [و] MODEL; the claim that Qur'anic pilgrimage months are *only* research announcements, with no temporal referent, is not established in the tested verses. Even a creative association between 'month' and 'publicity' cannot cancel the referent of 'in them' in Surah al-Baqarah 197.
Arafat, movement, the sacred landmark and remembrance
Surah al-Baqarah 198–199 places an act of coming forth from 'Arafat together with remembrance at al-Mash'ar al-Haram, followed by further movement, remembrance and a call to forgiveness. Expressions such as 'from' and 'at', and the named referents, demand explanation. A reading of 'Arafat' solely as 'stages of knowledge' or of *ifadah* solely as circulation of an academic manuscript does not yet satisfy those constraints.
There remains a productive symbolic comparison: knowledge is acquired, reconsidered and brought into public use; a person may need to return from social excitement to disciplined remembrance and humility. That application is not a geography lesson and does not replace geography. The method allows the reader to learn from the connection among movement, recollection and guidance without presenting the modern learning model as the original instruction of the rite.
Offerings, feeding, piety and truthful speech
Surah al-Hajj 36–37 joins the livestock offerings, eating and feeding of others with an explicit distinction between the meat or blood and the mindfulness of God that matters in worship. This is a powerful resource for ethical reflection. It does not logically remove the animal actions that the surrounding passage records. Similarly, the instructions to honour sacred bounds and avoid false speech (Surah al-Hajj 30) can inform a contemporary ethic of honest data, accurate quotations and fair dealing without defining idols as nothing but old scientific theories.
Other practices—completing the rites, constraints when prevented, fulfilment of commitments, and references to shaving or shortening in their respective passages—must be treated within their own contexts. The author may use finishing a project and discarding a failed hypothesis as an analogy, but an educational slogan is not a new exegesis of the prescribed actions. When the book asks how ritual conduct forms moral character, it does not claim the transformation occurs automatically for every participant.
Part Four — Benefit, Ethics and Human Flourishing
From 'witnessing benefits' to the public good
One of the book's recurring questions is whether a rite can have consequences in human relations and the conditions of collective life. The Qur'anic presentation connects remembrance to provisions, feeding, sacred constraints, a protected House and benefit. From these relations the author infers a field of inquiry into social welfare, justice, safety and the dignity of persons. The text itself does not dictate the design of a contemporary university, farm co-operative, open-access library or public agency. Those are proposals for the world's changing circumstances.
An important distinction follows. A purpose present in a verse is not evidence that the purpose has already been achieved in every observed practice. The presence of feeding in the text cannot certify that distribution in a given setting was equitable. The presence of security in the description of the House is not a substitute for investigating the actual safety of participants. Nor does praise for a value remove the need to evaluate present-day institutions with independent evidence rather than with attractive theological labels.
Remembrance, provisioning and those in need
In the pilgrimage passages, remembrance of God is connected with known days, provisions and feeding. The author reads this as a refusal to separate reverence from concern for others. Food and resources are not merely raw materials for a private spiritual experience; they are also linked in the text to persons who receive them. A modern extension may ask: who is excluded from the circulation of resources, and are benefits measured from the point of view of those with the least access?
For knowledge work, the analogy is an open resource that does not end at publication. A research team should consider who can understand or use its findings, what formats are accessible, and whether affected communities can question the conclusions. These are ethical decisions inspired by the verse network and the author's commitment to free learning, not additional legal conditions for the fulfilment of hajj.
Safe participation and an accountable 'standing'
The House is linked with safety in al-Baqarah 125 and with support for people in al-Ma'idah 97. The book draws from these relationships a model of civil protection: secure conditions allow people to participate, challenge assertions and exchange benefits without intimidation. Security in this sense is not silence enforced upon dissenters. A community that allows only the strongest or richest to speak would be a poor example of the ethical 'house' the model hopes to design.
Likewise, 'standing for people' can invite inquiry into conditions under which resources and rights are maintained. The text's association with the sacred month and offerings must stay visible; the contemporary idea of a fair institution is a proposed application. The author rejects the categorical claim that a particular economic or administrative organisation is explicitly prescribed by these words.
How the book proposes to test benefit
An illustrative evaluation card asks six questions: Who may benefit? What problem or resource is addressed? What indicates actual improvement? What adverse effects or exclusions may arise? Who can independently review the outcome? What remains unknown? In a voluntary project, a baseline can be compared with observations during work, after some months and after a longer interval. Descriptive change, long-term persistence and causal attribution are separate issues. A simple before-and-after story does not prove that the model alone caused an improvement.
The author considers agriculture, food distribution, education, scholarly publication and service to local communities as possible settings for such inquiry. Each must be evaluated with its own evidence. A technical innovation is not beneficial merely because it uses a sacred name; a well-attended conference is not necessarily a good outcome; and a useful enterprise is not thereby a ritual act. The Qur'anic and empirical questions remain distinct even when they speak to each other.
Honest speech, disagreement and the dignity of the reader
The book's ethic of investigation prizes accurate citation, disclosure of uncertainty, full consideration of adverse evidence and fairness toward a critic. The prohibition of false speech can motivate this ethic without turning every opponent into an accused wrongdoer. Qur'anic passages also distinguish admirable argument, ungrounded dispute and efforts to defeat truth with falsehood. The statement about avoiding dispute during hajj (al-Baqarah 197) must be read in its own ritual setting; it is not a warrant to suppress every legitimate scientific objection outside that setting.
The same restraint governs criticism of contemporary pilgrimage management. Costs, access, commercialisation and participant safety are legitimate subjects for evaluation, but strong universal charges require facts about specific practices. Criticising an institution does not prove that the Qur'anic House is imaginary, nor does defending a textual rite immunise its modern administration from scrutiny. Both forms of care require evidence and proportionate judgement.
Part Five — A Practical Model for Daily Work
Calling the model 'knowledge hajj'
The proposed *knowledge hajj* is a deliberately marked educational metaphor, not the designation of an additional Qur'anic rite. It uses relationships from pilgrimage discourse to teach a research cycle: recognise a real need; clarify the purpose; enter the subject through a responsible method; gather material; present a hypothesis; revisit the evidence; challenge the explanation; explain what survives; disclose the result; ask whom it benefits; and return to the question when new information appears.
The cycle is not imposed on the verses as if each named ritual act were secretly a research stage. It is the author's construction, useful insofar as it improves practice and transparent enough for others to criticise. Its success must be assessed on that level. A researcher can do the work in a village, a school, a laboratory or a home, as resources permit. This independence of location belongs to the contemporary model; it does not establish that the Qur'anic pilgrimage has no location.
Step-by-step use of QCROS
Read. Establish the exact wording and its place in the surah, or in a non-Qur'anic project establish an authoritative description of the problem. Gather. Build a corpus rather than a file of favourable illustrations. Compare. Distinguish roots, grammatical constructions, objects, settings and exceptions. Hypothesise. Write a claim clearly enough that it can be tested. Test. Seek the passage, observation or outcome most likely to resist the claim. Judge. Assign a status and describe its limits. Continue. Publish only at the justified level of certainty, then revise the record when stronger evidence appears.
In the expanded research sequence, observations, induction and relationships remain separate stages before hypothesis. This prevents a diagram from making the work seem shorter than it is. A useful visual mnemonic is seven reader actions; the more technical charter is TEXT → OBSERVATION → INDUCTION → RELATIONS → HYPOTHESIS → TEST → STATUS → LAW/MODEL. The diagram is a guide to responsible learning, not a new ordering of rites.
Publication readiness and the six tests
Intellectual readiness is defined in this model through six checks: a question and scope that can be stated precisely; a corpus selected without hiding contrary material; a method with known limitations; an examination of counterexamples; a clear separation of facts, inferences and models; and a statement of what can be communicated without exaggeration. A preliminary hypothesis may be shared before it becomes a settled finding, but it must be declared as such. 'Publish and permit criticism' is preferable to 'announce a law without showing the evidence.'
The role of *tawaf* in this model is return to sources with a changed question, not repetition of the same thesis. The paired images of *safa* and *marwah* signify the filtering of evidence and a conscientious account of findings, while their proposed linguistic identities stay OPEN. The language of announcement invites open publication, and the language of benefits prompts outcome assessment. The sequence remains a scholarly pedagogic tool, not proof that the Qur'an originally describes modern peer review.
Worked example: reducing water loss
Imagine a small research team investigating water wasted in a community system. Its initial observation is that water use appears high. This is not yet a causal claim. The team defines the setting, records flow and use, checks for seasonal variation, and compares several explanations. A proposed solution—repairing particular points, modifying delivery times or improving maintenance—is tested against baseline data and alternative causes. Users affected by the system are invited to report problems and evaluate results.
If the intervention seems to help, the report distinguishes measured improvement from uncertain wider effects. It discloses costs, possible burdens on other users and the duration of observation. The research is then presented in a form others can reuse, revise or contest. The model calls this responsible work a *knowledge hajj* metaphorically because it has purpose, disciplined evidence, review and public benefit. It does not say the work is a substitute for a visit to the House or a Qur'anic rite carried out at a water utility.
Worked example: a disputed interpretation
Consider a reader who proposes that every occurrence of a root refers to 'circulation around a centre'. An initial list of ritual tawaf verses may seem supportive. A broader collection introduces passages about people moving among one another and words for a faction or group. The hypothesis must therefore be narrowed, reformulated to address distinct constructions, or held OPEN. The critic who supplies those examples is not an enemy of insight; that critic has provided a better test of its range.
The same procedure applies to claims that the 'known months' name knowledge announcements, or that al-Safa and al-Marwah designate two academic functions. Such proposals may generate fruitful educational images but cannot become direct Qur'anic definitions without discriminating evidence. An accurate result may be 'a useful model, lexical hypothesis not proven'. That outcome is a successful exercise in reasoning even though it is not a triumphant etymology.
Closing the loop without closing inquiry
The Qur'anic instruction to remember God after the rites in al-Baqarah 200 can inspire the idea that finishing an action should not end ethical responsibility. Similarly, publishing a study should not end its review. The last QCROS action—continue—returns the researcher to the text and to new evidence. A book, dataset or community project can be complete enough to serve others while remaining revisable. The model's central practical demand is not permanent hesitation but truthful disclosure of the degree of knowledge attained.
Part Six — Open Theses, Countertests and Current Conclusions
Three claims that must never be confused
Claim A — The rite has purposes beyond bodily movement. The Qur'anic passages connect the House and actions with remembrance, piety, protection, feeding and benefits. This claim is supported in the specific contexts studied, though actual social outcomes must be investigated rather than assumed. Claim B — A separate educational model may be inspired by these relationships. Such a model can help researchers plan honest, beneficial work without sharing the rite's time and place requirements. This is [و] MODEL, not a rival act of worship. Claim C — Publishing a useful study fulfils the obligation of hajj to the House. This is NOT ESTABLISHED by the examined verses and is resisted by the House, route, arrival, time, place and acts named there.
One may accept A and find B useful without accepting C. The book's refusal to conflate them is a substantive outcome of its own internal review. It is not an attack on those interested in the intellectual benefits of pilgrimage, nor a declaration that only one form of spiritual growth is possible. It is a statement about what the cited Qur'anic evidence does, and does not, support.
The strongest resistant passages
Surah Al Imran 96–97 names the House and a way to it. Surah al-Baqarah 196 records completion, obstruction and offerings; 197 names known months and constraints of conduct; 198–199 locates movement from Arafat and remembrance at the sacred landmark. Surah al-Hajj 26–29 places the announcement, arrivals, benefits, remembrance, offerings and tawaf in a connected discourse. Surah al-Hajj 36–37 joins feeding and piety to the material act. Surah al-Ma'idah 97 sets the House alongside time and offerings. These passages resist an interpretation that replaces the entire framework with academic publication.
The resisting test is not a refusal to imagine. It asks a proposed definition to account for inconvenient details on equal terms with attractive ones. An interpretation that works only when the verses about the House, known times and offerings are left unread is weaker than one that can explain why they remain part of the described practice. The distinction between the text and a contemporary analogy allows an ethically serious modern use without forcing either category into the other.
Open lexical and structural questions
Some root-wide questions are unresolved: the precise semantic commonality of ḥ-j-j across pilgrimage, argument and years; the relation between tawaf and ta'ifah; whether proposed letter-pair dynamics have stable predictive value; and whether educational functions proposed for al-Safa and al-Marwah correspond to their historical or lexical identities. The question of months and announcements is also OPEN as a proposed parallel, but an erasure of temporal meaning is resisted in the relevant pilgrimage passages. A question about the exact range of the ban on dispute in al-Baqarah 197 likewise requires contextual precision.
The Arabic edition provides further inventories—h-j-j counted as 33 forms in 26 verses, t-w-f as 41 forms in 35 verses, and separate registers for time and 'known'—as aids to crosschecking. These figures belong to the source's morphological classification and are not offered here as independently rerun counts or as proof of a universal law. The complete datasets and variant definitions must be consulted in the Arabic volume and its audit records when numerical replication is important.
Historical and institutional hypotheses
A claim that hajj was originally a university, an agricultural fair, or a specialist innovation congress is not established by a verse that mentions benefits or livestock. A historical institution requires independent historical evidence. Similarly, criticism of modern pilgrimage fees and organisation requires specific contemporary evidence rather than categorical condemnation. A linguistic theory, a description of past practice and an empirical evaluation of a present institution use different kinds of proof. The book asks that these forms of inquiry be kept in conversation but not silently combined.
The closing judgement
The study arrives at a deliberately bounded conclusion. The Qur'anic corpus examined presents pilgrimage to a specified House, associated times, arrivals and acts; it also presents relations of remembrance, piety, benefit, feeding and safety. From those relationships the author has developed a potentially useful framework for scientific work and public responsibility. That framework can be taught, criticised and measured in its own right. It is not established as the sole meaning of the rite, and no presented test demonstrates that it discharges the duty of hajj.
The broader research on roots, letter-meanings and Mathani relationships is not therefore discarded. It is placed at its warranted level—generative for questions, vulnerable to counterevidence, and open where a complete demonstration is absent. The reader is invited to carry forward not a final slogan, but a disciplined habit: identify the text, make the inference visible, preserve the counterexample, state the limit, and leave room for a better account.
Conclusion — From Rite to Responsible Knowledge Without Substitution
The movement of this book is from a specific Qur'anic rite to a wider ethical question, and back to the limits of the text. It holds that spiritual purpose should not be severed from concern for the lives of others; that a social or scientific application must never hide the original setting from which it draws inspiration; and that integrity in public knowledge requires more than an uplifting analogy. A direct textual description, a contextual inference and a practical model can enrich one another precisely because they are not the same kind of statement.
Readers may disagree with a lexical proposal or extend an ethical application in directions the author has not imagined. The appropriate reply is neither to forbid inquiry nor to offer every conjecture the status of revelation. It is to examine grammar, setting, the larger Qur'anic network and the strongest contrary evidence, and to say what can be established now. The author invites the reader to use this method in research, teaching, environmental work, community action and personal reflection, with humility toward what remains unknown.

Appendix B — Selected Qur'anic Reference Map
This is a navigation map of passages repeatedly discussed, not a declaration that every verse in a range proves the same conclusion. Some verses are direct evidence, some form contextual limits, and others resist an overbroad claim. References follow the surah names used in the Arabic edition rather than replacing those names with numbers alone.
• Surah Al Imran 96–97: the House, place and capacity to reach it; central resistant test of replacement theories.
• Surah al-Baqarah 125–129: the House as a place of return and security, foundations, prayer and teaching in their distinct settings.
• Surah al-Baqarah 158: al-Safa and al-Marwah as rites and the movement associated with them.
• Surah al-Baqarah 189, 196–200: crescents, timing, completion, conduct, Arafat, remembrance and the end of the rites.
• Surah al-Hajj 26–30: the House, announcement, arrivals, benefits, remembrance, offerings and honest speech.
• Surah al-Hajj 32, 36–37: honouring signs, livestock, feeding and piety.
• Surah al-Ma'idah 97: the House as support for people with sacred time and offerings.
• Surah al-Tawbah 3, 36, 122: public announcement, months and a party that seeks understanding—three distinct contexts.
• Surah al-Qasas 27, al-An'am 83, al-Shura 16: years, a given argument and an invalid argument—limits on a single ḥ-j-j definition.
• Surah al-Nur 58 and al-Rahman 44: usages that resist reducing all tawaf forms to one spatial circle.
• Surah Sad 29, al-Nisa 82, al-Zumar 23 and Al Imran 7: contemplation and the methodological limits of proposed structural readings.
