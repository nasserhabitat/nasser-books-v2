II. Translator’s Note
The Bridge of Meaning
This English edition is a condensed conceptual adaptation. It is not a word-for-word translation, but an extraction of the book’s central argument. It presents the philosophical and methodological framework in accessible English while abridging extended linguistic discussions, repeated formulations, and overlapping diagrams.
For the academic researcher, the original Arabic version remains the primary source for detailed linguistic analysis, the full interpretive argument, and its references. This adaptation follows the Arabic reader’s edition that includes the author’s acknowledgement of the video series that prompted the book.
This adaptation preserves the methodological status of the Arabic edition. A claim marked OPEN remains open; a CANDIDATE remains a candidate; a RESISTED or STRONGLY RESISTED claim is not revived through simplification; and a CLOSED result remains closed only within its declared scope. These are rules of fidelity, not new classifications imposed on every argument. The source also uses SUPPORTED, SUPPORTED IN PART, and INTEGRATED. Here, support means support from the evidence presented, not immunity from further examination.
Key Qur’anic terms are retained in transliteration where a single English equivalent would prematurely narrow the inquiry. The principal terms in this book are kitāb, qurʾān, dhikr, furqān, and mathānī. Their working English glosses are explained in context. They are not offered as interchangeable dictionary equivalents.
The five main parts follow the Arabic book’s sequence. Closely related subsections are condensed together; the final decision register preserves the identifiers R-01 through R-14. The Arabic edition’s diagrams and tables are represented through prose and the register rather than reproduced in full.
Qur’anic references use chapter and verse numbers. English descriptions of verses are contextual renderings or paraphrases prepared for this edition, not quotations from a named published translation. The distinction between the Qur’anic wording, an account of its context, and the author’s inference remains essential throughout.

To the Reader
I wrote this book as a scholarly response to the video series referred to in my Arabic manuscript as “Faʾtū bi-ʿashri suwarin mithlihi,” or “Bring Ten Surahs Like It.” Its reflections on kitāb, qurʾān, dhikr, furqān, and the organization of the muṣḥaf prompted the inquiry developed here.
I thank those responsible for the series for their efforts in tadabbur and for the questions and observations they brought forward. Their work opened a valuable space for investigation. Where I agree, I explain the basis of that agreement. Where I disagree, I present my reasons and the textual evidence that resists the proposed conclusion. The disagreement belongs within a shared inquiry governed by evidence.
The central issue is whether differences among the names and functions of revelation require us to divide the muṣḥaf into separate textual entities. I argue that functional differentiation can illuminate the unity of revelation. A real difference in function need not establish a separate corpus.
I offer this book’s interpretive explanations, linguistic analyses, and architectural models as human judgments. The Qur’an is the governing source; my understanding of it is not infallible. Selecting connections between passages and drawing conclusions from them are acts of inquiry for which I remain responsible.
I invite you to read with your own reason and insight. Examine a passage in its context, test the movement from evidence to conclusion, and seek material that may qualify or challenge the interpretation. Agreement with the author is not the measure of a successful reading. A better explanation, a missed witness, or a stronger objection may carry the research further.
This book is a stage in an open course of study. Its diagrams do not replace the text, its terminology does not settle a question by naming it, and its decision labels do not release any claim from scrutiny. What I hope to contribute is material that can be examined, corrected, and developed by readers who take responsibility for their own judgment.
The Methodological Framework
The approach called Fiqh al-Lisān al-Qurʾānī studies the functional precision of Qur’anic language through context and relationships across the text. It resists treating distinct expressions as freely interchangeable. Yet the refusal of functional synonymy does not require the separation of their referent: several terms may concern one revelation while disclosing different dimensions of it.
The principle of balance, or mīzān, requires a proposed explanation to remain answerable to the evidence. An attractive pattern does not outweigh a passage that contradicts its application. Contextual and network inquiry therefore work together: the local passage establishes its situation, while related passages test the reach of a proposed meaning.
The seven QCROS operations
Text [ن]. Identify the Qur’anic wording and its immediate setting. The text must be distinguishable from a paraphrase of it.
Internal exposition [ب]. Explain the relations proposed among passages. This is the researcher’s account of those relations, not an additional revealed statement.
Corpus inquiry [ا]. Examine the occurrences of the relevant root, form, or construction. Selected illustrations alone do not demonstrate exhaustive coverage.
Hypothesis [ف]. State the proposed explanation before presenting it as a result.
Resistance and falsification testing [خ]. Seek the passages that most strongly challenge the hypothesis. Test whether substituting a proposed meaning preserves the passage’s distinctions, and examine contrary contexts.
Operational or compositional model [و]. Formulate a usable account of how the relations may work. A model is an intellectual aid whose scope must be stated.
Judgment and scope [ح]. Report what the examination supports, resists, integrates, or leaves as a candidate. A finding is not stronger than the procedure and evidence behind it.
This order makes disagreement productive. It allows a textual observation to be retained even when the theory built upon it fails. It also allows a wider model to incorporate a useful local pattern without presenting that pattern as an exclusive definition of revelation.
Part One
Compositional Architecture
Al-Fātiḥah and al-Baqarah
The first part proposes that the received arrangement of the muṣḥaf can be read as a network of thematic and functional relationships. I call this inquiry “compositional inimitability”: the study of coherence in the arrangement and distribution of the discourse. The proposed map is a human interpretation of that coherence.
Al-Fātiḥah provides the opening orientation. It introduces the source of the address, mercy, accountability, worship, dependence on divine help, and the request for guidance. Its movement culminates in the question of a path to be followed. In the book’s architectural model, the rest of the muṣḥaf develops the response to that opening request. “Master code” and “opening platform” are explanatory metaphors for this role.
Al-Baqarah is then read as a comprehensive thematic index. Its opening presents the kitāb and guidance, and distinguishes responses to the address. Its subsequent movement brings together Adam, the Children of Israel, Abraham, the formation of a community, worship, family relations, economic conduct, and conflict. The proposal is that these subjects establish foundational lines elaborated elsewhere.
I connect this arrangement with the relation between consolidation and elaboration expressed in 11:1. The verse speaks of a book whose verses are made firm and then set out in detail. Reading al-Baqarah as the trunk of a branching network is my compositional application of that relation; the verse does not itself name al-Baqarah as a table of contents.
Examples illustrate the proposal. Themes involving Adam and the Children of Israel are developed in al-Aʿrāf, Ṭā Hā, and al-Isrāʾ. Legal and social concerns meet further elaboration in al-Nisāʾ, al-Māʾidah, al-Nūr, and al-Ṭalāq. Scenes of accountability recur across the shorter surahs.
The interpretive value of this model lies in helping the reader connect a passage to a wider inquiry. Its usefulness does not establish that every surah has only one function or that every relation has already been mapped. Those stronger claims would require their own evidence.
Major Systems and Networks of Expression
The long surahs are treated as a foundational field in which major dimensions of communal life are developed. The full Arabic study associates Āl ʿImrān with political responsibility and conflict; al-Nisāʾ with social rights and the protection of vulnerable people; al-Māʾidah with covenants, lawful conduct, and economic relations; al-Anʿām with the critique of false religious authority; and al-Aʿrāf with prophetic address and inherited convention. Al-Anfāl and al-Tawbah are read together in relation to conflict, security, covenants, and international relations.
These are organizing emphases within my model. They help explain differentiated attention within one scripture. They should not be mistaken for exhaustive descriptions that remove other subjects from the same surahs.
The book also reads Qur’anic narratives as models of recurring moral and social relations. Pharaoh, Hāmān, and Qārūn are considered through the relations among domination, administrative power, and wealth. The purpose is to understand patterns of conduct rather than confine the narratives to an inaccessible past.
A methodological guard is essential here. A recurring pattern may help illuminate present conditions, but it does not authorize a reader to identify a particular modern person, weapon, or political event as the exclusive referent of a verse. The text’s wider address cannot be reduced to a contingent application.
The example of the heavens and angelic intercession
The book connects the language of rupture and creation across 67:3, 19:90, 42:5, and 82:1. The heavens are considered as an ordered creation, as almost rupturing in passages concerning grave religious claims, and as undergoing rupture in the final horizon. The shared root provides a route of comparison; its local uses still require separate contextual examination.
Another comparison places the angels’ prayer for believers in 40:7 beside their seeking forgiveness for those on earth in 42:5. I read the latter in relation to mercy, warning, and the opportunity for return. This is a network interpretation of the passages. The repeated wording supplies evidence for comparison, while the explanation of their relation remains an interpretive judgment.
Part Two
Examining the Ten-Surah Thesis
The series discussed in the Arabic book identifies ten surahs: al-Aʿrāf, al-Raʿd, Maryam, Ṭā Hā, al-Naml, Yā Sīn, Ṣād, al-Shūrā, Qāf, and al-Qalam. As represented in the manuscript, the thesis links their opening letters and patterns of address to a claim that these ten alone constitute “the Qur’an,” while other portions of the muṣḥaf belong to a different category.
The response distinguishes three operations: noticing a pattern, proposing an explanation for it, and restricting the name qurʾān to a numerical set. The third operation does not follow merely from the first two. A surah’s distinctive emphasis may identify its role within a whole rather than establish a separate textual identity.
The initial-letter rule also faces internal pressure. Al-Shūrā begins with ḥā mīm, a sequence shared with other surahs, followed by ʿayn sīn qāf. Treating this configuration as a special exception requires an account of why the original criterion is still reliable. The book regards the exception as a difficulty for the proposed restriction.
More substantially, passages outside the restricted set use the language of qurʾān. Yūsuf 12:1–3 connects the clear book with an Arabic qurʾān. Fuṣṣilat 41:3 speaks of a book whose verses are detailed as an Arabic qurʾān. Al-Zukhruf 43:1–3 likewise joins the language of book and qurʾān. These passages resist a sharp division into two separate corpora.
The definite form al-qurʾān and indefinite expressions such as qurʾānan ʿarabiyyan do not, in my analysis, establish different physical collections. Al-Shūrā 42:7 itself uses the indefinite expression while belonging to the series’ preferred ten.
The resulting judgment is twofold. The numerical restriction is STRONGLY RESISTED. Observations about narrative, warning, patterns of address, and surah composition can nevertheless be INTEGRATED into a model of differentiated functions within one muṣḥaf. Rejecting the restriction need not require discarding every observation that motivated it.
Al-Shūrā as a Study of Revelation
The book gives special attention to al-Shūrā. Its distinctive composition is retained as a valuable object of study even though it is not accepted as evidence for isolating the ten surahs.
The opening describes revelation to the present messenger and to those before him (42:3). Verse 7 speaks of an Arabic qurʾān and its warning. Verse 13 links the present revelation to the religious responsibility entrusted to earlier messengers. Verses 51–52 return to divine communication and the giving of a spirit from God’s command. I read these passages as a compositional sequence concerning source, communication, reception, and responsibility.
The expression kadhālika, “thus” or “in this manner,” is important to this reading. The book relates its occurrences in the surah to continuity in revelation and to what has been realized in the messenger’s own address. This connection is offered as an internal architectural interpretation.
Modes of communication and the receiving heart
Verse 42:51 distinguishes communication by waḥy, from behind a veil, and through a sent messenger who communicates by permission. The Arabic discussion compares these modes with passages concerning Moses, Mary, and the reception of revelation. Its distinctions should not be flattened into one undifferentiated mechanism.
The receiving heart is explicit in 2:97 and 26:193–194. The book brings 42:24, with its reference to sealing the heart in a passage addressing the accusation of fabrication, into this network. The stronger contextual claims about what the sealing implies are my inferences; they should be distinguished from the direct statements about revelation reaching the heart.
The divine names framing the surah are also compared. The opening brings together might, wisdom, exaltedness, and greatness; the account of communication in 42:51 concludes with exaltedness and wisdom. I see a compositional correspondence between the height of the source and the wisdom of its communication.
This study is INTEGRATED as a reading of al-Shūrā within the wider muṣḥaf. It does not reinstate the rejected numerical partition. The structural observation and the exclusivist conclusion remain separate questions.
Challenge Passages and the Audiences of Guidance
The Qur’anic challenge appears in several forms. The book compares a discourse like it (52:33–34), the like of this qurʾān (17:88), ten fabricated surahs like it (11:13), a surah like it (10:38), and a surah of its like (2:23). It reads these variations in relation to the claims and situations being answered.
In Yūnus, the mention of “this qurʾān” in 10:37 immediately precedes the challenge in 10:38. I treat that local reference as decisive resistance to redirecting the challenge toward a specially selected surah merely to preserve a numerical theory. The number in one challenge does not, by itself, state how many surahs the qurʾān contains.
The question of guidance is equally important. Verse 28:49 invites the production of a book from God that offers better guidance. I therefore resist reducing the challenge to verbal elegance alone. Narrative truthfulness, guidance, and the capacity of the address to orient judgment belong within the inquiry.
The invitation to call on helpers is read alongside the accusation that other people assisted in fabrication (25:4–5). Within this interpretation, the challenge responds to the allegation on its own terms. This connection is accepted in part, without accepting the series’ numerical conclusion.
Humanity and the God-conscious
Al-Baqarah describes the kitāb as guidance for the God-conscious (2:2), and the qurʾān as guidance for humanity (2:185). Āl ʿImrān 3:138 combines a clarification for people with guidance and admonition for the God-conscious. I read these differences as distinctions of audience and function within one revelation.
Public warning addresses people before they have accepted a covenant of conduct. Teaching develops understanding and responsibility among those who respond. The fasting passage places obligation and universal guidance close together (2:183–185), illustrating their relationship rather than establishing two scriptures.
The distinction is retained as an operational interpretation. It does not mean that guidance for one audience is irrelevant to another, or that textual functions must occupy mutually exclusive physical locations.
Part Three
Letter Pairs and Semantic Movement
MATHANI-NET proposes a way of investigating the internal relations of letters within roots. It treats overlapping letter pairs as possible indicators of a movement in meaning. In this account, a word is approached through the interaction of its components rather than explained only by substitution with a near-synonym.
This is my exploratory framework. Its diagrams use expressions such as direction, movement, and energy to describe semantic relations. These expressions belong to the proposed linguistic model; they do not make its findings independent of contextual and corpus testing.
The four principal terms
Kitāb. The analysis of k-t-b links the pair k-t with gathering and containment, and t-b with stability and continuing reference. The resulting functional proposal is an ordered and binding reference that gathers and preserves its contents.
Qurʾān. The analysis relates q-r to gathering or settling, and connects the remaining elements with manifestation and present address. My functional emphasis is the living act of recitation: revelation gathered and communicated to the hearer in the present.
Dhikr. The pair dh-k is associated with penetration and cognitive awakening, while k-r is associated with return and renewed recollection. The proposed function is remembrance that repeatedly brings neglected truth into awareness.
Furqān. The analysis associates f-r with separation and r-q with fine differentiation, together with my treatment of realization in the present. The proposed function is discriminating insight that separates truth from confusion and becomes effective in judgment.
The condensed presentation preserves the direction of the Arabic argument without reproducing every letter-level discussion. These analyses must be read together with the functional and contextual studies in Part Four. A plausible letter-pair account does not by itself demonstrate that every occurrence of a word bears exactly the same restricted application.
The central methodological condition is that the proposed movement of meaning must survive encounters with the actual passages. A linguistic model may generate a hypothesis. It cannot exempt that hypothesis from the resistant evidence that QCROS requires it to confront.
The Meaning of ʿArabī and the Scope of the Model
The full Arabic study reads the description ʿarabī through a network of passages concerning clarity, detail, understanding, and freedom from crookedness. Its main witnesses include 39:28, 41:3 and 44, 16:103, and 42:7.
Within MATHANI-NET, the pair ʿ-r is associated with disclosure and emergence, and r-b with ordered connection and development. I use this proposed relation to argue for a functional dimension in ʿarabī: discourse whose meaning is articulated and whose relations can be made clear.
The comparison with aʿjamī is central to this account. I see the contrast as concerning the accessibility and articulation of the address, and argue against reducing the description to ethnic belonging. The Arabic edition marks this interpretation as SUPPORTED. In this adaptation, that label records my judgment on the witnesses presented; it does not turn the letter-pair analysis into an independently demonstrated linguistic consensus.
Four functions within one revelation
The terms can now be read together. Kitāb emphasizes ordered reference and obligation. Qurʾān emphasizes communicated address. Dhikr emphasizes the recovery and continued presence of meaning in awareness. Furqān emphasizes differentiation and its consequences in thought and conduct.
The book’s governing distinction is between functional difference and separation of the referent. These terms need not be interchangeable to concern the same revelation. Conversely, the fact that they concern one revelation does not require their functions to be identical.
The operational sequence developed in the following part is a model of reception. It connects reference, communication, remembrance, and discernment. It is not a claim that the muṣḥaf consists of four separately bounded collections, nor that the reader may override a passage’s context whenever it does not fit the diagram.
For an English-speaking reader, transliteration is therefore useful. “Book,” “recitation,” “remembrance,” and “criterion” can orient the discussion, but each English word captures only part of my proposed field. Keeping the Arabic terms visible allows the argument to remain open to the full linguistic evidence in the source language.
Part Four
From Reference to Living Address
The operational cycle asks how revelation becomes effective in the consciousness and conduct of a recipient. It connects kitāb, qurʾān, dhikr, and furqān without dividing revelation into separate textual objects.
Kitāb is first examined through ordered reference, obligation, and accountability. The root k-t-b appears in prescriptions such as fasting, retaliation, and fighting. The term also belongs to passages concerning records and the disclosure of deeds, including 18:49. I bring the book of responsibility and the record of accountability into a common inquiry about what is preserved, made binding, and brought to judgment.
The distinction between recitation and teaching develops this account. Verses 2:129 and 151, 3:164, and 62:2 bring together recitation, purification, and teaching the book and wisdom. The revised Arabic edition does not treat their order as one necessary chronological sequence: purification follows teaching in 2:129, whereas other passages order them differently.
Nor can teaching be categorically excluded from qurʾān, since 55:2 speaks of teaching the Qur’an. The distinction proposed here concerns functions and contexts, with attention to differences of agent and audience. It is not a universal prohibition on combining the ideas of teaching and qurʾān.
Recitation and the relation of sound to writing
The book reads qurʾān as an address that is recited and heard. Verse 17:106 connects its articulation with recitation to people over time; 7:204 calls for listening and attention when it is recited. Writing preserves the address, while recitation makes its communication present to the listener.
The comparison between the opening letters of al-Baqarah and the expression alam in al-Fīl and al-Sharḥ illustrates the importance of received pronunciation. I argue that written form and sounding are complementary dimensions of transmission rather than grounds for separating qurʾān from kitāb.
Moses’ instruction to listen in 20:13 is retained as a witness from his own context. It must not be presented as an instruction originally addressed to Muhammad. Connecting prophetic reception across passages requires preserving the identity and situation of each addressee.
Hearing Warning and the Responsibility to Communicate
The source compares the precedence of hearing over sight in passages such as 16:78, 23:78, 67:23, and 76:2 with the order of seeing and hearing in the scene of 32:12. In that latter passage, the guilty speak from the position of having witnessed what they had failed to heed.
The revised Arabic edition treats this as a contextual CANDIDATE, not a universal law inferred from word order alone. The expression in 18:26 belongs to the account of God’s knowledge of the Companions of the Cave and of the unseen; it is not another description of the guilty seeing first on the Last Day. The contrast must therefore be argued within its actual settings.
Two horizons of warning
Al-Shūrā 42:7 distinguishes warning the Mother of Cities and those around it from warning of the Day of Gathering. I read this as a distinction between a historically situated field of address and the continuing horizon of final accountability.
The Arabic discussion further connects the first horizon with the presence of a messenger and the conditions of collective punishment, drawing on 28:59. Its claim that this particular warning belongs to the direct prophetic situation is an interpretive conclusion, not the wording of 42:7 itself. Its practical implication in the book is that later readers cannot appoint themselves agents authorized to pronounce divine destruction on contemporary populations.
Neglect opposition and perseverance
The book interprets the abandonment of the qurʾān in 25:30 functionally: the words may remain present while their guidance is disregarded. It relates this to the effort to drown out the recitation in 41:26. The inquiry concerns the obstruction of attentive reception and the loss of the address’s effect.
Communication also imposes a burden on the messenger. The discussion gathers 11:12, 5:67, 17:73, 68:48–50, and 15:97–99 around fidelity, pressure, patience, and the example of the companion of the fish. These passages support the book’s account of persevering in communication without claiming control over the hearer’s response.
The obligation to convey and the freedom of the recipient remain distinct. Fidelity to the message does not confer ownership of another person’s conscience.
Dhikr and Furqān
Dhikr operates in the book on two connected levels. It is the revealed remembrance received from beyond the individual, and it is the awakened awareness formed through reflection. The first is discussed through 15:9; the second through passages such as 38:29, which connects the sending of a blessed book with reflection and remembrance.
The Arabic text also brings 56:77–79 into its study of inward reception and the guarded book. The cognitive account developed from that comparison is an interpretation. It should not be confused with a direct definition supplied by those verses.
The relation between dhikr and tadhkirah gives the model a temporal dimension. I emphasize recollection of earlier peoples and their outcomes on one side, and preparedness for what lies ahead on the other. Examples include 21:24, 20:3, 69:12, 73:19, and 74:54. The source supports this temporal reading; the condensed edition retains it as my account of the linked witnesses rather than an unrestricted replacement meaning for every use of either term.
Forgetting is then considered as a failure of orientation. Prolonged enjoyment, inherited comfort, and companions who divert a person from remembrance appear in the discussion of 25:18 and 29, 6:44, and 43:36. The lapse of Adam in 20:115 is distinguished from deliberate turning away in 20:124. Recollection and return remain possible, while persistent disregard carries a different moral trajectory.
The counter-movement is reverence before direct sight: the responsiveness described in 36:11 and 50:33, and the recovery of awareness in 7:201. The point is not simply retaining information. Dhikr becomes effective when remembered meaning changes what a person notices, values, and does.
Furqān represents discernment in this operational cycle. Verse 8:29 links it with God-consciousness, while 8:41 refers to a day of decisive separation. I relate inward discrimination to its historical and practical expression.
Reference becomes address; address can awaken remembrance; remembrance can inform discernment. The sequence is offered as an operational model whose value is tested by understanding and conduct. It does not convert my reconstruction into a divinely stated four-stage formula.
Part Five
The Seven Mathānī
The full Arabic study rejects the restriction of the “seven of the mathānī” in 15:87 to al-Shuʿarāʾ alone, and rejects using that restriction to separate the surah from the qurʾān. At the same time, it retains the observation that repeated narrative units and refrains in al-Shuʿarāʾ deserve compositional study.
The governing comparison is with 39:23, where the revealed book is described with the language of mutual resemblance and mathānī. I argue that a wider description of the book resists treating one local pattern as the entire scope of the concept.
At the linguistic level, MATHANI-NET connects th-n with folding or turning one side toward another, and n-y with an inward movement. At the compositional level, I study correspondences among passages and surahs. These two levels form the book’s proposed account of paired relations that disclose meanings not visible in an isolated element.
Al-Fātiḥah is treated as an opening laboratory of this structure. The longer surahs are also considered through paired relations, including al-Baqarah with Āl ʿImrān, al-Nisāʾ with al-Māʾidah, al-Anʿām with al-Aʿrāf, and al-Anfāl with al-Tawbah as a concluding combined unit. These arrangements are part of my architectural model.
In 15:87, the discussion of min and the conjunction between the seven mathānī and the great qurʾān permits, in my reading, a relation of particular and whole or of connected descriptions. The mere presence of a conjunction does not establish two mutually exclusive physical collections. Other coordinated descriptions, including 27:1 and 21:48, are brought into the comparison.
The reference to another descent in 53:13–18 is not accepted as evidence that two separate scriptures were delivered on two occasions. The passage’s account of seeing does not state the proposed division of the muṣḥaf.
The judgment is consequently precise: the exclusive restriction and the two-corpus inference are STRONGLY RESISTED; the repeated pattern in al-Shuʿarāʾ is retained as a subsidiary compositional model. Rejection of exclusivity does not erase the local observation.
Qaṣaṣ Ḥadīth and the Horizon of Events
The book distinguishes qaṣaṣ from ḥadīth by examining how earlier lives and events are presented. The physical following of a trace in 18:64 and 28:11 helps orient its account of qaṣaṣ: following a course step by step. I read narrative accounts of messengers and their communities through this movement of tracing a path.
In contrast, the discussion of ḥadīth emphasizes the event that has occurred and become an account to be told. Passages in which peoples become aḥādīth after their destruction, including 34:19 and 23:44, are central to this proposal.
The Arabic analysis brings together the formula asking whether an account has reached the hearer in passages concerning Moses (20:9; 79:15), Abraham’s guests (51:24), the hosts associated with Pharaoh and Thamūd (85:17–18), and al-Ghāshiyah (88:1). It contrasts this with the wording used for the disputants in the account of David (38:21).
A resistant passage and the proposed response
Hūd 11:100 is important because the verb associated with qaṣaṣ is used of towns, some remaining and others cut down. This challenges a simple division in which qaṣaṣ would concern only survivors while ḥadīth would concern only the destroyed.
My response distinguishes the way an account is narrated from its subject matter. The verb can describe tracing the sequence of a community’s relation to its messenger, even when that sequence ends in destruction. The narrative method and the final event need not be competing names for the same function.
The source marks the distinction as SUPPORTED. The adaptation preserves both the support judgment and the resistant example with the response given to it. Omitting that example would make the condensed claim appear easier to establish than the Arabic discussion allows.
The treatment of al-Ghāshiyah is an INTEGRATED MODEL. It connects overwhelming events within history to the final horizon of return and accounting in 88:25–26. This wider application does not remove the final accountability explicitly present in the surah, and it does not license an exclusive identification with a contemporary event.
Knowledge Disclosure and the Limits of Guardianship
The expression ahl al-dhikr, “the people of remembrance,” is examined in 16:43 and 21:7. The immediate issue is the humanity of earlier messengers. I read the instruction to ask those who know as an appeal to informed testimony about that history, not as the institution of an exclusive class entitled to control all understanding.
Related witnesses include 13:43 and 26:197. The distinction matters: consulting knowledge can be necessary without transferring responsibility for judgment to an unaccountable authority.
The discussion of tabyīn emphasizes making known and disclosing what has been concealed. It compares the messenger’s responsibility in 16:44 and 64 with the covenant to make the book known rather than conceal it in 3:187. The book’s emphasis on disclosure is an interpretive account of this network; it should remain distinguishable from the wording of each individual passage.
Three mechanisms of concealment are examined. Truth can be mixed with falsehood (2:42; 3:71). A text can be selectively displayed while much is hidden (6:91). Human writing or distorted recitation can be presented as divine material (2:79; 3:78). Together these passages support my critique of controlling access to knowledge through substitution and selective presentation.
The believer from Pharaoh’s household in 40:28 provides a contrasting situation: concealing faith under danger is not identical to withholding testimony for advantage. Similar words must be examined within different agents, purposes, and circumstances.
Conveying without owning the response
Al-Shūrā 42:6 and 48 distinguishes God’s guardianship from the messenger’s duty to convey. The book connects these passages with 6:107 and related statements limiting human control over belief. It develops an ethic of communication through evidence without coercive ownership of conscience.
Finally, accusations of madness are examined as a way of discrediting an unwelcome message. I connect independent reflection in pairs or alone in 34:46 with freedom from the pressure of collective repetition. This becomes an invitation to consider the evidence deliberately, rather than accept or reject an argument because of the group that voices it.
Continuing the Inquiry
The book’s principal conclusion is that functional differentiation need not fragment revelation. Kitāb, qurʾān, dhikr, and furqān may name distinct dimensions of reference, communication, remembrance, and discernment within one revealed address.
The architectural model seeks to relate the opening orientation of al-Fātiḥah, the foundational range of al-Baqarah, the thematic development of the long surahs, and the networks of narrative and repeated expression across the muṣḥaf. The rejection of the ten-surah restriction does not itself prove every feature of this alternative model. Each proposed relationship remains accountable to its own evidence.
The same discipline governs the letter-pair inquiry. MATHANI-NET supplies a proposed means of exploring semantic relations. It must remain in conversation with actual usage. If a model can survive only by ignoring a passage, the passage has exposed a limit in the model.
The practical aim is a more responsible reception of revelation. A reader attends to the wording, compares its contexts, recognizes the human work involved in interpretation, and allows remembered meaning to inform discernment and action. This work depends on reason and insight rather than submission to my terminology.
I therefore return to the invitation with which the book began. Read the evidence, examine what resists the proposed explanation, and consider whether the response adequately answers that resistance. Where a different reading explains the material more fully, the inquiry should benefit from it. Where the evidence remains insufficient, the proper task is to continue investigating.
My thanks to the contributors whose reflections prompted this response is part of that commitment. Their work offered questions worth examining. This book contributes its own observations and objections to the same field of study, without claiming the final word.
The materials that follow are tools for further reading. The register separates the main decisions and their scope. The glossary preserves my terminology without replacing the Arabic evidence. The reading route identifies passages and sections to revisit. Together they support continued inquiry; none is a substitute for it.

R-01 to R-07
The labels below record the judgments in the Arabic edition. SUPPORTED means supported by the witnesses presented; INTEGRATED identifies an idea’s place in the model. Neither label implies an exhaustive independent verification by this adaptation.
R-01 — Restricting the Qur’an to ten surahs. STRONGLY RESISTED as an exclusive numerical claim; INTEGRATED as a set of observations concerning warning and remembrance. Principal witnesses: 2:185; 10:37–38; 12:1–3; 17:9, 106; 43:1–3.
R-02 — Challenge passages and better guidance. SUPPORTED IN PART. The author retains guidance as a central criterion and the contextual relation between accusations and challenge, while rejecting the inference that ten challenged surahs define the entire corpus. Witnesses: 28:49; 52:34; 17:88; 11:13; 10:38; 2:23.
R-03 — Humanity and the God-conscious. INTEGRATED as an operational distinction within one revelation. The difference between recitation and teaching does not establish two scriptures. The revised discussion also considers 55:2 and the differing order in 2:129. Other witnesses: 2:2, 151, 185; 3:138, 164; 27:76.
R-04 — Dhikr and tadhkirah. SUPPORTED in the source’s temporal interpretation linking remembered experience with preparedness for the future. The claim is retained within the discussed witnesses. Principal references: 21:2, 24; 15:9; 20:3; 73:19; 74:54; 69:12.
R-05 — Qaṣaṣ and ḥadīth. SUPPORTED in the source’s distinction between tracing a course and the event that becomes an account. The resistance supplied by 11:100 and the author’s method-versus-content response must remain visible. Other witnesses: 28:25; 12:3; 34:19; 38:21.
R-06 — Al-Ghāshiyah and shared expressions. INTEGRATED MODEL connecting historical overwhelming events with the final accounting. It does not erase the latter or fix an exclusive contemporary application. Witnesses: 88:1–26; 7:41; 10:27; 14:50.
R-07 — Forgetting and reverence before sight. SUPPORTED as a reading of the relation among prolonged enjoyment, diversion, forgetting, and renewed awareness. Witnesses: 25:18, 29; 6:44; 20:115, 124; 36:11; 7:201.

R-08 — The burden of communication. SUPPORTED in the source’s account of fidelity and perseverance under pressure. The example of the companion of the fish is read within this argument. Principal witnesses: 11:12; 17:73; 68:48–50; 15:97–99; 5:67.
R-09 — The seven mathānī and two descents. STRONGLY RESISTED in the exclusive identification with al-Shuʿarāʾ and the inference of two separately delivered corpora. The repeated narrative pattern is retained as a SUB-MODEL. Witnesses: 15:87; 39:23; 26:1–227; 53:13–18.
R-10 — People of remembrance and disclosure. SUPPORTED in the source’s reading of informed historical testimony and the critique of concealment. The functional explanation is distinct from the wording of the passages. Witnesses: 16:43–44, 89; 13:43; 2:42, 79, 159; 6:91; 40:28.
R-11 — Accusations of madness and independent reflection. SUPPORTED in the source’s analysis of discrediting the messenger and the invitation to deliberate outside group pressure. Witnesses: 15:6; 68:1–4; 44:14; 34:46.
R-12 — The composition of al-Shūrā. INTEGRATED as a study of revelation, the receiving heart, recurrent expressions, and the relation of opening and conclusion. It supplies no restored basis for the ten-surah restriction. Witnesses: 42:1–4, 7, 13, 24, 51–52; 4:163–164; 13:30.
R-13 — The heavens angelic prayer and guardianship. SUPPORTED / SYSTEMIC MODEL in the source. The comparison of shared expressions supports inquiry into their relationships, while its explanation remains a model. Witnesses: 42:5–6, 48; 67:3; 19:90; 40:7; 34:40–41; 6:107.
R-14 — Hearing writing and related inferences. The revised Arabic edition treats the hearing-and-sight sequence as a CANDIDATE contextual reading. Its other linguistic and warning-related inferences are to be considered separately rather than absorbed into a single blanket approval. Witnesses include 2:1; 105:1; 29:48; 32:12; 41:3, 26, 44; 42:7–8. The corrected discussion excludes 18:26 as a scene of the guilty on the Last Day.
Working Glossary
Fiqh al-Lisān al-Qurʾānī. The author’s project of disciplined inquiry into the functional precision of Qur’anic language through its own contexts and networks. The term is retained to distinguish the project’s usage from other established fields.
Muṣḥaf. The collected, ordered textual form of the Qur’an considered in the book’s compositional inquiry.
Tadabbur. Sustained reflection on the text and its relations. In this book it involves human reasoning whose conclusions remain open to examination.
Kitāb. Ordered and preserved reference, with particular attention to binding responsibility and accountability in the author’s functional analysis. “Book” is an introductory gloss, not a complete definition.
Qurʾān. Revelation considered through its living recitation and communicated address in the author’s operational model. The term is not restricted here to the ten surahs discussed by the opposing thesis.
Dhikr. Revealed remembrance and the renewed presence of meaning in awareness. The author relates received remembrance to inward cognitive and ethical response.
Tadhkirah. Reminding or admonitory address, emphasized in the book’s account of preparedness for what lies ahead.
Furqān. Discriminating insight and decisive separation, examined in both cognitive and practical settings. “Criterion” captures only part of this functional account.
Mathānī. Paired or mutually returning relations as explored by the author at letter-pair and compositional levels. This working description expresses the proposed model rather than settling the full lexical question.
MATHANI-NET. The author’s framework for exploring letter pairs and wider structural correspondences. It generates proposals that remain subject to contextual and corpus testing.
QCROS. The source’s seven-part examination framework, separating text, exposition, corpus inquiry, hypothesis, resistance testing, operational model, and judgment with scope.
Resistant evidence. A passage or context that challenges a proposed explanation or restricts its range. Its presence is material to a fair account of the argument.
Operational model. A humanly constructed account of relations and functions that assists understanding and application without becoming identical to the revealed text.
