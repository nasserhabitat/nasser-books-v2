The Disputants’ Account and the Measure of Stewardship in Surah Sad — The Concept of al-Naʿjah: From the Ewe to Productive Land and the Incubator of Ideas and Interpretations

A structural, contemplative study of the disputants’ account in Surah Sad (38:21–26). It distinguishes the Qur’anic wording and the lexical sense of naʿjah (ewe) from the attested expression ard nāʿijah (productive land) and from an agrarian property-and-easement hypothesis, compares five interpretive approaches, and holds that complementary layers of understanding carry unequal evidential weight. Condensed English adaptation.

Opening of the book

The account of the disputants (nabaʾ al-khaṣm) in Surah Sad (verses 21–26) is short and extraordinarily concentrated. Visitors scale the wall of David’s private chamber. David is startled. They say that they are litigants, that some have wronged others, and that they seek a judgment in truth without excess. One complainant then describes an asymmetry: his brother has ninety-nine naʿajāt, while he has only one; the brother has demanded akfilnīhā and has prevailed over him in speech. David pronounces the demand unjust. The account proceeds to the wrongdoing of many associates, David’s recognition of a divine trial, his request for forgiveness, and the divine address that appoints him to rule with truth and not follow desire.

It is tempting to substitute a complete historical narrative for this deliberately compressed report. Yet the Qur’an does not specify a farm, an access road, a water source, a price offer, or the unheard litigant’s explanation. Nor does it narrate the notorious story about Uriah and a woman that appears in some exegetical traditions. Such gaps are meaningful constraints on interpretation: they permit questions but not undocumented certainties.

The book begins with the widely recognized sense of naʿjah as a ewe and tests an alternative suggested by Arabic lexical material, particularly the expression arḍ nāʿijah (an easy, level piece of land favorable to vegetation). It then develops a property-and-easement scenario to ask why a seemingly small parcel could matter to a much larger holding. Finally, it proposes a distinct cognitive application: a single independent conviction might be subject to pressure from a powerful interpretive system. The last two are research models of different kinds, not established historical or dictionary meanings of the noun in the Qur’anic account.
