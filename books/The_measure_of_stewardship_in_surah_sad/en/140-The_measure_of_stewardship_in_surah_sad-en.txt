Introduction — Why Revisit the Word Naʿjah?
The account of the disputants (nabaʾ al-khaṣm) in Surah Sad (verses 21–26) is short and extraordinarily concentrated. Visitors scale the wall of David’s private chamber. David is startled. They say that they are litigants, that some have wronged others, and that they seek a judgment in truth without excess. One complainant then describes an asymmetry: his brother has ninety-nine naʿajāt, while he has only one; the brother has demanded akfilnīhā and has prevailed over him in speech. David pronounces the demand unjust. The account proceeds to the wrongdoing of many associates, David’s recognition of a divine trial, his request for forgiveness, and the divine address that appoints him to rule with truth and not follow desire.
It is tempting to substitute a complete historical narrative for this deliberately compressed report. Yet the Qur’an does not specify a farm, an access road, a water source, a price offer, or the unheard litigant’s explanation. Nor does it narrate the notorious story about Uriah and a woman that appears in some exegetical traditions. Such gaps are meaningful constraints on interpretation: they permit questions but not undocumented certainties.
The book begins with the widely recognized sense of naʿjah as a ewe and tests an alternative suggested by Arabic lexical material, particularly the expression arḍ nāʿijah (an easy, level piece of land favorable to vegetation). It then develops a property-and-easement scenario to ask why a seemingly small parcel could matter to a much larger holding. Finally, it proposes a distinct cognitive application: a single independent conviction might be subject to pressure from a powerful interpretive system. The last two are research models of different kinds, not established historical or dictionary meanings of the noun in the Qur’anic account.
This distinction is the governing principle of the book: the compatibility of several layers of reflection does not make their evidential status equal. The Arabic original deploys QCROS, a protocol that moves from text to observation and comparison, then to induction, hypotheses, testing, and expressly bounded conclusions. Here the same logic is retained in an accessible register. The labels [T] Text, [L] Linguistic witness, [I] Inference, [H] Hypothesis, [M] Operational model, [B] Boundary, and [X] Test are explanatory English equivalents for the author’s fuller Arabic status system; they are not a substitute for it.
For readers of any faith or none, the underlying ethical question is accessible: How should a judge distinguish a compelling claim from a sufficiently tested claim? Can unequal holdings create a presumptive injustice without deciding every fact? And how do communities protect both legitimate cooperation and individual independence?
PART ONE — THE QUR’ANIC TEXT AND THE DISPUTE
1. Surah Sad and the Setting of Stewardship
The Qur’anic sequence is important. Surah Sad first depicts David as a figure of strength, devotion, wisdom, established authority, and faṣl al-khiṭāb—discernment or decisive speech (verses 17–20). Then the account of the disputants begins abruptly. The sequence ends in the commissioning words addressed to David: he has been made a khalīfah upon the earth and is instructed to judge among people with truth and not follow hawā, self-directed inclination or desire (verse 26).
The juxtaposition enables an interpretation organized around stewardship and adjudication. A ruler’s strength is not enough; a justice system requires measured attention to evidence, proportionality, and the rights of parties who possess unequal resources. The account’s placement allows a comparison between power and accountability. It does not, by proximity alone, establish that the asset in dispute must be land or that a particular procedural error occurred. Textual sequence supports an interpretive question; sequence is not a free-standing proof of causation.
The author therefore reads the story as a training ground for the ethics of istikhlāf: power is entrusted, not made absolute. A person responsible for other people’s rights must not substitute numerical disparity, rhetorical skill, apparent vulnerability, or personal intuition for the careful pursuit of truth. The Qur’anic ending supplies the general criterion of justice even where the precise factual reconstruction remains debated.
2. The Chamber, the Complaint, and the Limits of the Record
The visitors tasawwarū al-miḥrāb—they scaled the wall or entered the chamber in an exceptional manner. David’s startled reaction is explicitly recorded. They appeal for judgment bi-l-ḥaqq, by truth, and insist that the judge not tushṭiṭ, deviate beyond proper bounds. They ask to be guided toward sawāʾ al-ṣirāṭ, the equitable or straight course. These words establish a striking legal and ethical frame before the asset is named.
One disputant reports that the other has ninety-nine ewes, while he has one; that the other has asked for his single naʿjah; and that the other has overpowered him in address (wa-ʿazzanī fī al-khiṭāb). Here the record becomes especially compressed. The Qur’an quotes one claim and David’s response. It does not reproduce a complete exchange, a cross-examination, an inventory of property, or the final terms of a settlement.
A possible reading is that David responded before the other litigant’s answer could be heard. The author explores that possibility as a warning against an emotionally persuasive first account. But other exegetical explanations exist, including the suggestion that the other party conceded the claim without the Qur’an recording that concession. Neither explanation should be presented as a procedural fact fixed by the text. The text decisively establishes David’s reported judgment and subsequent recognition of a trial; the circumstances that connect them remain interpretively open.
This disciplined refusal to fill silence with certainty is an affirmative research practice. It allows us to consider the dispute as a real adjudicative lesson without inventing witness statements. It also permits evaluation of the view that the scene was a representational test involving angels, provided that view is identified as an exegetical interpretation rather than a line of the Qur’an itself.
3. The Vocabulary: Naʿjah, Akfilnīhā, ʿAzzanī, and Khulaṭāʾ
Four occurrences of the n–ʿ–j root appear in the account: two instances of the singular naʿjah in verse 23, followed by naʿjatika (your ewe) and niʿājihi (his ewes) in verse 24. They occur in this same reported dispute. That concentration marks the word as worth studying, but frequency alone does not dislodge its ordinary animal sense. The different Qur’anic distributions of naʿjah, ghanam (sheep), and ḍaʾn (sheep of the ovine kind) are observable, while the conclusion that each term must encode a wholly separate functional essence requires an independent test.
Akfilnīhā is pivotal. The k–f–l family can express entrusting to care, providing for, guaranteeing, or making someone responsible for something. The Arabic original explores whether the demand can be understood as a request to take over management rather than title. But a precise right of management, retained ownership, and assured yield are not stated in the verse. Classical exegesis also allows a sense of adding the ewe to the larger owner’s possession or control. The word opens a rights-based investigation; it does not, alone, identify the contract.
Wa-ʿazzanī fī al-khiṭāb describes prevailing over someone in discourse. It suggests disparity in argumentative strength or standing, though it does not tell us the exact technique employed. Khulaṭāʾ, commonly rendered associates or partners, evokes the closeness in which rights can collide. The statement that many of them wrong one another provides a warning about relationships where interests intersect. It does not demonstrate that these two individuals were neighbors holding adjoining parcels with shared irrigation rights. [T] The words are present; [H] their hypothetical agricultural setting is not.
The pair “ninety-nine versus one” is a narrative asymmetry before it is an economic explanation. A judge should recognize what the numbers show and what they do not show. They reveal a disproportion in holdings as narrated by one party. They do not disclose the motive for the demand or the reason for the dispute’s escalation. The author uses this gap to motivate a new model without claiming that the gap itself proves the model.
PART TWO — THE FIVE INTERPRETIVE APPROACHES
4. The Ewe as a Female Sheep
The best-known reading takes naʿjah in its familiar Arabic sense: an adult female sheep. On this understanding, a man who already possesses a substantial flock seeks to add his brother’s only ewe. The linguistic advantage of this account is directness. An ordinary word receives its ordinary meaning, and the numerical contrast immediately conveys the moral pressure of the complaint. The Arabic book rightly acknowledges that this is not an invented or superficial dictionary sense.
The author’s challenge concerns explanatory scope. Why would someone with ninety-nine animals seek one more so insistently? Could the disparity in means affect the perceived justice of the demand? These are legitimate questions, but an economy of motives cannot be imposed on the report. An owner might seek an additional animal from greed, entitlement, or another unstated reason. The fact that one item is small relative to ninety-nine does not logically exclude avarice. The ewe interpretation is therefore not disproved by the author’s unanswered question.
This first approach remains the main lexical baseline against which the book’s hypotheses must be tested. A rival interpretation gains credibility not by dismissing a conventional meaning as irrational but by showing why its own contextual and linguistic evidence is more discriminating. In this condensed edition, the animal reading is neither erased nor treated as a straw opponent.
5. The Woman Reading and Narratives Concerning Uriah
Some historical commentaries report an allegorical understanding: the ewes stand for women, and the account is linked to narratives about David, a soldier commonly identified as Uriah, and a woman. The Arabic study strongly rejects attributing a grave transgression to David on the basis of these reports. It stresses that the Qur’an itself never narrates the Uriah episode. The mere presence of a report in an exegetical collection establishes the history of transmission, not the certainty of the episode’s contents.
A source-critical distinction is essential. Al-Ṭabarī’s Jāmiʿ al-bayān preserves accounts and lines of interpretation; al-Thaʿlabī’s al-Kashf wa-l-bayān likewise presents exegetical material including figurative readings. The existence of such accounts can be documented. Their chains of transmission, variants, possible Israelite backgrounds, and theological implications must be evaluated separately. Not all narrations in a large compilation have the same chain or evidential value, and a single verdict should not be applied to them without review.
The book’s position is clear at the level of interpretive preference: it refuses to make these stories a necessary explanation of the Qur’anic verses or an unquestioned fact about prophetic character. That position should not be confused with a claim that every transmitted line has already undergone full, edition-by-edition hadith criticism. The Arabic research edition itself retains source-verification tasks. In this English adaptation, the dispute is summarized without reproducing graphic or speculative elaborations that do not aid the argument.
6. Representation, Modern Social Readings, and the Five-Way Comparison
A third cluster of readings regards the exchange as an enacted lesson rather than a literal transaction. On one variant, angels appear as litigants to provide a moral or judicial test. The unusual entrance and David’s fear are taken as circumstantial support. Other modern symbolic suggestions relate the numbers to responsibilities for widows, orphans, and social care. The author evaluates these as interpretive constructions that need both textual and linguistic grounding.
A representational reading should not be dismissed merely because it is representational: parables can teach jurisprudential restraint. Equally, neither an angelic identification nor the widow-and-orphan scheme is stated in the cited verses. A reading becomes weaker when it imports detailed participants and legal relationships without sufficiently independent indicators. The same standard must be applied to the author’s proposed land scenario; imaginative coherence is not textual proof.
The study therefore organizes five broad approaches: (1) the literal female sheep; (2) the woman allegory associated with extraneous reports; (3) representational or socially symbolic accounts; (4) the proposed productive-land interpretation with easements; and (5) a cognitive or operational application concerning ideas and interpretive autonomy. These should not be treated as five readings with identical epistemic status. Some are lexical readings, some are received interpretations of a story, some are reconstructed scenarios, and the fifth is primarily a modern ethical analogy.
The point of comparison is not to crown a hypothesis by rhetorical force. Each claim must answer: What exactly does the Qur’an state? What does Arabic usage support? Which inferences depend on the hypothesis? What would count against it? And what remains unspecified? The fifth approach will later be assessed as a model of reflection, not a newly discovered dictionary definition of naʿjah.
PART THREE — LANGUAGE, LAND, AND A PROPOSED ROOT MODEL
7. What the Lexicons Actually Establish
The Arabic original turns to Ibn Manẓūr’s Lisān al-ʿArab to recover the range of the n–ʿ–j word family. It reports the familiar noun naʿjah as a female of the sheep kind, with extensions in certain uses to female gazelles or other animals. It also highlights a quite different expression: arḍ nāʿijah, literally land described as level, easy, and favorable to vegetation, including the growth of ramth. This is the lexical doorway to the book’s most distinctive hypothesis.
The doorway must not be confused with the destination. Naʿjah is a noun, while nāʿijah is a derived descriptive form used with arḍ, land. The sharing of the root consonants does not make the two expressions interchangeable. A dictionary documenting arḍ nāʿijah demonstrates that a cognate form can describe land. It does not document the noun naʿjah used alone as a technical name for a plot of land. The author accepts this as a substantive limit on the proposed reinterpretation.
Further comparison with Ibn Fāris’s Maqāyīs al-lughah shows why a root cannot be reduced to an attractive single modern image. The lexical discussion covers a broader historical field, including associations that are not simply “a concealed incubator of productivity.” A sound argument must work through the actual forms and contexts, not infer the sense of a verse from a modern story told about its consonants.
This book’s linguistic contribution is therefore twofold: it makes a relatively neglected descriptive usage visible to modern readers, and it demonstrates the necessity of separating a verified lexical occurrence from an interpretive assignment to a Qur’anic occurrence. They are related research steps, not the same finding. One may value the question without mistaking it for a completed proof.
8. Ramth, Level Ground, and Economic Productivity
The expression arḍ nāʿijah leads to ramth, a desert plant associated in lexicons with grazing and the ecology of dry land. In the author’s development, the image is one of terrain that is comparatively level and hospitable to growth. Such land can be understood as a productive resource rather than a merely passive surface. Water, routes of access, and adjacent holdings may determine the importance of a small tract beyond its size.
The phrase “favorable to vegetation” supplies a plausible context for thinking about agriculture or pasture. It does not establish the presence of a particular plant, ownership structure, or irrigation system in David’s dispute. Nor does it necessarily mean fertile agricultural soil in the modern technical sense: lexical description and agronomic evaluation are distinct tasks. The book’s economic analysis begins with these features as a conceptual bridge rather than as surveyed facts about the historical setting.
Why does this matter for justice? Owners may hold rights that appear unequal in quantity while each depends on infrastructure controlled by another. A small path, crossing, canal intake, or well can be strategically indispensable. If a single productive unit lies at the meeting point of other holdings, cooperation may be rational even when one party already controls far more land. This is a recognized form of economic reasoning, but the text of Surah Sad does not identify such a setting. The research value lies in showing how a hypothesis can explain a neglected question while remaining exposed to contrary evidence.
9. The Consonants n–ʿ–j and the Mathani Hypothesis
A distinctive part of the author’s method, Fiqh al-Lisān al-Qurʾānī (a proposed jurisprudence of Qur’anic expression), considers the apparent movement of meaning across consonants and pairs of letters. In the n–ʿ–j sequence the author provisionally associates nūn with inward concealment or containment, ʿayn with depth or penetration, and jīm with exertion and outward emergence. The overlapping pairs n–ʿ, ʿ–j, and the non-adjacent n–j are explored through the Mathani framework.
The resulting model describes “a deep receptacle that shelters a generative potential and brings its effect forth through effort.” It lends itself to images of gestation, growth, and the formation of convictions. Its evocative power is clear, especially when the author moves from a living animal to fertile land and, later, to a fertile idea. Yet a model of consonantal motion is not self-proving. To establish a lexical rule one would need systematically collected examples, counterexamples, historical sound and morphology analysis, and tests against readings that the model does not conveniently explain.
The Arabic study increasingly marks this construction as a bounded research model [M], not an agreed ancient definition of each letter. The English edition retains the model because it explains the direction of the author’s inquiry, while refusing to use its poetic elegance to settle the meaning of a contested verse. A hypothesis should survive tests in texts independent of the passages that first inspired it; otherwise its apparent success can become circular.
For practical readers the lesson is broader than any single root: phonetic, etymological, and conceptual analogies can generate questions. They must not be confused with an independently verified answer. A useful generative metaphor may illuminate relationships even when it cannot be promoted into a dictionary gloss or a binding interpretive law.
10. From a Concrete Noun to a Proposed Unifying Description
The Arabic book uses the distinction between the thing named and the description proposed as a bridge. The animal reading names a ewe. A woman’s association with the word belongs to reported figurative usage and exegetical tradition, not to a claim that women and animals are identical. Land described by nāʿijah belongs to another lexical construction. To group all three under “generative protection and emergence” requires interpretive work; it is a possible analytic description, not a word-for-word translation common to all forms.
The author accordingly asks whether a concept can be studied at several levels without flattening their differences. At the biological level a ewe is a living reproductive animal. At the environmental or economic level land supports growth. At the cognitive level ideas mature and yield behavior. These are real analogical possibilities, but they do not form one undifferentiated semantic proof. Three layers should not be assigned equal authority merely because they share a powerful image.
An argument for land as the referent in Surah Sad would require a bridge beyond root-sharing: for example, a convincing historical Arabic instance of naʿjah referring to property in a similar construction, or compelling internal contextual evidence that discriminates it from the sheep reading. The source edition treats this bridge as an open challenge. The cognitive layer requires even less lexical commitment: it can be useful as an explicitly figurative moral model without claiming that the first audience heard “an idea” when the Qur’an said naʿjah.
Thus, what begins as a lexical investigation matures into a method for organizing the limits of interpretation. The words of revelation, lexicographic evidence, reconstructed settings, and contemporary applications may converse with one another. They cannot replace one another. The more ambitious an interpretation, the more precisely it must state what would falsify or weaken its conclusions.
PART FOUR — THE PROPERTY AND EASEMENT HYPOTHESIS
11. Reconstructing a Property Dispute
Suppose, for a thought experiment, that the ninety-nine naʿajāt designate ninety-nine productive properties rather than animals, and that the single naʿjah is an independent parcel. Under this supposition the key question changes: not “Why would a rich owner desire one more unit?” but “What function could the remaining unit perform in an interdependent system?” This is the central shift from numerical comparison to location and function.
The thought experiment can render the apparent insistence economically intelligible. A plot that lies at a junction or between otherwise separated holdings may be strategically valuable for transport, water, or coherent cultivation. One parcel can create a bottleneck that affects many others. The legal problem might then concern the proper allocation of access and maintenance rather than an unrestricted transfer of title. This scenario supplies a possible answer to the author’s recurring question about insistence on the single unit.
That plausibility does not establish the truth of the reconstruction. The Qur’an does not say that there were one hundred parcels, that the parcel lay in the center, or that the claim involved a land register. Nor does a seventy-ninefold or ninety-ninefold advantage in possessions logically rule out simple greed. The scenario must be weighed against the more ordinary animal reading and against interpretations of the scene as a representational test.
The book’s preferred practice is to label each step: the reported number belongs to [T] text; the alternative property referent is [H] hypothesis; and the strategic bottleneck is a [M] constructed example within that hypothesis. If future research cannot independently support the transition from naʿjah to land, the economic example may remain educationally useful without becoming an exegesis of the noun.
12. Akfilnīhā: Care, Ownership, Administration, and Use
The distinction between ownership of an asset, administrative control, and the right to benefit from an asset is essential to the book’s legal imagination. Someone may retain title while another person manages irrigation, paths, maintenance, or outputs. This distinction makes it possible to imagine settlement terms that preserve a small owner’s position while serving a larger productive network. The verb akfilnīhā invites the author to ask whether some form of custodianship could be implicated.
But the linguistic family of kafālah does not compel that narrow legal result. In exegetical treatments of Surah Sad the verb can be interpreted as asking to add the ewe to one’s possession or protection, and formulations akin to “relinquish it to me” are also transmitted. Nothing in the quoted verse says, “I will administer it while you retain title and receive its yield.” Such clauses belong to the author’s possible legal reconstruction, not to the divine wording.
In the land scenario, one might distinguish three rights: title (who owns the land), management (who directs its operation), and usufruct (who receives or exercises beneficial use). A fair settlement need not automatically award all three to one party. It might recognize access while preventing appropriation. It might impose duties that limit damage without extinguishing lawful independence. These are contemporary analytical questions inspired by the text’s ethical framework, not specific rules explicitly enacted in David’s recorded decision.
The phrase “he overpowered me in speech” is particularly relevant to the ethics of voluntary consent. Whether the requested arrangement was commercially rational is a separate question from whether it was demanded justly. An efficient proposal can become oppressive if pressed by coercion or domination. Equally, the reported legal facts do not prove that the smaller owner obstructed a fair arrangement. Neither litigant’s unrecorded intentions should be supplied as fact.
13. The Cultivated Field: David and Solomon in Surah Al-Anbiya
A second Qur’anic passage offers a careful comparison. Surah Al-Anbiya (verses 78–79) describes David and Solomon judging a dispute concerning a cultivated field into which the people’s sheep had strayed or grazed destructively. The Qur’an says that God made Solomon understand the matter, while granting both judgment and knowledge. Here the field and sheep are explicitly identified, unlike the unnamed physical setting of the naʿjah report.
Exegetical traditions provide details about the respective rulings. Some narrate an initial award of the sheep to the crop owner, followed by a more restorative arrangement whereby benefits and productive assets would be managed while the damaged field recovered. These particular mechanics are reported in exegesis; they should not be quoted as though every term occurs in the two Qur’anic verses. The book learns from the reported contrast while respecting the difference between revelation and narration about it.
For the author’s argument the comparison is suggestive. A judicial system can focus on compensation, temporary use, restoration, and the future productivity of an asset rather than treating every injury as a once-for-all transfer of ownership. The Al-Anbiya passage illustrates a concern with practical judgment in a case where agricultural production and sheep actually meet. The lesson can inform reflection on possible settlements in Surah Sad without proving that the two cases concern identical assets or procedures.
The same limitation protects the prophetic narratives from forced symmetry. David appears in both passages, but the former names litigants and ewes while the latter names a field and a flock. The Qur’an neither merges these cases nor supplies a universal one-to-one mapping of the reported rulings. The author uses the parallel as a supporting comparison [I], never as an independent definition of naʿjah or proof that David transferred property unlawfully.
14. Why Location, Passage, and Water Can Matter
Imagine a parcel of land placed between larger fields. If the only viable route for labor, animals, or harvest transport crosses it, a right of passage becomes consequential. If the parcel includes a wellhead or sits at a water connection supplying the surrounding lands, water rights may matter even more. The land’s economic value in such a situation is not proportional to its surface area alone. It derives partly from the position it holds within a working network.
Two forms of easement structure this conceptual example: access and irrigation. The first concerns lawful passage; the second concerns sharing a water source or route. When rights overlap, an unbounded insistence on one owner’s absolute control can injure another. But coercive integration by the dominant owner can also become baghy, encroachment beyond what is right. The justice problem is therefore double: avoid capture by concentrated power, and avoid unnecessary obstruction of legitimate common use.
The model also distinguishes three questions often collapsed into one. What is the shared technical problem? A blocked route or water supply. What is the rightful arrangement? A negotiated, adjudicated, or otherwise just allocation of use and maintenance. How was the arrangement demanded? Possibly through persuasive legal argument, but perhaps with undue pressure. An arrangement good in operational terms is not necessarily justified if one party’s autonomy is extinguished without sufficient reason.
The Arabic book at times develops this case dramatically—offers of a higher price, guarantees of income, a small owner refusing all proposals, and an ensuing confrontation. Such details can help a reader visualize competing claims, but they are not contained in Surah Sad. The refined publication edition marks them as a scenario under test. We cannot conclude that both parties committed baghy, or that the single owner was secretly at fault, merely because a hypothetical conflict could be constructed to make that possible.
The payoff is a method for reading disputes about shared resources without reducing every disagreement to a contest between a villain and a victim. Number alone cannot decide a right, and power alone cannot create one. This operational lesson stands even when the literal identity of the asset remains unsettled.
PART FIVE — JUDGMENT, TESTING, AND COGNITIVE APPLICATION
15. “He Wronged You”: What the Judgment Does and Does Not Establish
David’s response is forceful: the complainant has been wronged by the request to add his naʿjah to the other man’s niʿāj. The next sentence turns from an individual case to a broader moral observation: many khulaṭāʾ, people bound by association or partnership, transgress against one another, except those who believe and act rightly; such people are few. The combination makes the account more than a story about an accumulation of possessions. It addresses a structural danger in relationships of unequal leverage.
The author reads the phrase “by asking for your ewe to be added to his ewes” with particular attention to the way the demand is made. A request may contain injustice because it seeks an unwarranted transfer, or because the stronger party exerts pressure in a conflict of rights. The reported victory “in speech” lends force to the concern about imbalance in a judicial encounter. Yet the text does not describe the exact threats, proposals, or bargaining history. These cannot be reconstructed simply from the strength of David’s rebuke.
Nor does the author require the judge to favor the weaker party regardless of facts. Compassion is relevant, but the Qur’anic standard is ḥaqq—truth or justice. In an actual case the claimant’s account, the opposing account, documentary evidence, relevant shared rights, and possible harms should all be examined. This is the book’s principal judicial application: a compelling narrative is not identical to an exhaustive record.
The prospect of a mistaken response is used here as a pedagogical possibility, not a proven charge against David. Some exegetes understand the judgment differently, and the Qur’an does not supply a transcript showing the second party being silenced. What may confidently be drawn from the revealed sequence is the high standard imposed on one entrusted with justice. What remains contested must be retained as such even if the hypothesis makes an elegant moral lesson.
16. The Trial of David and the Meaning of Return
Surah Sad reports David’s recognition that he had been tested, his request for forgiveness, his falling in devotion, and his turning back to God. The following verse speaks of forgiveness and a favorable return. These expressions anchor the passage in spiritual responsibility as well as legal inquiry. They do not, by themselves, give a detailed diagnosis of the trial’s trigger or a list of steps David later took to amend a legal ruling.
A familiar scholarly interpretation sees the trial in the possibility of haste: a judgment pronounced before the factual situation was fully tested. The author’s interest in this reading is understandable, for it offers a compelling lesson about the first presentation of grievances. Nevertheless, it remains an interpretive reading. Other commentators offer explanations in which the opposing litigant may have acknowledged the claim, or the encounter itself may have been a representational test. A scientifically disciplined book cannot declare one reconstruction to be a direct Qur’anic report while acknowledging that the narrative is silent on the disputed detail.
The operative ethical insight can be taught without a definitive verdict on the reconstruction. A judge must remain alert to surprise, imbalance in speech, plausible but unverified premises, and personal attachment to a seemingly obvious result. A researcher faces similar risks when an attractive explanation fits a desired conclusion. The act of inābah, returning or turning back, can therefore illuminate intellectual self-correction: revise judgments when reasons do not withstand examination.
The author also distinguishes prophetic dignity from unfounded anecdotes. The Qur’an records both divine favor and trial; neither should be distorted by sensational stories imported as established biography. The proper response to interpretive tension is not to invent a new certainty, but to state the textual datum, the competing accounts, the scope of inquiry, and what still needs verification.
17. The Alleged Hundred Words: A Methodological Test
A numerical idea circulating around this account is that the passage contains exactly one hundred words, matching the combined ninety-nine and one naʿajāt. The Arabic book dedicates a chapter and a statistical appendix to this claim. Its governing requirement is simple: define a word-counting unit and a textual boundary before counting. A procedure adjusted after a desired total is known can create a striking result without creating evidence.
The tested baseline counts written units separated by spaces in the Qur’anic passage in Surah Sad, verses 21–25, following the stated Hafs written presentation. Under this protocol, affixed conjunctions, prepositions, and pronouns are not counted as independent written words merely because grammatical analysis can separate their functions. A morphologically segmented count would be a different project and would require its own complete protocol.

The closed five-verse passage thus yields 86, not one hundred. Extending the count into verse 26 through the phrase wa-lā tattabiʿ al-hawā (“and do not follow inclination”) adds thirteen written units and produces ninety-nine. Including the next written unit, fa-yuḍillaka (“lest it lead you astray”), produces one hundred. This is a count under a newly extended and specific endpoint, not proof that the account naturally comprises exactly one hundred words. The stopping point must be defended independently, and neighboring endpoints must be tested on the same basis.
The potential match is therefore a numerical hypothesis with conditional arithmetic, not a stand-alone miracle or a decisive linguistic argument. It cannot prove the ewe is land, prove the cause of David’s trial, or furnish legal rules. A useful religious or literary observation does not become a scientific law by arriving at a memorable integer.
The chapter also offers a general warning: comparing script units with grammatical segments, or splitting certain prefixed letters while leaving others attached, can manufacture totals. The numerical neatness of a result is not a substitute for a fixed, reproducible method. The strongest contribution of this chapter may be its willingness to refuse a pleasing numerical conclusion.
18. Principles of Reading and the Limits of Interpretation
The Arabic study ends its principal methodological argument by separating four necessary inquiries: the word, its construction, its context, and the proposed event. Each restricts the others. One cannot read an isolated dictionary gloss into a verse against the grammar, nor fill the gaps of an account with an elaborate setting and then cite that setting as the reason the original word must have a particular meaning.
Several safeguards govern this process. First, a previous author’s conclusion is not independent evidence for a new corpus claim. Second, adjacency does not establish causation: a story placed before a command about stewardship is relevant to that command, but the relationship does not specify every material aspect of the story. Third, the descriptive adjective is not the noun: arḍ nāʿijah does not automatically define naʿjah. Fourth, a successful explanatory model is not the event itself: a hypothetical well and access road may help us understand the stakes of a property dispute while remaining absent from the Qur’anic report.
A responsible interpretation must be capable of meeting a reverse test. What happens if the asset is an animal? Does the moral concern about domination persist? What happens if the property has no strategic location? Does the easement explanation collapse? If the other litigant conceded the accusation, does the inference of a one-sided hearing still follow? If the one-hundred-word endpoint is moved by one unit, does the proposed numerical significance disappear? These questions are not hostility to contemplation; they are the conditions of its credibility.
The book also warns against turning a modern analytical label into a scriptural term. “Easement,” “cognitive monopoly,” “operational guardianship,” and “productive incubator” are conceptual tools. Their usefulness depends on the investigator’s honesty about what they add. The Arabic original’s status-register vocabulary is intended to make these additions visible instead of disguising them.
The final question is practical: How much explanatory power does a proposal gain, and how many unsupported premises does it require? The property hypothesis may be illuminating as a model for shared resources. The cognitive analogy may be illuminating as a model for the ethics of thought. Neither may dismiss contrary lexical evidence simply because it has produced a moving moral narrative.
19. The Cognitive Layer: Ideas, Convictions, and Interpretive Power
The fifth approach, added in the expanded Arabic edition, is deliberately different from an attempt to identify the original physical asset. It asks whether the structure of the dispute can be used as a model of intellectual relations. An interpretation, conviction, or novel idea may be nurtured over time and may generate conduct, institutions, and new understanding. In this sense the author calls it a “generative incubator”—a metaphorical naʿjah in the life of thought. This is not a claim that the Qur’anic word literally means “idea.”
The contrast of ninety-nine to one becomes a schematic picture of a large intellectual system confronted by a single independent conviction. The larger system may own the channels of public explanation, the inherited vocabulary, or the authority to certify what counts as knowledge. A lone idea might nevertheless expose a weakness in that system. The analogy dramatizes an ethically important risk: numbers of advocates, institutions, or interpretations do not by themselves settle a truth claim.
Within this model, akfilnīhā depicts a possible form of paternalistic inclusion: an institution claims to “protect” a dissident idea by placing it under a dominant framework. The expression wa-ʿazzanī fī al-khiṭāb becomes a lens for the experience of rhetorical overpowering. Neither phrase proves that the historical litigants were debating doctrine, or that the Qur’an identifies a particular modern institution. These are operational comparisons [M] meant to teach restraint in the use of intellectual power.
The Qur’anic use of ḥarth supplies an analogy for the shift of domain. The word appears in physical contexts of cultivation and also in the contrast between the ḥarth of this world and the ḥarth of the hereafter (Surah Al-Shura, verse 20). The documented semantic flexibility of ḥarth can encourage consideration of ethical “harvest” as a metaphor for knowledge, but it does not establish an equivalent lexical shift in the separate word naʿjah. Shared patterns of application do not supply shared definitions.
This cognitive model has a second dimension: a marginal or singular claim should not be automatically declared true by virtue of its independence. The single naʿjah is not morally infallible in the analogy, just as the ninety-nine are not automatically wrong. Both established systems and new challengers must submit their assertions to evidence, consistency, and the right of fair reply. Protecting a person’s capacity to question is compatible with testing that person’s propositions severely.
The author’s strongest contemporary application is therefore an ethic of epistemic stewardship. Legitimate institutions should facilitate access to evidence, protect disagreement from coercion, and make their authority answerable to reason. Individuals, in turn, should distinguish courage from certainty and independence from immunity to critique. In this carefully bounded sense, justice in the chamber of David becomes an instructive image for the justice of discussion.
Conclusion — What This Study Establishes, Proposes, and Leaves Open
The Arabic book advances seven connected findings and four practical recommendations. In condensed form, the findings are these.
First, the Qur’an’s account should be distinguished from reports imported by later storytelling. This does not mean that every transmitted narration has already been individually and finally classified. The fact that a source preserves a report is not proof that the Qur’an contains it.
Second, the distinct appearances of naʿjah, ghanam, and ḍaʾn merit lexical examination. Their differences can be documented; a comprehensive hidden functional rule derived from their consonants cannot be assumed without further tests.
Third, the expression arḍ nāʿijah is a useful lexical witness, bringing a land-related derivative of n–ʿ–j into view. It is not a demonstrated equation of the isolated noun naʿjah with a plot of land in Surah Sad.
Fourth, a property’s location can be as important as its size when many holdings depend upon a route or water supply. This explains the logic of the author’s proposed easement scenario, but the scenario remains an unverified reconstruction of the Qur’anic event.
Fifth, the difference between ownership, administration, and beneficial use generates important legal questions about akfilnīhā. The text does not explicitly specify retained title, service guarantees, or a negotiated management contract.
Sixth, the trial, David’s turning back, and the command to judge with truth provide an ethical framework for judicial restraint. The assertion that David erred by failing to hear the other side remains an exegetical proposal, not a verbatim procedural finding of the Qur’an.
Seventh, concepts can be considered at more than one level of human application without all levels having the same proof-status. The comparison between productive land and productive thought can enrich reflection, but it does not transform an analogy into a new literal meaning of revelation.
The four practical recommendations follow. (1) Resist letting familiar usage end inquiry prematurely, without abandoning the familiar usage’s evidential weight. (2) Keep explicit text distinct from inferred relations, imagined scenarios, and present-day applications. (3) Study the justice of shared resources, including routes, water, maintenance, and protection from abuse, with care for both individual and collective rights. (4) Teach adjudication and scholarship as disciplines of evidential patience: listen carefully, test contrary accounts, declare uncertainty, and remain willing to revise.
The book does not close the subject. It opens three further research pathways: a corpus-grounded vocabulary of Qur’anic stewardship and shared resources; a controlled comparison of the passages about David and Solomon; and a wider evaluation of root-based conceptual models against independent examples and failure cases.
The last word is a methodological one: multiple layers of understanding may coexist, but they do not occupy equal layers of proof. A faithful translation must preserve that distinction as diligently as it preserves an author’s central imagination.

Compact Appendix B — How to Reproduce the Word Count
The Arabic work’s number experiment can be reproduced with four declared steps. Choose a specified Arabic text presentation; use a consistent separation rule for written units; define the beginning and ending words before tallying; and report totals for neighboring endpoints rather than only for the one that produces the desired result.
For Surah Sad, verses 21–25, the Arabic edition’s stated written-unit total is 7 + 23 + 15 + 32 + 9 = 86. The endpoint at al-hawā in verse 26 yields 99; adding fa-yuḍillaka yields 100. The count is reproducible only under the chosen script-and-boundary convention. A grammatical segmentation that separates attached particles must not be silently substituted for a count of written units.
This appendix does not establish numerical miraculousness. It illustrates the author’s stricter claim: measurement should test an idea rather than being redesigned to reward it.
Compact Appendix C — Glossary of Working Terms
al-Naʿjah / naʿjah (نعجة) — Commonly “ewe”; the disputed noun in the Qur’anic account. Do not silently render it as “land” or “idea” when quoting the verse.
arḍ nāʿijah (أرض ناعجة) — A descriptive lexical expression involving land. The source of the author’s hypothesis, but morphologically different from the noun naʿjah.
akfilnīhā (أكفلنيها) — The litigant’s request. In translation, “entrust her/it to me” or “place her/it in my care” can signal an aspect of kafālah, while the full range, including forms of addition to possession, must remain open to investigation.
kafālah (كفالة) — Guardianship, care, guarantee, or custodial responsibility, according to construction and context. Not a uniquely defined modern property-management contract.
khulaṭāʾ (خلطاء) — Persons with mingled interests, associates, or partners. It does not itself prove adjoining title deeds or common irrigation works.
baghy (بغي) — Wrongful encroachment, oppressive transgression, or overstepping what is right. Its appearance in the narrative does not specify every party’s motive.
ʿazzanī fī al-khiṭāb (عزني في الخطاب) — “He prevailed over me in speech.” A starting point for studying rhetorical disparity; details of negotiation are not included.
ḥarth (حرث) — Cultivation, tillage, or productive endeavor depending on context. The word’s Qur’anic range is used as an analogy, not proof of a new definition of naʿjah.
istikhlāf / khalīfah (استخلاف / خليفة) — Appointment to a position of stewardship or entrusted authority. In Surah Sad, verse 26, this authority is explicitly tied to judging with truth.
miḥrāb (محراب) — The chamber or special place scaled by the disputants. Its exact architectural description is not essential to the main argument.
Mathani system (نظام المثاني) — The author’s proposed procedure for analyzing paired structures, including consonantal pairs. A heuristic subject to comparison and falsification.
QCROS — The author’s protocol for distinguishing text, observations, inductions, proposed relations, hypotheses, tests, bounded findings, and models. This English adaptation uses simplified status labels; the primary Arabic register remains definitive for the project’s technical decisions.
Operational model — A deliberately constructed analogy to illuminate practical relations in economics, law, or intellectual life. Its applicability must not be mistaken for a direct Qur’anic description.
Right of easement / irtifāq (ارتفاق) — A legal or practical right connected with access, water, or use of another’s property. It belongs to the book’s hypothesized property setting, not an explicitly reported term in Surah Sad’s dispute.
Compact Appendix D — Sources and a Guide for Further Study
The Arabic original is the controlling source for the expanded arguments, textual comparisons, tables, the eight appendices, visual charts, and bibliographic apparatus. This English edition names the principal source families and indicates where the inquiry remains unfinished; it intentionally does not fabricate page references for editions not fully checked against printed images.
Primary Qur’anic passages. Surah Sad, verses 17–26, especially verses 21–26 for the disputants and judgment; Surah Al-Anbiya, verses 78–79 for the cultivated-field comparison; Surah Al-Shura, verse 20 for the change in application of ḥarth; and Surah Al-Baqara, verse 223 for another, context-specific use of the word ḥarth. The meaning of one passage must be examined in its own setting before importing it into another.
Arabic lexical sources. Ibn Manẓūr, Lisān al-ʿArab, especially entries n–ʿ–j and r–m–th; Ibn Fāris, Maqāyīs al-lughah, entry n–ʿ–j. The reported land expression is an important witness; the form-and-context distinction is a central question for further research.
Classical and modern commentary. Al-Ṭabarī, Jāmiʿ al-bayān on Surah Sad, especially verses 23–24; al-Thaʿlabī, al-Kashf wa-l-bayān on the same passage; and Muḥammad Ḥusayn al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī, al-Mīzān fī tafsīr al-Qurʾān, volume 17 in the edition consulted by the Arabic research team. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī’s treatment encompasses the ordinary animal meaning, the language of custodianship and authority, and alternatives concerning the nature of the encounter; it cannot be reduced to a single isolated stance. The Arabic edition records that the printed-page verification of some citations and detailed isnād assessment are still not complete.
A test for the independent researcher. First, read the Qur’anic account without a reconstructed setting. Next, list the ordinary senses of the key words in the precise forms used. Then review the land-related lexical adjective and ask what evidence would be needed to connect it to the Qur’anic noun. Compare the sheep, land, and representational readings without granting any one of them immunity from critique. Only then consider how the story might inform contemporary thought about shared property or intellectual freedom. Record separately what the text says, what a source reports, what a hypothesis proposes, and what a modern analogy teaches.
