III. Why This Book?
This book is not a continuation of contemporary self-help literature, nor an attempt to project modern psychological or spiritual theories onto the Qur'an. It does not begin with a preconceived definition of the "heart" and then search for verses that confirm it. Rather, it starts from a more fundamental question:
What does the Qur'an say about the heart when we let the Qur'an define the heart by itself?
This question may seem like a limited linguistic inquiry, but it opens onto a broader epistemological problem: the concept of the heart in modern consciousness is no longer a purely Qur'anic concept. Layers of medical, psychological, mystical, philosophical, and developmental meanings have accumulated around it, making it difficult to distinguish between what the Qur'an actually says, what tradition has said about it, what modern culture has added, and what has become "truth" merely through repetition.
Hence the necessity of returning to a methodological zero point: not to ask first what we know about the heart, then search for verses that support it; but to ask what the Qur'anic system says about the heart, and then let the results of induction take shape gradually.
The Qur'an does not present the heart in a single place or through a direct definition, but distributes it across a wide network of functions, states, and relationships. The heart comprehends, understands, remembers, humbles itself, and finds peace. It is also described as sound, diseased, hardened, sealed, stamped, deviant, heedless, and repentant. This diversity is not merely a list of adjacent attributes, but raw material for a deeper question:
What is the structure that allows the heart to perform these functions and to move between these states?
Therefore, this book does not treat the "heart" as an isolated word for which a synonym should be found, but as a node within a Qur'anic semantic network. The goal is not to say that the heart means such-and-such, but to discover how the concept operates within the system: What is it associated with? How is it described? What functions are attributed to it? What states can it undergo? What is its relationship to revelation, guidance, response, rejection, repentance, deviation, contentment, tranquility, and behavior?
This is the difference between searching for the "meaning of a word" and searching for the "structure of a concept."
IV. The Central Problem
The central problem this book poses is:
How does the Qur'an present the "heart" within a network of functions, states, and relationships that make it a central element in human orientation, without projecting modern psychological concepts or foreign energetic and philosophical models onto it?
From this central problem, several deeper questions branch:
If the Qur'an makes the heart the locus of comprehension, understanding, remembrance, humility, and tranquility, does this indicate that the heart in the Qur'anic lexicon is primarily a cognitive concept? Or do these functions reveal only one aspect of a broader structure?
And if the heart is simultaneously described as sound, diseased, hardened, sealed, stamped, deviant, heedless, and repentant, are we facing different "states" of a single structure? Or are we facing multiple semantic uses that cannot be brought together under a single model before induction is complete?
And if relationships appear in the text between revelation, the heart, response, contentment, tranquility, and behavior, do these relationships reveal an operational sequence? Or are they a network of intersecting concepts that cannot be converted into a causal line merely because they appear together?
These questions make the subject of the heart transcend lexical inquiry to structural inquiry. The problem is not that people do not know the meaning of the word "heart," but that the concept itself has undergone, throughout history, processes of reduction, redefinition, and projection, to the point where it has become necessary to reconstruct its boundaries from within the text.
V. Why the Heart?
The preliminary induction of Qur'anic discourse raises a question that should not be passed over quickly: Why did the Qur'an make the "heart" the locus of a number of cognitive and perceptual functions, and not make "the intellect" a central term in the later technical sense that became established in philosophical and theological discourses?
This does not mean that the Qur'an denies the intellect or negates thinking, nor that the heart and the intellect are opposites. Rather, the point is more subtle:
Why does the Qur'an express a number of processes that we today call mental through verbs attributed to the heart?
The Qur'an says that the heart comprehends, understands, and remembers, and also connects it to humility, tranquility, faith, deviation, harshness, and disease. This network deserves to be read as it is, before we translate it into modern psychological or philosophical terms.
Thus, the question "Why the heart?" becomes a question about the structure of the Qur'anic language itself, not a question about the preference between two organs or systems.
The book does not offer a definitive answer at the outset. It considers the centrality of the heart a research hypothesis worth testing. The first hypothesis that induction allows is that the heart is not merely a locus of emotional states, but a structure involved in shaping human direction and response to revelation. But this formulation is not elevated to the level of law until the resources of the root, its network, and its relationships are fully induced.
VI. The Inductive Method
The method adopted in this book is complete induction before judgment.
Complete induction here does not mean merely collecting a number of verses in which the word "heart" appears, but building a controlled corpus of the concept's resources, then studying each resource in its context, then tracking the conjugations, associations, and relationships, then searching for the constants that recur across different contexts.
The method does not merely ask: Where does the heart appear? It also asks: How does it appear? With which verbs? With which descriptions? In which syntactic structures? What changes when the context changes? And what remains constant despite changing contexts?
This is where the distinction between the "semantic core" and the "contextual properties" becomes important. The core should not be the sum of meanings that appear in a single place, but what can be defended as a common element across the entire network of resources.
Thus, the meaning of the heart is not extracted from a single derivation, nor from a single witness, nor from a beautiful metaphor, nor from a phonetic coincidence, but from the network of usage.

VII. The Governing Rules
1. Qur'anic System Independence (PQSI)
The Qur'anic concept is not initially interpreted by any philosophical, scientific, or psychological theory, ancient or modern. Rather, its definition is extracted from within the network of Qur'anic usage first. Then the results, after their construction is complete, are compared with what the human sciences and knowledge affirm, without those sciences being the source of defining the concept or the criterion for judging the text.
This rule does not mean rejecting science, but separating the functions of knowledge. The Qur'an is the source of establishing the concept within this study. Empirical science is a source of independent comparison and testing. Contemporary philosophical, psychological, and spiritual models are material for comparison and critique, not material for building Qur'anic meaning from within.
2. Structure Before Sequence (SBS)
No judgment is made that one Qur'anic concept precedes another or results from it unless induction indicates an explicit or necessary sequential relationship. Mere association or integration of concepts is not sufficient to prove temporal or causal sequence.
This is a crucial rule in studying the heart, because the ease of building operational models may tempt the researcher to convert the network of concepts into a chain:
text
Revelation → Heart → Response → Acceptance → Contentment → Tranquility → Behavior
But the mere presence of these concepts in one network does not necessarily prove that one occurs temporally before another, nor that one is a direct cause of another.
3. Falsifiability (FR)
No law or model in Qur'anic Linguistic Jurisprudence is adopted unless it is possible to specify the conditions that, if proven by induction, would invalidate that law or necessitate its modification.
To say that "the heart is a structure for reorientation" is not enough to be beautiful or consistent with some verses; it must be testable across all resources of the root. If resources appear that cannot be explained within the model, there are multiple methodological possibilities: the model may be incomplete, or the core may be broader than we assumed, or the concept of "reorientation" may be a contextual property rather than a general core, or the entire model may need rebuilding.

5. Competing Hypotheses (CHR)
It is not enough to prove that a particular hypothesis is consistent with a set of texts, because consistency alone is a weak criterion. Several hypotheses may be able to explain the same set of evidence. Therefore, alternative hypotheses must be presented when available, and all must be tested on the same corpus and by the same criteria.
VIII. The Intellectual Journey: From the Word to the Structure
The Core Idea in its Simplest Form
The method of this book can be summarized in one chain:
Source → Occurrence → Identity → Record → Relationships → Patterns → Model → Test → Result
That is, research does not proceed like this:
Idea about the heart → Searching for supporting verses → Building a pattern → Extracting meaning
But rather, it proceeds in the opposite direction:
Source → Comprehensive induction → Establishing facts → Comparing relationships → Discovering the pattern → Testing the pattern → Formulating the result
What is the "Textual Container"?
The textual container is the set of facts that have been previously identified according to a clear inductive criterion, so that one can say: This is the material we will study, these are its boundaries, and these are the identities of its elements.
If the subject of research is the root (Q-L-B), it is not enough to say: We collected the words of "heart" in the Qur'an. We must know:
What is the criterion for entering an occurrence?
What morphological structures are included?
What structures are excluded?
What is the source from which we took the list?
How many facts are there?
Where does each fact occur?
What is its textual form?
What is its position within the verse?
What is its morphological classification?
Can another person re-access and verify it?
Only then do we have a verifiable textual container.
What is Occurrence Identity?
One might think that identifying the word is sufficient to identify the occurrence. But a word like "heart" does not alone constitute a complete textual occurrence. The occurrence is not complete except with its coordinates. Therefore, the research adopted this chain:
text
Root → Lemma → Morphological Form → Verse → Word Position → Orthographic Occurrence
For example, it is not enough to say: "Heart" appears in Surah Al-Shura. We must specify the occurrence itself:
42:24:12
And its textual form:
قَلْبِكَ
Thus, the word moves from being a "linguistic example" to being a textual occurrence with a fixed identity.
IX. The Crisis of QLB-RM v0.5: When Error Reveals the Limits of Method
The audit of the QLB-RM v0.5 record revealed that the problem was not merely a numerical error correctable through local adjustment, but a flaw in the "identity of the container" itself.
It was discovered that the resource 11:24:4 was included in the list of resources, even though it does not exist in the reference source within the Noun qalb list. Conversely, the resource 83:14:5 exists in the source, but was missing from the record.
This might seem at first glance like a simple substitution of one resource for another while maintaining the same number, but this understanding underestimates the seriousness of the problem. The flaw is not in the number 132, but in the relationship between the number and the facts it represents. If a non-existent resource has entered the container, and an existing resource has left it, then the list of resources no longer represents the source. At this point, every conclusion built upon it becomes doubtful, no matter how logical it may seem afterward.
If a relationship is extracted from QLB-059, and the identity of QLB-059 itself is incorrect, then the relationship is not merely "incorrect information," but a relationship that has no subject in the corpus.
Hence the necessity of rebuilding QLB-RM from the source, not repairing it based on the previous version.
The Break with Local Correction
The previous error reveals a serious methodological danger: when the researcher has an expected sequence, they may unconsciously tend to preserve the structure of the sequence, then search for a resource that fills the missing slot. Here, the direction of knowledge is reversed: instead of:
text
Source → Sequence
It becomes:
text
Sequence → Search for source
This is the path that must be prevented methodologically. The rule is not:
"How do we maintain the number 132?"
But rather:
"How many facts does the source actually produce according to the adopted inclusion criterion?"
Therefore, the correct rebuilding of QLB-RM v0.6 does not begin from an attempt to modify v0.5, but from re-examining the source itself. Thus, the principle was established:
We do not correct the source according to the record; we correct the record according to the source.

How does the Qur'an describe the heart when it places it in different situations, what changes in these situations, and what remains constant?

The Heart in the Qur'anic System – The Final Statement:
The heart in the Qur'an is a central structural entity, whose relations are distributed across four main axes:
Axis of Reception (The Container): The heart is a container for internal contents (disease, deviation, faith, tranquility).
Axis of Affectedness (Objecthood): The heart receives external actions (sealing, stamping, binding, making).
Axis of Self-Action (Subjecthood): The heart emits self-actions (harshness, tranquility, humility, earning).
Axis of Stable Description (Attributive): The heart is described by stable states (sound, repentant, covered, sinful).
These four axes are not temporally linear; they are a network of possible relationships.
And the semantic core of the root (Q-L-B) is: structural transformation that changes the direction, state, or function of something.
XIV. The Synthetic Law of the Book
The human being in the Qur'anic system is not understood through the heart alone, nor the self alone, nor the spirit alone, but through a network of functions, states, and relationships. In this network, the heart is the axis of orientation, the self is the field of purification and obligation, the spirit is the source of life and support, revelation is the source of guidance, response is the point of transformation, and orientation is the final outcome of movement. The spirit and the concept of divine support remain areas that require independent induction before being incorporated into the final operational model.

The heart is the axis of cognitive functions: comprehension, understanding, remembrance, humility, tranquility, repentance, piety, response, rejection, deviation.
The heart is described in multiple states: sound, diseased, hardened, sealed, stamped, deviant, heedless, repentant.
The self (nafs) is the entity of obligation, with a defined capacity (wus') that God has made the measure of obligation.
The heart is a central structure that can turn toward or away from guidance.
What the Induction Establishes
The root (Q-L-B) occurs 168 times in the Qur'an, with 132 nominal occurrences (qalb/qulub).
132 verified relations extracted from a closed and verified corpus.
5 patterns: SUBJECT, LOCATIVE, OBJECT, PREDICATE, POSSESSIVE.
The semantic core: structural transformation that changes the direction, state, or function of something.
The Models Proposed
MODEL-01: Four axes (Reception, Affectedness, Self-Action, Stable Description).
MODEL-03: Integrative model of the heart, self, spirit, and revelation.
MODEL-04: The heart as a feedback system.

We began from the raw text, established the identity of each occurrence, extracted direct relationships, discovered recurring patterns, built the structural model, and tested the major hypothesis.
