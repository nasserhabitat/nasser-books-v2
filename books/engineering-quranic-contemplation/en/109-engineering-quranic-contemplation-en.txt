II. Translator’s Note: The Bridge of Meaning
This English edition is a condensed conceptual adaptation. It is not a word-for-word translation, but rather an "extraction of essence." It presents the core philosophical framework in accessible English, omitting the exhaustive linguistic debates and classical references found in the original Arabic text.
For the academic researcher: The original Arabic version remains the primary source for comprehensive linguistic analysis, detailed exegesis (Tafsir), and the complete bibliography.
Edition note. The Arabic edition is the primary reference for the author's fuller argument. The scope of its linguistic analysis, exegesis, and references must be assessed from that edition itself; these words do not certify an exhaustive classical survey or bibliography in the present book.
Reading this English edition
This book examines how a claim about the Quran can be investigated, questioned, and revised. Its central case is Adam, whose narrative connects learning, responsibility, temptation, error, repentance, and guidance. The reader needs no prior knowledge of Arabic to follow the argument. Where an argument depends on Arabic word structure, this edition explains the issue and preserves its uncertainty rather than treating an English rendering as linguistic proof.
The five parts follow the Arabic book's twenty chapters. Each chapter presents a complete condensed argument. Part One introduces the research tools in Chapters 1 to 4. Part Two investigates Adam's identity, responsibility, the names, and prostration in Chapters 5 to 8. Part Three examines choice, the tree, temptation, and recovery in Chapters 9 to 13. Part Four distinguishes creation, origination, coming into being, and the question of recreation in Chapters 14 to 16. Part Five teaches the reader to examine a claim and repeat the method independently in Chapters 17 to 20. Appendices A to E provide reference lists, a hypothesis register, a glossary, and a reusable research worksheet.
How to read the evidence labels
[Textual paraphrase] marks a brief English representation of a Quranic passage, accompanied by its chapter and verse reference. It is an interpretive aid, not the Arabic Quran itself, and must be checked against the cited passage. This edition does not reproduce a named published English translation.
[Observation] describes something a reader can inspect, such as the order of actions or a change of speaker. [Hypothesis] proposes an explanation that requires testing. [Candidate] identifies a proposal that has gained support while retaining unresolved conditions. [Stable finding] records a conclusion supported within the stated corpus and the tests performed. Stability remains limited to that scope and permits later revision. OPEN is a status attached to a claim whose investigation has not been completed; it is not an additional rank of evidence.
We also distinguish explanation and inference from an operational model. An explanation describes relationships; an inference derives a conclusion from stated premises; an operational model organizes a human practice, such as learning or reviewing a decision. A useful model does not establish the physical nature of Adam, the tree, or revelation.
References use the form 2:31, meaning chapter 2, verse 31 of the Quran. The Quranic text governs the investigation. The author's previous books can supply questions and tools, but each new conclusion requires evidence from its own corpus. The governing rule remains: Previous Book ≠ Evidence for New Corpus.
Major Introduction
Why Adam Is a Laboratory for Reflective Reading
This book begins with a provocative question: will Adam be created anew? Its purpose, however, is to discover what a carefully bounded inquiry can establish. Resurrection, the repetition of an original pattern, the recreation of a particular person, and a technological imitation of human abilities are different questions. Evidence for one cannot silently become evidence for all.
The author calls the method “the engineering of Quranic contemplation.” Engineering here means the deliberate organization of inquiry: preserve the text, define the question, assemble a corpus, describe relationships, propose explanations, and test their limits. It does not imply that the Quran is a manual of mechanical engineering. The author's “fiqh of the Quranic tongue” is an inquiry into how Quranic expressions function in their textual relationships; the term is broader here than jurisprudence alone.
Two methodological commitments guide this inquiry. Functional non-synonymy asks what a change of expression contributes, even where English translations use the same word. The law of balance places an expression, inference, or model in proportion to its context and supporting evidence. Neither commitment licenses us to invent differences or impose a complete conceptual system in advance.
Claim labels make the argument accountable. [Textual paraphrase] identifies an English restatement of a cited passage, rather than its Arabic wording. [Observation] describes something a reader can check. [Hypothesis] proposes an explanation requiring tests. [Candidate] marks a supported interpretation that remains provisional. [Stable finding] marks a bounded conclusion that has survived the tests actually conducted. OPEN records an unresolved question; it is a research status, not a failure.
Adam is a demanding test case because his textual network includes learning, communication, honor, enmity, covenant, forgetting, choice, wrongdoing, repentance, and guidance. No single feature can replace this whole network. Technical comparisons may help us ask questions, but they must answer to the passages they purport to explain.
The five parts move from research tools through Adam's relationships and choices to creation, renewed existence, and independent inquiry. Earlier books contribute questions and models, never substitute evidence: Previous Book ≠ Evidence for New Corpus. The aim is to teach readers how to determine what the text can support, rather than prescribe in advance what they must believe about Adam.
Part One
Tools for Reflective Inquiry
Chapter 1
How Does a Quranic Research Question Arise
A sentence does not become a research question merely because it ends with a question mark. “How were Adam's names computer codes?” has already assumed the equation it appears to investigate. A responsible question exposes its assumptions and permits the inquiry to reject them. We therefore begin by separating the possibilities contained in “Will Adam be created anew?”
The question could concern Adam's resurrection among humanity, a repetition of his formation, another being resembling him, or a machine bearing his name. These possibilities differ in subject, agent, and burden of proof. A statement about renewed creation does not by itself describe an industrial project. Naming a machine “Adam” does not make it the referent of a Quranic passage.
[Textual paraphrase] In 17:49–51, people question their resurrection after becoming bones and fragments; the response directs them to the One who originated them the first time. [Observation] The pronouns connect the proposed return to the people making the objection. The passage addresses resurrection. Applying it specifically to Adam requires an explicit inferential step; applying it to a robot requires another step for which this context supplies no direct support.
We can now ask manageable questions. Which passages connect Adam with creation? Which describe renewed creation or another emergence? Do any specify the historical repetition of Adam's earthly experience? These questions precede a [Hypothesis], such as the proposal that certain relationships in Adam's story can inform a contemporary model of learning. That proposal differs from claiming that the Quran predicts a particular machine. Educational application and textual prediction require different evidence.
A research question must also permit resistance. If the teaching of names supposedly entails complete knowledge of cosmic laws, what limits does the passage place on that knowledge? If knowledge guarantees correct action, how can the same textual network contain forgetting and disobedience? These are tests arising within the inquiry, not distractions from it.
The researcher records the question, its possible assumptions, and every justified enlargement of the corpus. Passages on resurrection may belong to this inquiry; a new investigation of the physics of angels does not automatically belong. If the question changes, the record should show why.
[Stable finding] A question specifies what we seek; a hypothesis proposes a relation; a result reports what the test supports. A striking question has no right to demand an equally striking answer.
Chapter 2
Building the Corpus Before Building Meaning
A corpus is a defined collection of passages assembled to examine a particular question. Its inclusion rules, classifications, and limits must be explicit. Selecting a few supportive verses is not a complete induction. Another researcher should be able to repeat the collection and understand why each passage was included or excluded.
For Adam, the name provides a controlled starting point. [Observation] It occurs twenty-five times across nine surahs: five in al-Baqarah, two in Al Imran, one in al-Ma'idah, seven in al-A'raf, two in al-Isra', one in al-Kahf, one in Maryam, five in Ta Ha, and one in Ya Sin. In this collection the occurrence count and verse count coincide. They need not coincide elsewhere: the root sh-j-r has twenty-seven occurrences in twenty-six verses because 7:22 contains two occurrences. A word, a verse, and a contextual passage are different counting units.
Classification precedes interpretation. A direct mention of Adam, an address to him, an expression referring to prostration before him, “the children of Adam,” “the two sons of Adam,” and references to his descendants perform different grammatical and narrative functions. None should automatically be treated as a definition of his name.
[Textual paraphrase] In 7:27, the children of Adam are warned against satanic deception through reference to what happened to their parents. [Observation] The passage establishes an instructive connection between generations. It does not establish that every physical detail of the parental experience repeats in every descendant.
The inquiry therefore uses four corpus layers. The direct corpus contains the twenty-five named occurrences. The contextual corpus includes surrounding passages needed to understand them, such as 2:30–39 and 20:115–123. The root corpus collects forms of a root when a chapter examines its usage. The resistant corpus deliberately tests an interpretation against passages that could limit it. A proposal that “tree” means knowledge, for example, must encounter passages about oil, eating, pens, and growth.
Freezing the collection prevents the researcher from continually rescuing a favored hypothesis through selective additions. It does not forbid correcting an omission. Corrections must be recorded and their consequences assessed. The corpus remains open to documented repair while the inference remains accountable.
[Stable finding] Accurate collection is indispensable, but a correct list alone does not produce a complete reading. The researcher must preserve both the reproducibility of the sample and the relationships that make its passages intelligible.
Chapter 3
From the Word to the Network of Relationships
A word is an entry point into a passage, not an isolated semantic island. Studying “Adam” without teaching, communication, covenant, and repentance separates the name from the construction of the character. Yet turning all these relationships into the lexical meaning of the name produces the opposite error. Network reading preserves the distinction between whom a name identifies and what the text says about that person.
The network in al-Baqarah contains different relations. Adam receives teaching, is directed to communicate names, stands within the episode of commanded prostration, receives a prohibition, and later receives words from his Lord. These connections are not interchangeable. Teaching is explicitly attributed to him; his association with the announced khalifah is contextual; the claim that teaching exclusively caused the prostration is a further causal hypothesis.
[Textual paraphrase] In 2:31–33, Adam is taught names and then communicates names when directed to do so. [Observation] The passage supports a movement from receiving knowledge to demonstrating it through communication. It does not state that this gives Adam control of every physical law. Drawing a network connecting knowledge and authority cannot supply the missing evidence.
Sequence also differs from causation. Teaching precedes prostration in al-Baqarah, while 15:29 and 38:72 place the command in connection with formation and the breathing of spirit. [Hypothesis] Knowledge may be one dimension of Adam's position. The stronger proposition that every presentation of prostration has teaching as its sole cause does not account for the other contexts. The law of balance requires each relationship to retain its actual evidential weight.
The same restraint applies to character. Forgetting in a particular covenant episode does not define Adam as “forgetfulness.” Teaching does not define him as absolute knowledge. A technical metaphor such as “program” may organize an explanation, but adjusting that metaphor whenever error appears cannot establish an actual computer system in the narrative.
Local networks can then be compared: covenant in Ta Ha, enmity and exposure in al-A'raf, selection in Al Imran, and the address to Adam's descendants elsewhere. Comparison does not require constructing a single exhaustive chronology from every detail. Different emphases remain objects of study.
[Candidate] Descent from Adam combines belonging with responsibility rather than guaranteeing correct conduct. [Stable finding] Contextual relation is not lexical identity; narrative succession is not sufficient proof of causation; and a character's function is not automatically the meaning of his name.
Chapter 4
Mathani and the Meanings of Letters The Tool and Its Limits
The author's mathani system explores possible functional relationships within a word's letter structure. It can generate questions that an initial reading overlooks. Its capacity to generate an interpretation, however, does not establish that interpretation. In this book mathani is a proposed analytical tool, distinct from morphological root classification and direct contextual evidence.
Consider three procedures retained from the Arabic investigation. The root sh-j-r supplies a collection rule for tree-related forms; “shj + r” proposes a different internal analysis. The root kh-l-q groups creation-related forms; “kh-l + l-q” proposes overlapping pairs. “ʾā + dm,” proposed for Adam, separates non-overlapping components. These are not identical operations. If all are permitted without prior rules, the interpreter acquires considerable freedom to produce a desired answer.
The researcher must therefore specify letter representation, segmentation, overlap, and the reason for choosing a procedure. A favorable result for one procedure does not validate another. Nor does a shared sequence of letters prove that all words containing it share a single function.
Suggested letter-functions such as extension, containment, or flow remain [Hypothesis] claims. They require independent evidence. If the meaning of “dm” is inferred from a preferred reading of Adam and then used to prove that reading, the argument is circular. Extremely general functions also need discrimination: a model that fits nearly everything may distinguish almost nothing. Would rearranging the letters leave the proposed explanation unchanged?
[Textual paraphrase] In 39:23, the Quran is described with the term mathani. This occurrence does not itself establish that universal segmentation into letter-pairs is the procedure intended by that verse. The project's name for its tool and its interpretation of the Quranic term are separate questions.
Testing proceeds in both directions. A forward test asks whether the proposed core explains the collected occurrences without strained additions. A reverse test asks whether the claimed function specifically points to that structure, and how alternative explanations perform. A modest educational analogy and a universal law of letter-meaning do not bear the same evidential burden.
The status remains OPEN when segmentation lacks a prior standard, the resistant sample is incomplete, or alternatives have not been distinguished. This preserves the suggestion for further inquiry without treating it as established knowledge.
[Stable finding] Mathani analysis here generates and organizes hypotheses. Its results depend on contextual induction and reverse testing. Segmentation alone establishes neither Adam's nature, the identity of the prohibited tree, nor the physical mechanism of creation.
Part Two
The Adam Laboratory and the Quranic Portrait
Chapter 5
What Does “Adam” Mean
The apparently simple question of Adam's meaning contains several questions. Whom does the name identify? What is its linguistic origin? What functions does its bearer perform? Could its letter structure disclose a shared functional core? Success in one inquiry does not automatically settle the others. A proper name can have a clear referent while its derivation remains uncertain.
[Observation] The direct passages identify Adam as a character in the network of teaching, prostration, covenant, and repentance. Direct address, reference to his spouse, and expressions involving his descendants support that reading. “Children of Adam” extends the discourse to a related community; it does not make Adam a generic word for every human in every occurrence.
[Textual paraphrase] In 3:59, Adam appears in the comparison concerning Jesus, with reference to creation from dust. The passage identifies a feature of creation. It does not state that the name Adam derives from dust. A conceptual association with earth may motivate an etymological question, but it cannot replace linguistic evidence.
The Arabic draft considers connections with adim, associated with the surface of the earth, and udmah. These are [Hypothesis] proposals requiring independent documentation of forms, usage, and derivational rules. Their status remains OPEN within the present corpus. Deriving a name from any attribute of its bearer would remove the inquiry's ability to distinguish between competing explanations.
The mathani proposal “ʾā + dm” faces a similar requirement. The draft associates its second component with ideas including continuity, materiality, earth, and the flow of life. If blood is chosen when bloodshed appears, continuity when descendants appear, and earth when dust appears, the proposed core may merely rename evidence already known. It must be defined independently before application.
Testing should include selection in 3:33, covenant in 20:115, and honor in 17:70, rather than relying on an attractive association with bloodshed in 2:30. The latter verse does not itself name Adam. Connecting adjacent contexts is legitimate, but turning that connection into derivation is an additional claim.
[Candidate] The Adamic model developed in this book brings together learning, choice, responsibility, and restoration of direction. Its support comes from the network of relationships, not from established letter-functions. It is a description of the character's roles rather than a dictionary definition.
[Stable finding] The name's reference is secure within the direct passages. Proposed etymologies and mathani segmentation remain [Hypothesis, OPEN]. They cannot serve as settled foundations for interpreting Adam's subsequent responsibilities.
Chapter 6
Adam and Khilafah From Context to Function
The draft places khilafah at the center of Adam's project and relates it to responsible knowledge and resistance to corruption. For English readers, the Arabic term must retain room for appointment, succession, and responsibility. Translating it immediately as unlimited “dominion” or divine “representation” would settle questions the inquiry has yet to examine.
[Textual paraphrase] In 2:30, God announces a khalifah on earth. The passage then names Adam in the episodes of teaching, communication, prostration, residence, prohibition, and repentance. [Observation] Adam is the named human figure in this connected context. [Candidate] He is the immediate contextual referent of its announced project. This is a strong contextual inference, rather than a quotation saying explicitly, “Adam is the khalifah.”
The distinction matters. It does not independently determine whom the khalifah succeeds, establish creatures before Adam, or demonstrate representation on God's behalf. Each proposition carries an additional burden of proof.
Comparison extends to nine nominal occurrences of khalifah, khulafa', and khala'if; this is a bounded sample, not every form of the root kh-l-f. [Textual paraphrase] In 38:26, David's appointment is connected with judging in truth and resisting desire. In 6:165, succession on earth is connected with differences in what people receive and with testing. [Observation] The sample connects position with standards of conduct, succession, and accountability. It does not establish unrestricted control of matter or guaranteed immortality.
The draft proposes different emphases for khulafa' and khala'if: active historical agents in the former, positions and testing in the latter. Some passages encourage this comparison, but 10:73 provides resistance. Khala'if occurs there in a collective historical succession following rescue and destruction. An exclusive division assigning history to one form and testing to the other therefore fails. A possible difference in emphasis remains [Hypothesis, OPEN]; functional non-synonymy does not supply a difference before the evidence does.
The author's expression “luminous khilafah” can designate a model of increasingly informed and responsible action. That designation does not establish a change in human biological nature, angelic identity, or mastery of physical dimensions. Similarly, entropy is an analogy unless a measurable physical system has been specified.
[Stable finding] Khilafah in this sample connects succession and appointment with responsibility, truth, and testing. [Candidate] The connected Adam passage locates him within this project. The model's further technical and metaphysical extensions remain separate, unresolved propositions.
Chapter 7
Teaching the Names From Knowledge to Communication
The teaching of names is central to the draft's account of Adam. The passage warrants close attention precisely because it can attract expansive interpretations: names as properties, codes, algorithms, or the operating system of the universe. These proposals have different meanings and burdens of proof. They must be examined individually.
[Textual paraphrase] In 2:31–33, God teaches Adam the names, presents the relevant referents, and asks the angels to communicate their names. The angels acknowledge that their knowledge is limited to what God has taught them. Adam then communicates the names when directed to do so. The passage ends with a declaration of God's knowledge of the unseen and of what the addressees disclose and conceal.
[Observation] The sequence involves teaching, presentation, acknowledged limits, and communication. The taught knowledge has a demonstrated function in that setting. [Candidate] It enables identification and informative communication there. This is narrower than the assertion that Adam possesses every law of the universe.
Calling the names knowledge is defensible when it means that teaching them is a cognitive act. It becomes overextension if every form of knowledge is thereby equated with names. “All” qualifies the names mentioned; it cannot silently be transferred to an unlimited field of possible knowledge. Likewise, knowing a name and knowing all the properties of its referent are not equivalent accomplishments.
The proposition “names are properties” therefore remains [Hypothesis, OPEN]. Properties may help explain identification, but the researcher must distinguish the meaning of a name from what knowing its referent might involve. “Knowledge enabling communication” remains closer to the observable relationship while leaving the detailed content unresolved.
Technical comparisons can help at a modest level. A code may illustrate a relation between a sign and information. An algorithm, however, refers to specified procedures, which the passage does not supply. An operating system would make a still stronger claim about mechanism or nature. We cannot prove that Adam was programmed because names were codes, then prove that names were codes because Adam was programmed.
The wider network supplies resistance: the taught Adam subsequently forgets and disobeys. More decisively, the comprehensive knowledge of the unseen asserted at the passage's end belongs to God, not Adam. Transferring that attribute to the learner exceeds the evidence.
[Stable finding] Teaching and communication are explicit relationships. Their value need not depend on equating human knowledge with divine omniscience. Algorithmic and cosmic operating-system readings do not become established conclusions of this corpus.
Chapter 8
Prostration and Iblis What Changes in Adam's Network
The command to prostrate before Adam recurs across the story's network and is connected with Iblis's refusal. It offers a setting for examining command, response, pride, and enmity. Reading it as the submission of physical forces to human technology requires additional evidence; prostration cannot simply be replaced with technological harnessing.
[Textual paraphrase] In 2:34, the angels respond to the command, while Iblis refuses and acts arrogantly. In 18:50, Iblis is identified as belonging to the jinn and departing from his Lord's command. [Observation] His appearance within the episode's exception does not erase the latter identification. A network must preserve both the episode's relation and the stated description.
Iblis's argument also requires attention to the speaker. He compares materials of creation and claims superiority. The reported argument is evidence of his reasoning within the narrative, not a divine judgment endorsing it. This distinction will matter again when the deceiver promises immortality through the tree. A character's promise must not be promoted into the narrator's factual statement.
The cause of prostration likewise needs restraint. Teaching and communication precede the command in al-Baqarah. [Textual paraphrase] In 15:29 and 38:72, the command is presented in relation to formation and the breathing of spirit. Sequence in one passage cannot establish an exclusive explanation for every presentation. [Hypothesis] Teaching contributes to the portrayal of Adam's position; its status as the sole cause is unsupported by this comparison.
The refusal develops into active enmity. [Textual paraphrase] In 20:117, Adam and his spouse are warned about the enemy and the danger to their situation. The network therefore moves from refusal to efforts to redirect action. Describing this as “disruption of a path” can organize an educational model, but it does not exhaust the narrative or identify Iblis with a physical energy.
The draft's suggestion that applying a physical law amounts to making an angel prostrate remains an analogy. A literal identification would have to specify which angel corresponds to which force and explain discourse, knowledge, command, and response. Numerical resemblance between paired, triple, or quadruple wings and imagined physical dimensions supplies no missing connection.
[Stable finding] The corpus establishes the command, angelic response, Iblis's refusal and arrogance, and subsequent enmity. It establishes neither teaching as the sole cause of prostration nor angels as known physical forces. The next inquiry turns to covenant and Adam's own choice under warning.
Part Three
Exploring Choice in the Garden Tree and Error
Chapter 9
Covenant, Forgetfulness, and Resolve
Adam’s story distinguishes receiving knowledge from remaining faithful to it at a moment of choice. This distinction challenges two reductions: that knowledge automatically produces right action, and that one failure defines a person permanently. The chapter examines a particular narrated situation rather than constructing a complete psychology of Adam or treating forgetfulness as the meaning of his name.
[Textual paraphrase] God had previously entrusted Adam with a covenant; Adam forgot, and the passage reports that resolve was not found in him in that situation (20:115). Later, the narrative describes eating from the tree, their nakedness becoming apparent, disobedience, and loss of direction (20:121). [Observation] These descriptions occupy different positions in the narrative. Forgetting concerns the covenant; disobedience concerns the relation between action and divine direction. Their proximity does not make them interchangeable terms. A comprehensive definition of each would require a wider corpus.
The warning about an enemy precedes the temptation (20:117). Knowing that danger exists therefore differs from holding the warning in view when a tempting offer appears. [Candidate] The contrast between taught knowledge and sustained commitment is a central educational relationship in Adam’s story. We may use it to distinguish receiving information, retaining guidance, and acting consistently with it. Such distinctions form a human analytical model; they do not demonstrate the presence of a computer memory, processor, or execution system in Adam.
Nor should forgetfulness erase the narrative’s description of disobedience. Conversely, disobedience should not erase forgetfulness. [Hypothesis, OPEN] The attractiveness of the offer and failure to remain firm in the covenant may help describe the movement toward the violation. This remains an interpretation of the sequence, not an exhaustive diagnosis of intention or responsibility. Preserving several descriptions allows the text to resist a theory that explains everything through a single mechanism.
The next verse introduces divine choosing, acceptance, and guidance (20:122), while 2:37 describes Adam receiving words from his Lord. These are resistant passages for any model that freezes Adam at the point of failure. [Stable finding] The corpus presents knowledge, vulnerability, wrongdoing, and renewed direction within one continuing relationship. An educational application follows: prior understanding does not remove the need to examine a decision, and an error does not eliminate the possibility of correction. This application remains a limited working model. It does not claim that every human failure arises from forgetfulness or that every recovery follows an identical sequence.
Chapter 10
The Tree From Plant to Branching Structure
Can the Quran’s uses of the tree illuminate a shared pattern of connection, branching, and entanglement? The author’s draft proposes this possibility. Testing it requires beginning with the actual occurrences rather than assigning a symbolic meaning in advance. A structural resemblance may help connect contexts without replacing the ordinary object that those contexts describe.
[Observation] The selected morphological index records twenty-seven occurrences of the root sh-j-r: one verb and twenty-six nouns. This is a reproducible count, not a finding about meaning. The corpus includes Adam’s tree, trees used in explicit comparisons, an olive tree, trees imagined as pens, a tree associated with a pledge, and a verb used for matters arising between disputants. A theory must explain why these settings differ as well as what they may share.
[Textual paraphrase] A tree provides oil and food-related benefit (23:20); trees are imagined as pens in a comparison concerning God’s words (31:27); believers make a pledge beneath a tree (48:18). Elsewhere, a good word is compared with a good tree, while a bad word is compared with an uprooted tree (14:24–26). [Observation] The comparison is explicitly a comparison. Its force depends on a relationship between distinct things; it does not establish that every tree means a word, knowledge, or an information system. Roots and branches can clarify stability and extension without dissolving the plant into its illustration.
The verb in 4:65 appears in a setting of judgment concerning what arises between people. [Candidate, OPEN] Entanglement and branching may help describe a connection between this use and the tree’s visible structure. Yet this proposed common pattern is broad. Many connected or complex structures are not called trees. The proposal therefore needs limits and independent criteria. Adding a new component whenever an occurrence resists the definition can make the definition too flexible to test.
The draft also explores a mathani division of the Arabic form, “shj + r.” [Hypothesis, OPEN] This division requires evidence for its components and a consistent explanation of why that boundary is chosen. The observed branching of plants cannot independently establish a letter’s universal function. [Stable finding] The corpus contains botanical, comparative, and dispute-related uses whose contexts must remain visible. Connected branching is a promising descriptive pattern, but no exclusive definition has been established. In particular, the corpus does not warrant equating the tree with knowledge. That restraint prepares the next chapter’s examination of Adam’s particular tree.
Chapter 11
Adam’s Tree A Branching Point in Choice
Adam’s tree enters a field of permission through a specific prohibition. The immediate question is what role that boundary plays in the narrative, rather than which botanical species or abstract symbol the tree must represent. The selected passages do not name its species or identify it as a tree of knowledge. An absence of specification is a limit on our answer, not an invitation to fill the gap freely.
[Textual paraphrase] Adam and his spouse may inhabit the garden and eat, but must not approach the specified tree (2:35). The tempter presents the prohibition as withholding a desirable condition, such as becoming angels or attaining lasting existence (7:20). [Observation] The offer changes the apparent meaning of the boundary: direction becomes an obstacle to an alleged benefit. The speaker must remain identifiable. A claim made by the tempter is evidence about the temptation; it is not, by itself, an authoritative explanation of God’s purpose.
After tasting, their nakedness becomes apparent and the pair attempt to cover themselves (7:22). One may interpret this bodily exposure as revealing vulnerability. However, learning something through an event does not establish that the object involved means knowledge. The thing, its narrative function, and a possible cognitive consequence are separate claims. The earlier teaching of the names (2:31–33) also resists the assertion that all knowledge originates at the tree. Nor may the event be renamed a necessary liberation of consciousness while its prohibition, deception, and painful consequences disappear.
[Candidate] The tree functions as a branching point in choice: observing the boundary or crossing it, retaining the warning or accepting the offer. This is a description of its position in the narrative. It neither means that the Arabic word for tree universally signifies a decision nor removes eating and tasting from the account. The narrower model can organize relationships without claiming to reveal the tree’s material identity.
The paired forms in the accounts also matter. Both Adam and his spouse appear in the prohibition and its consequences, even where another passage foregrounds Adam. Their shared presence does not authorize invented assignments of blame or a general theory of gender. [Stable finding] The tree is a boundary targeted by temptation, and crossing it leads to narrated consequences. [Hypothesis, OPEN] Its equation with knowledge remains unestablished. Its species and unspecified nature remain OPEN. Reading the tree as a decision point preserves more of the narrative while adding fewer assumptions than an identity claim about knowledge or machinery.
Chapter 12
Lasting Life, Dominion, and the Garden
The temptation becomes intelligible against the condition it addresses. Adam is warned about losing a state of protection, while the offer promises permanence and possession that will not decay. This chapter examines that contrast within the selected narrative. It does not attempt to settle every Quranic use of the garden, Paradise, or the life to come.
[Textual paraphrase] Adam is assured freedom from hunger, nakedness, thirst, and exposure to the sun in the described condition (20:118–119). [Observation] The passage concerns recognizable needs and hardships. Protection from hunger does not demonstrate the absence of a body or the elimination of biology; eating remains part of the subsequent narrative. Likewise, the description alone does not locate the garden geographically or establish its complete identity with every other garden mentioned in the Quran. Those larger questions remain OPEN here.
[Textual paraphrase] The tempter offers to direct Adam toward a tree of lasting life and dominion that does not wear away (20:120). The related account adds the prospect of becoming angels or joining those who endure, accompanied by a claim to sincere advice (7:20–21). [Observation] The attraction combines alleged benefit with alleged trustworthiness. Its terms must not be collapsed: continuation, dominion, and freedom from decay are related within the offer but are not interchangeable descriptions.
Above all, the offer must retain its speaker. The phrase describing a tree of lasting life appears within temptation. It does not establish an objective power of the tree to grant immortality. Likewise, the prospect of becoming angels is not a divine declaration that human development culminates in an angelic transformation. A researcher may explore the human desire to overcome limitation as a philosophical question, but cannot convert the tempter’s claim into evidence of the intended goal of Adam’s creation.
The narrated outcome does not report fulfillment of lasting life or inexhaustible dominion. It reports their nakedness becoming apparent, efforts to cover themselves, disobedience, and then renewed divine choosing and guidance (20:121–122). [Stable finding] The promised benefit and the narrated consequence differ. From that contrast we can build a limited working model: identify who promises the benefit, what path is proposed, which boundary must be crossed, and what outcome actually follows. It may illuminate contemporary promises of digital immortality or unlimited power. Such use is an ethical application, not a claim that a particular technology is predicted by these verses. The distinction keeps the text’s relationships available without making them dependent on a modern scenario.
Chapter 13
From Disobedience to Restoring Direction
A reading that ends at disobedience makes Adam an example of failure without continuation. A reading that starts with acceptance and removes the violation loses the meaning of correction. The narrative holds both together. Restoring direction neither licenses the earlier action retrospectively nor makes wrongdoing the permanent identity of the person who acted.
[Textual paraphrase] Adam receives words from his Lord, and God turns toward him in acceptance (2:37). The pair acknowledge wronging themselves and their need for forgiveness and mercy (7:23). [Observation] The agent matters: God’s turning toward Adam and the pair’s acknowledgment are different relationships. They may be studied together, but the selected evidence does not explicitly identify every detail of the received words with the wording of that prayer. A strong contextual connection still differs from a direct statement of identity.
[Textual paraphrase] After the report of disobedience, Adam’s Lord chooses him, turns toward him, and guides him (20:122). Guidance then enters the account of the subsequent path: following divine guidance is associated with avoiding loss of direction and misery (20:123). [Observation] Choosing, acceptance, and guidance are distinct actions in the sequence. Their association does not reduce them to one psychological or technical process. Nor is guidance merely possession of information: the following passage connects it with following and action.
The author’s expression “the Adam program” can therefore be retained as a working model for learning and correction. It organizes direction, a boundary, temptation, decision, consequence, renewed reception, and restored movement. It does not assert a material code or an autonomous mechanism that repairs itself. The narrative’s relationship with the Lord must remain part of the model. Nor does correction establish that the violation was a necessary or intended condition of advancement. Learning after a mistake does not mean that the original instruction required making that mistake.
The addresses to the children of Adam connect the story with clothing, vigilance against temptation, measured conduct, and the reception of messengers (7:26–27, 31, 35). The covenant also appears in collective address (36:60). [Candidate] Adam’s narrative can inform education beyond an individual biography, while the distinct settings remain intact. Its application may support knowledge governance: recognize a limit, identify the source of a claim, examine the promised benefit, record an error, and revise the path. These are human formulations derived from relationships, not a Quranic checklist. [Stable finding] Adam’s narrated trajectory includes renewed reception and guidance; it cannot be reduced to either initial knowledge or a single failure.
Part Four
From Adam to Creation and Coming into Being
Chapter 14
Adam’s Creation What Does the Text Establish
The draft brings together dust, clay, human formation, shaping, proportioning, and breathing. These terms invite comparison, but their accumulation does not itself produce a complete physical mechanism. We must first distinguish a passage naming Adam, passages connected to him through the command to prostrate, and statements about human creation more generally. The strength of each connection should remain visible.
[Textual paraphrase] In a comparison concerning Jesus, the Quran explicitly names Adam and describes his creation from dust, followed by the divine command to be (3:59). [Observation] The passage establishes a stated material relation and its divine agency. It does not specify duration, laboratory stages, genetic organization, or a computing device. The order of its expressions matters, but that order cannot be converted into a numerical timeline without further evidence.
Passages in 15:28–29 and 38:71–72 describe a human being, clay-related formation, proportioning, breathing, and the command to prostrate. Their connection with Adam has substantial contextual support from other prostration passages; Adam’s name nevertheless does not occur in every cited verse. Dust, clay, sounding clay, and altered mud should not be exchanged freely as if no difference existed. Yet preserving their differences does not establish a fixed chemical sequence. The same discipline applies to creating, shaping, proportioning, and breathing: their distinct positions deserve study, not an immediate translation into assembly, joining parts, and starting a processor.
In 7:11, the discourse moves from plural address concerning creation and shaping to Adam’s named place in the prostration scene. [Observation] This change of reference must be recorded. A theory of species or populations cannot settle its meaning by assuming the conclusion in advance. Contextual connections permit questions; they do not automatically determine a biological history.
The draft also proposes the mathani division “kh-l + l-q” and associates creation with material arrangement. [Hypothesis, OPEN] A universal version of that claim meets resistance: 29:17 uses the root in relation to fabricating falsehood. Meanwhile, 25:2 places creation and determining in its own stated sequence. These passages do not eliminate every possible connection between creation and arrangement, but resist confinement to one physical operation. [Stable finding] The direct passage states Adam’s creation from dust, while related passages supply further descriptions at different levels of reference. The detailed material mechanism remains OPEN in this corpus. Technical models can stimulate inquiry, but their usefulness does not make them a description supplied by the text itself.
Chapter 15
Creating, Originating, Assigning, and Bringing Forth
Several Quranic expressions are often gathered under the broad English idea of bringing something into existence. The author’s principle of functional non-synonymy asks us to examine their differences before treating them as equivalents. English glosses such as creating, originating, assigning, and bringing forth remain provisional aids. They cannot substitute for the contextual study of khalq, fatr, jaʿl, and nashʾa.
[Observation] In the resurrection discussion, the question refers to new creation (17:49), while the answer points to the one who originated the questioners the first time (17:51). This difference warrants investigation. It does not establish that creating means assembling material while originating means emergence from nothing without stages. [Textual paraphrase] Other passages attribute beginning and repeating creation to God (30:27; 21:104). The argument concerns divine power, not a condition that humans must first discover a method for storing or rebuilding themselves.
The verb jaʿl occurs in assigning a successor on earth (2:30), making angels messengers (35:1), and providing fire from a green tree (36:80). [Candidate] In these settings it may foreground a condition, relationship, or benefit. Its general semantic core, however, requires a larger corpus. The reference to making darkness and light in 6:1 resists a rigid division that assigns all material existence to khalq and all function to jaʿl. Resistance limits an exclusive rule without proving that the terms have no functional differences.
References to the first and later bringing forth connect reflection on beginnings with the life to come (29:20; 56:62). They do not identify that later condition with a current technological era or digital replication. Likewise, distinguishing creation, provision, death, and life in 30:40 preserves the narrated actions without revealing the design of a cosmic computational system. A contemporary model may organize our questions about transition; it cannot supply the missing mechanism as though the Quran had stated it.
One proposed draft distinction treats originating as fixed and creation as changeable, then cites 30:30 under the first category. [Observation] The cited phrase actually concerns God’s creation. This mismatch must be recorded and explained; it cannot silently prove an exclusive opposition between the terms. [Stable finding] The selected passages distinguish expressions in their contexts and affirm beginning, repetition, and subsequent bringing forth. [Hypothesis, OPEN] Universal letter-based definitions and mappings to hardware, protocols, or applications remain unestablished. The method preserves potentially meaningful differences while refusing to make an attractive technical classification stronger than the corpus that is supposed to test it.
Chapter 16
Will Adam Be Created Anew
The initiating question becomes more precise after the preceding comparisons. “Created anew” might mean resurrection, repetition of Adam’s original experience, simulation of some human functions, or the claim that technological civilization is the later bringing forth described by the Quran. These are different propositions. Answering them together would allow evidence for one to confer unjustified authority on the others.
[Stable finding] The twenty-five occurrences of Adam’s name present a named figure within creation, teaching, covenant, choice, and correction. They do not state that his creation will be repeated in earthly history as a technical model. The resurrection passages address God’s power to begin and bring forth again. Including Adam within the general resurrection of humanity is an inference from that general address, not a special statement that the tree’s test or the teaching of the names will recur in identical detail.
[Textual paraphrase] Questioners object to being brought forth after becoming bones and remains. The reply invokes even stone, iron, or something they consider harder to restore, then points to the one who originated them first (17:49–51). The next verse turns toward the day when they are called (17:52). [Observation] The argument answers an objection through divine power. It does not make resurrection depend on a human discovery or technological reconstruction.
The draft explores the movement of heads in 17:51 as the first activation of reconstructed bodies. The immediate context resists that reading: the movement is directed toward the addressee and accompanies asking when the event will occur; the day of calling is then introduced in 17:52. [Stable finding] Here the head movement belongs to the disputants’ response in the argument, not a narrated first motion after resurrection. Similarly, iron does not by itself identify a robot or computing apparatus, and stone cannot simply replace the bodily questioners with rigid ideas.
A constructed machine may illustrate how parts can be assembled, but that analogy establishes neither personal identity nor resurrection nor transfer of consciousness. Those questions require their own scientific and philosophical inquiry. [Hypothesis, OPEN] Identifying the technological age with the Quranic later bringing forth remains unsupported by this corpus. Simulation is instead a human project assessed through its methods and limits. The resulting answer preserves the resurrection discourse while declining to invent a special technological future for Adam. This is a substantive outcome: a single dramatic question has become four examinable claims, each carrying its own burden of evidence.
Part Five
Becoming an Independent Researcher
Chapter 17
Examining a Bold Hypothesis
The proposal that Adam's tree is knowledge brings several features into an attractive pattern: eating, newly exposed vulnerability, branching, language, and awareness. Such a proposal can generate useful questions. Its attractiveness, however, does not establish its truth. We must identify what kind of claim it makes and what each supporting passage can actually bear.
“The tree is knowledge” asserts identity. If it concerns every Quranic occurrence of the tree root, it must explain the complete root corpus. If it concerns only Adam's tree, it must justify that restriction while preserving prohibition, tasting, eating, and consequence. If it is an educational analogy, it must say so. A hypothesis cannot claim generality when collecting support, become local when encountering resistance, and become metaphorical whenever proof is requested.
[Observation] Exposure follows tasting in Adam's story. This establishes a relationship between an action and its consequence; it does not name the tree's essence. Likewise, the comparison of a good word to a good tree in 14:24 supplies a comparison, not an equation between knowledge and every tree. The hypothesis also encounters knowledge preceding the prohibited action: Adam has already received instruction in the names. Redefining the proposal as “a particular knowledge” requires independent grounds for identifying that knowledge.
Other passages resist the general equation: a tree yielding oil and food (23:20), trees imagined as pens (31:27), and a pledge made beneath a tree (48:18). Calling each “knowledge in another form” adds interpretations without specifying evidence. A claim that can absorb every objection through an undefined meaning becomes difficult to test. The verbal occurrence concerning dispute in 4:65 also distinguishes a possible structural idea of entanglement from a universal cognitive identity.
A narrower proposal survives better: Adam's tree occupies a branching point within a decision. [Candidate] This describes its position between respecting a boundary and crossing it. It does not replace the tree with an abstract decision or remove eating from the account. Its explanatory value lies in organizing the sequence of prohibition, persuasion, action, and consequence.
[Stable finding] The examined evidence does not establish tree–knowledge identity. This is a finding about the limits of the argument, not a final identification of the tree's species or nature. The record preserves the stronger proposal and explains its revision. Research advances here by making a claim smaller and more accountable, rather than by continuously enlarging its reach.
Chapter 18
Reverse Testing and Resistant Passages
A search that collects only favorable examples can make a broad hypothesis appear persuasive. Reverse testing asks whether it distinguishes anything. If branching is proposed as a tree's semantic function, does branching identify this word rather than many other structures? If a proposed letter analysis reproduces meanings already supplied by the narrative, what independent explanatory work has it done?
Forward testing begins with a fixed formulation and applies it to every occurrence in the declared corpus. We distinguish direct fit, fit requiring additional assumptions, and resistance requiring a changed proposal. A botanical tree visibly offers a relation between origin and branches; the dispute-related verb in 4:65 needs a different contextual account of entanglement. Reporting both as an unqualified success hides the difference that research should explain.
Reverse testing begins with the proposed function and searches beyond the target expression. The examined corpus does not establish that every connected or branching structure is called a tree. Likewise, learning, continuity, and descent occur beyond the name Adam. These findings do not make the observations worthless. They prevent a recurrent function from becoming a sufficient definition of a word or name. Contextual association, a linguistic core, and analogy carry different evidential burdens.
The author's double-reverse procedure works through both directions, compares an alternative, and returns to the original occurrences to identify what remains distinctive. This is a practical research procedure, not a universal requirement that every meaning and word form a necessary-and-sufficient pair. For the proposed analysis of Adam as ءا + دم, a comparison with the narrative network asks whether the letter analysis adds something beyond creation, instruction, commitment, and correction already visible in the passages.
Resistant examples should be selected before judgment wherever possible. The tree yielding oil challenges a cognitive equation. The collective succession in 10:73 challenges an exclusive division between khulafāʾ and khalāʾif. The speakers' question and the subsequent summons in 17:51–52 challenge the claim that their movement describes the activation of resurrected bodies. These are tests within the evidence, not distractions from it.
[Stable finding] Compatibility differs from distinctiveness, and a useful teaching model differs from an established semantic law. A revised hypothesis requires a fresh test and a recorded version. Failure to find a counterexample is inconclusive when the sample is incomplete or the proposal changes at every difficulty. OPEN preserves the question while identifying what further evidence is needed.
Chapter 19
The Claim Card Findings and Open Questions
A cautious proposal can become an assumed fact as a manuscript develops. “The names may be codes” can quietly turn into “Adam possessed the source code.” No new evidence has justified that change. A claim card prevents this silent promotion by linking every use of an idea to its formulation, corpus, evidence, resistance, and current status.
Each card records a stable identifier, a precise statement, the kind of claim, the examined corpus, supporting passages, resistant passages, unstated premises, present status, and conditions for revision. Repetition across an author's previous books documents an idea's history; it does not establish its validity in a new inquiry. The governing rule remains: Previous Book ≠ Evidence for New Corpus.
Five distinctions organize the record. Quranic text is the cited Arabic wording itself. In this condensed English edition, [Textual paraphrase] identifies a rendering of its sense, not an identical replacement for that wording. [Observation] records a checkable feature. [Hypothesis] proposes a relationship still requiring examination. [Candidate] marks an interpretation supported by a specified test while retaining conditions. [Stable finding] states a limited conclusion that has met the declared examination. OPEN identifies an unresolved question and may accompany a hypothesis or candidate.
Consider NAMES-H01: “The names taught to Adam are computational procedures governing matter.” The direct corpus is 2:31–33. [Textual paraphrase] The passage describes teaching, presentation, and Adam's reporting of names. Turning these into computational control requires additional premises: name equals algorithm, and reporting equals material command. The passage supplies neither equation. Its closing distinction concerning God's knowledge of the unseen also prevents instruction from becoming unlimited knowledge.
The narrower statement that Adam receives knowledge enabling him to report in the presented situation is a [Candidate] closely supported by the narrative actions. Acceptance of this statement does not promote the algorithmic hypothesis. The difference concerns what is being asserted, not the researcher's private confidence. Similarly, finding that an identity claim is unproved does not establish its opposite or close every related question.
Cards also preserve source roles. The Quran governs the inquiry; the local draft supplies research material; earlier books contribute hypotheses and comparisons; digital indexes assist collection. A published source does not confer certainty on its interpretations, and a draft does not lack value merely because it remains unfinished.
[Stable finding] An explicitly explained open question is a legitimate research outcome. Intellectual accountability requires a recorded new argument before a claim acquires a stronger status.
Chapter 20
A Protocol for Independent Tadabbur
Adam becomes a successful research example when the reader can investigate another subject without waiting for a ready-made conclusion. The protocol offered here is a human research tool, not a Quranic list of methodological commands. Its purpose is to make questions, evidence, interpretations, and applications open to inspection while retaining the text's governing role.
Begin with one definite question and disclose its assumptions. Someone studying fuʾād, for example, should distinguish collecting its occurrences from claiming that it corresponds to a particular organ or device. Specify whether the unit of collection is a word, root, or relationship. State inclusion rules, counting rules, and reasons for extending the sample. These choices determine what a claim of completeness can legitimately mean.
Preserve the passages and their references. Record speaker, addressee, setting, neighboring expressions, and pronoun relationships before proposing a general meaning. A statement attributed to an adversary does not carry the same status as the divine account of an event. A comparison does not make its two sides identical. These distinctions matter in the tempter's promises concerning permanence and in the comparison between a word and a tree.
Describe relationships before settling every definition. Distinguish explicit attribution from contextual identification, association from causation, function from identity, and a name from the properties of its bearer. Functional non-synonymy prevents premature equivalence; it does not authorize invented absolute differences. The law of balance weighs both expressions and conclusions according to their contextual positions and actual evidence.
Generate a hypothesis that can encounter failure. Fix its scope and compare an alternative. If a mathānī analysis is used, state its procedure and establish its component functions independently of the conclusion. Then conduct forward testing across the defined sample, reverse testing of the proposed function, and examination of resistant passages. Record revisions as new versions. Possible outcomes include a narrower proposal, a limited finding, rejection of one formulation, or an explained OPEN status.
Write the claim card before the conclusion. The conclusion must not say more than its supporting cards permit. “Not established” must not become “impossible”; a pattern fitting several passages must not become a universal law. A subsequent educational or organizational model should identify itself as a human application and explain what it transfers from textual relationships.
Preserve the research notebook and defer attractive questions beyond the chosen scope. [Stable finding] A reviewable path can connect question, corpus, relationships, hypothesis, tests, resistance, and provisional application. Adam teaches this method through a network of creation, knowledge, commitment, failure, and correction that no single interpretation should absorb.
Appendix A
The Direct Adam Corpus
The direct corpus counts the name Adam, not every passage relevant to his story. [Observation] It contains 25 occurrences in 25 verses across nine surahs. Its contextual classification is not an etymology or definition of the name.
The references are: 2:31, 2:33, 2:34, 2:35, 2:37; 3:33, 3:59; 5:27; 7:11, 7:19, 7:26, 7:27, 7:31, 7:35, 7:172; 17:61, 17:70; 18:50; 19:58; 20:115, 20:116, 20:117, 20:120, 20:121; 36:60.
There are seven Banī Ādam expressions, five li-Ādam expressions, five direct addresses to Adam, one reference to Adam's two sons, one to his descendants, and six remaining uses of the name. Addressing the children of Adam is distinct from directly addressing Adam himself.
The network includes instruction, reporting, prostration, residence, prohibition, received words and repentance, selection, creation, descent, human dignity, commitment, temptation, and action. These categories guide comparison without reducing the name to one function. Surrounding verses may be added to a contextual corpus when their relevance is explained. The full Arabic appendix preserves the passages; this adaptation supplies their references for independent checking.

Appendix D
Working Glossary
Corpus: A text sample defined by declared collection rules. Completeness always concerns its stated scope.
Contextual and network induction: Examine speakers, settings, references, and relationships across passages without turning association into identity.
Fiqh al-lisān al-Qurʾānī: The author's inquiry into Quranic expressions through function, context, comparison, and testing. The method's name does not guarantee any particular result.
Functional non-synonymy: Avoid assuming that different expressions perform identical functions; establish differences rather than invent them.
Law of balance: Weigh terms by their position and weigh conclusions by the evidence supporting them.
Mathānī analysis: A proposed layer of analysis involving letter units and functional relationships. It differs from morphological root classification and requires independent validation.
Resistant passage: Evidence likely to expose a hidden premise, restrict a generalization, or require revision.
Operational model: A human tool for organizing or applying relationships. Its usefulness does not establish the physical identity of textual entities.
Interpretive economy: Prefer an account preserving the evidence with fewer unsupported premises.
OPEN: An explained unresolved status, with conditions for renewed investigation. A [Stable finding] remains limited and reviewable.
Appendix E
A Reusable Research Notebook
Preserve each version of the notebook. Keep cited wording, paraphrase, observation, and inference visibly distinct.
Research identity: Researcher; topic; identifier; version; date; initial question; motivation; unproved assumptions; scope; deferred topics.
Corpus design: Target word, root, or relationship; text source; checking method; inclusion and exclusion rules; counting unit; occurrence and verse totals; contextual extensions and their reasons.
Passage card: Reference; preserved text; [Textual paraphrase] if needed; speaker and addressee; setting; associated expressions; [Observation]; explicit relationships; inferred relationships; resistance to the proposal.
Hypothesis card: Identifier; precise claim; identity, function, cause, or analogy; supporting evidence; additional premises; resistant evidence; component-analysis procedure if used; forward and reverse test results; compared alternatives; conditions for rejection or demotion.
Status and application: [Hypothesis], [Candidate], or [Stable finding]; OPEN and its reason; bounded conclusion; limits of generalization; revision conditions; proposed operational model; changes between versions.
Before closing, check whether the speaker and context remain intact, analogy has become identity, evidence has been omitted, or the conclusion exceeds its strongest supporting card. An unresolved material gap should stay explicit and guide the next inquiry.
